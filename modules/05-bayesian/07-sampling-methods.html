Sampling from posteriors

When there’s no formula, draw

Fit a logistic model to ignition tests of a solvent vapor, a posterior with no conjugate shortcut. Sample it three ways: directly from a grid, by rejection sampling with the prior as the proposal, and by importance sampling from a t approximation at the posterior mode. Measure each method’s efficiency, resample importance draws, and see an importance sampler fail when its proposal is too narrow.

Module 5 · Lesson 7 of 13 Week 82 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The plant is introducing a new cleaning solvent, and the process safety team needs to know at what concentration its vapor becomes flammable in air. The safety lab ran the standard test: a spark in a vessel of vapor and air, at five concentrations, six times each, recording whether the mixture ignited.

Vapor in air (% by volume) 1.0 1.2 1.4 1.6 1.8
Ignitions out of 6 0 0 4 4 6

A logistic model describes it: the probability of ignition at concentration c is

P(\text{ignition}) = \text{logit}^{-1}\big(\alpha + \beta\,(c - 1.4)\big),

centered at 1.4% so that \alpha is the log-odds at 1.4%. The safety question is about the concentration where ignition becomes likely, and especially about how sure we are that low concentrations are safe.

There’s no conjugate prior for logistic regression, so the posterior has no formula. Module 4 sampled such posteriors with MCMC. This lesson covers the simpler methods that came first, which still matter: they’re exact, they’re easy to check, and for one or two parameters, they’re often all you need.

Flammability limits! Now that’s chemical engineering. Six sparks per concentration and a lot of careful thinking. No flames on my watch!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • write the log-posterior of a logistic model
  • sample a posterior directly from a grid
  • use rejection sampling, with the prior as the proposal
  • approximate the posterior with a normal distribution at its mode
  • use importance sampling, measure its efficiency, and resample its draws

The log-posterior

The prior is weakly informative: a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 2.5 for \alpha, which allows ignition probabilities at 1.4% from about 1% to 99%, and a normal with mean 0 and SD 25 for \beta, which allows the probability to go from near 0 to near 1 within a fraction of a percent of concentration, or hardly change at all. The setup code has the data, x (the concentration minus 1.4), y (ignitions) and m (tests), and a function log_lik(a, b) that computes the log-likelihood for vectors of \alpha and \beta values at once.

Your turn. Write the log-posterior, up to its constant, and evaluate it at \alpha = 0, \beta = 10.

Add the log-prior, log_prior(a, b).

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
log_post(0, 10)

−10.54. As in Module 4, the value itself doesn’t mean anything, but differences between values do. Because log_lik() works on vectors, so does log_post(): you can evaluate it at 40,000 points in one call.

Direct sampling from a grid

With two parameters, a grid still works. Evaluate the posterior on a 200 × 200 grid, sample grid points in proportion to it, and add a little uniform jitter within each cell so the draws aren’t stuck on grid lines. The draws are exact, apart from the grid’s resolution.

The quantity the safety team wants is the LFL50, the concentration with a 50% chance of ignition. At that concentration, \alpha + \beta(c - 1.4) = 0, so it’s 1.4 - \alpha/\beta: a function of both parameters, whose posterior comes straight from the draws.

Your turn. Make the grid, sample 10,000 draws, and summarize the LFL50.

Sample the grid points with their posterior probabilities, post.

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
g <- expand.grid(a = seq(-4, 4, length.out = 200), b = seq(0, 60, length.out = 200))
lp <- log_post(g$a, g$b)
post <- exp(lp - max(lp))
post <- post / sum(post)
set.seed(5071)
i <- sample(nrow(g), 10000, replace = TRUE, prob = post)
a <- g$a[i] + runif(10000, -0.02, 0.02)               # jitter within each grid cell
b <- g$b[i] + runif(10000, -0.15, 0.15)
lfl50 <- 1.4 - a / b
c(mean = mean(lfl50), quantile(lfl50, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A posterior mean LFL50 of 1.43%, with a 95% credible interval from 1.32% to 1.54%. Two practical checks matter with grids. Subtracting max(lp) before exp() avoids underflow (Module 4, Lesson 2). And the grid must cover the posterior: here, only about two parts in a million of the posterior lie near its edges.

Here are 100 of the posterior’s ignition curves, with the observed proportions:

cc <- seq(0.9, 1.9, by = 0.01)
curves <- do.call(rbind, lapply(1:100, function(k) data.frame(id = k, conc = cc, p = plogis(a_draws[k] + b_draws[k] * (cc - 1.4)))))
ggplot(curves, aes(x = conc, y = p, group = id)) +

  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::percent) +

Your turn. The safety case for the solvent store assumes that vapor never exceeds 1.1%. What’s the posterior of the ignition probability at 1.1%? Use the grid draws, a and b.

The concentration is 1.1%.

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
g <- expand.grid(a = seq(-4, 4, length.out = 200), b = seq(0, 60, length.out = 200))
lp <- log_post(g$a, g$b)
post <- exp(lp - max(lp)) / sum(exp(lp - max(lp)))
set.seed(5071)
i <- sample(nrow(g), 10000, replace = TRUE, prob = post)
a <- g$a[i] + runif(10000, -0.02, 0.02)
b <- g$b[i] + runif(10000, -0.15, 0.15)
p_low <- plogis(a + b * (1.1 - 1.4))
c(mean = mean(p_low), quantile(p_low, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A posterior mean of 4.2%, but a 95% credible interval from 0.1% to 17%. Nothing ignited at 1.0% or 1.2%, yet 30 tests can’t rule out a substantial ignition probability at 1.1%, because the curve’s steepness is so uncertain. For a safety case, that upper end is what matters. The honest conclusion is that 1.1% isn’t demonstrably safe, and the lab should run more tests between 1.0% and 1.3%, where the uncertainty is.

Rejection sampling

A grid in 5 dimensions would need 200^5 points, so we need methods that don’t. Rejection sampling (Module 4, Lesson 4) draws from a proposal and accepts each draw with a probability that corrects for the difference from the target. With the prior as the proposal there’s a neat version: accept a prior draw with probability L(\theta)/L_{\max}, its likelihood divided by the largest possible likelihood. Prior times likelihood is proportional to the posterior, so the accepted draws are exact posterior draws.

Your turn. Draw 100,000 values from the prior, accept them with probability L(\theta)/L_{\max} (on the log scale), and report the acceptance rate and the posterior mean LFL50. The maximum log-likelihood is −4.3146.

Subtract the maximum log-likelihood, max_ll.

set.seed(5072)
a0 <- rnorm(100000, 0, 2.5)                  # draws from the prior
b0 <- rnorm(100000, 0, 25)
max_ll <- -4.314570629                       # the maximum of the log-likelihood
keep <- log(runif(100000)) < log_lik(a0, b0) - max_ll
c(acceptance = mean(keep), lfl50 = mean(1.4 - a0[keep] / b0[keep]))

Only 3.0% of the prior draws survived, about 3,000 out of 100,000, but they’re exact posterior draws, and their mean LFL50, 1.432%, agrees with the grid’s 1.431%. The acceptance rate is the problem: the more the data say, the narrower the posterior is compared with the prior, and the more draws are wasted. With a few more parameters, or a little more data, it drops to one in a million.

The normal approximation

A much better proposal starts from the posterior’s mode, found by optimization, as in Module 4, Lesson 9. Near its mode, a posterior is often roughly normal, with a covariance matrix equal to the inverse of the Hessian of the negative log-posterior there. That’s the Laplace approximation, or normal approximation. With lots of data it’s very accurate on its own, and even when it isn’t, it makes an excellent proposal.

Your turn. Find the mode with optim(), and the approximate posterior SDs from the Hessian.

Invert the Hessian, fit$hessian.

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
fit <- optim(c(0, 10), function(t) -log_post(t[1], t[2]), hessian = TRUE)
V <- solve(fit$hessian)                       # the approximate posterior covariance
c(mode = fit$par, sd = sqrt(diag(V)))

The mode is at \alpha = -0.31, \beta = 9.67, with approximate SDs of 0.56 and 3.28. But the grid’s posterior mean of \beta was 11.5, well above the mode: the posterior of \beta is skewed, with a long right tail toward very steep curves. A normal approximation can’t capture that. It’s a good proposal, though, especially if you fatten its tails.

Importance sampling

Importance sampling (Module 4, Lesson 5) keeps every draw from a proposal q, and weights it by w = p(\theta \mid y)/q(\theta), which only needs the posterior up to its constant if the weights are normalized to sum to 1. A posterior expectation is then the weighted average. As a proposal, use a t distribution with 4 degrees of freedom, centered at the mode with the Laplace covariance: like the normal approximation, but with heavier tails, so it covers the posterior’s skew.

The effective sample size of the weights, 1/\sum w_i^2, says how many equally weighted draws they’re worth. If a few draws have nearly all the weight, it’s tiny, and the estimate can’t be trusted.

Your turn. Draw 10,000 values from the t proposal, compute the normalized weights, and find the weighted mean LFL50 and the effective sample size.

The effective sample size uses the normalized weights, w.

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
fit <- optim(c(0, 10), function(t) -log_post(t[1], t[2]), hessian = TRUE)
V <- solve(fit$hessian)
set.seed(5073)
th <- mvtnorm::rmvt(10000, sigma = V, df = 4, delta = fit$par)
log_w <- log_post(th[, 1], th[, 2]) - mvtnorm::dmvt(th, delta = fit$par, sigma = V, df = 4, log = TRUE)
w <- exp(log_w - max(log_w))
w <- w / sum(w)
lfl50 <- 1.4 - th[, 1] / th[, 2]
c(lfl50 = sum(w * lfl50), ess = 1 / sum(w^2))

An LFL50 of 1.430%, matching the other two methods, and the 10,000 weighted draws are worth 7,844 independent ones: a 78% efficiency, against rejection sampling’s 3%. A good proposal makes all the difference. (mvtnorm::dmvt() returns the log-density with log = TRUE, but note that its default is log = TRUE anyway, an unusual choice.)

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · From weights to draws. Many tools want plain, unweighted draws. Sampling importance resampling (SIR) gets them: resample the proposal draws with their weights as probabilities. Draw 4,000, and compare the LFL50’s 2.5%, 50% and 97.5% quantiles with the grid’s: 1.320%, 1.432% and 1.542%.

Resample with the importance weights, w.

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
fit <- optim(c(0, 10), function(t) -log_post(t[1], t[2]), hessian = TRUE)
V <- solve(fit$hessian)
set.seed(5073)
th <- mvtnorm::rmvt(10000, sigma = V, df = 4, delta = fit$par)
log_w <- log_post(th[, 1], th[, 2]) - mvtnorm::dmvt(th, delta = fit$par, sigma = V, df = 4, log = TRUE)
w <- exp(log_w - max(log_w)) / sum(exp(log_w - max(log_w)))
lfl50 <- 1.4 - th[, 1] / th[, 2]
set.seed(5074)
r <- sample(10000, 4000, replace = TRUE, prob = w)
quantile(lfl50[r], c(0.025, 0.5, 0.975))

1.323%, 1.429% and 1.540%: three methods, one posterior. Resampling with replacement repeats the heavily weighted draws, so the 4,000 aren’t all distinct, but they’re draws from (approximately) the posterior. The same idea, run as a sequence, is behind particle filters, which track the state of a process in real time.

Challenge 2 · A proposal that’s too narrow. Importance sampling fails quietly when the proposal is narrower than the posterior: the tails are rarely sampled, and the few draws that land there get enormous weights. Repeat it with a normal proposal with a quarter of the Laplace SDs (V / 16), and look at the effective sample size and the largest weight.

The proposal density must be the one you drew from: V / 16.

log_post <- function(a, b) log_lik(a, b) + log_prior(a, b)
fit <- optim(c(0, 10), function(t) -log_post(t[1], t[2]), hessian = TRUE)
V <- solve(fit$hessian)
set.seed(5075)
th <- mvtnorm::rmvnorm(10000, fit$par, V / 16)
log_w <- log_post(th[, 1], th[, 2]) - mvtnorm::dmvnorm(th, fit$par, V / 16, log = TRUE)
w <- exp(log_w - max(log_w))
w <- w / sum(w)
c(ess = 1 / sum(w^2), largest_weight = max(w))

10,000 draws worth 10, and a single draw carrying 31% of the total weight. Estimates from these weights would be dominated by a handful of draws, and they’d change completely with another seed. Always check the effective sample size. Better still, choose a proposal with heavier tails than the posterior, like the t, so that the weights stay bounded. This weakness is why MCMC, the next lesson’s subject, took over for anything beyond a few parameters.

Quick quiz

1. Why subtract the maximum log-posterior before exponentiating on a grid?

Log-posteriors like −1,000 would underflow to 0. The constant cancels when you normalize.

2. With the prior as the proposal, what is the acceptance probability in rejection sampling?

Prior × L/L_{\max} is proportional to the posterior, so the accepted draws come from the posterior.

3. Why does rejection sampling from the prior get worse with more data?

Here, 3% were accepted. With ten times the data, it would be far fewer.

4. What is the Laplace approximation?

It’s accurate when the posterior is close to normal, and a good proposal when it isn’t quite.

5. 10,000 importance draws have an effective sample size of 12. What does that tell you?

Use a proposal with heavier tails, centered on the posterior mode.

6. Why use a t proposal rather than a normal one for importance sampling?

A proposal should be wider than the target in every direction.

Wrap-up

You can now sample a posterior that has no formula, three ways. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Grid sampling is exact up to the grid’s resolution, and it works for one or two parameters: the solvent’s LFL50 is 1.43% (95% credible interval 1.32% to 1.54%).
  • The posterior of any function of the parameters comes from the draws: the ignition probability at 1.1% could be as high as 17%, so more tests are needed at low concentrations.
  • Rejection sampling with the prior as the proposal is exact but wasteful: 3% acceptance here.
  • The Laplace approximation puts a normal distribution at the mode. Here it missed the skew in \beta, whose posterior mean is 11.5 against a mode of 9.7.
  • Importance sampling with a t proposal at the mode was 78% efficient. Always check the effective sample size: a too-narrow proposal gave 10 from 10,000.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Grid sampling
Drawing grid points in proportion to the posterior, with jitter within each cell. Exact up to the resolution, practical in one or two dimensions.
Rejection sampling
Drawing from a proposal and accepting each draw with a probability that corrects for the difference from the target.
Mode
The parameter value with the highest posterior density, found by optimization.
Laplace (normal) approximation
A normal distribution centered at the posterior mode, with covariance equal to the inverse Hessian of the negative log-posterior.
Importance sampling
Weighting draws from a proposal by the ratio of the target to the proposal density.
Effective sample size of importance weights
1/\sum w_i^2 for normalized weights: how many equally weighted draws the weighted draws are worth.
Sampling importance resampling (SIR)
Resampling importance draws with their weights as probabilities, to get unweighted draws.
