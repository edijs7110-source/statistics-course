Module 5 project: a Bayesian hierarchical analysis

Which cooling-water locations are corroding too fast?

The Module 5 project brief: two years of corrosion coupons from 16 locations in the plant’s cooling-water network, some with eight coupons and some with one. Fit, check and compare complete-pooling, no-pooling and hierarchical models, with and without the water temperature as a location-level predictor, and tell water treatment which locations are probably above the corrosion target, how sure you are, and what partial pooling changed.

The plant’s cooling water runs through 16 heat exchangers, coolers and headers, and all of them are carbon steel. To watch for corrosion, water treatment hangs small steel coupons at each location, about 90 days at a time, and the lab weighs them before and after. The weight lost, over the coupon’s area and the exposure time, gives a corrosion rate in mm per year. The treatment program’s target is 0.125 mm/yr at every location.

Ines, the water treatment engineer, has budget to act at three locations this year: change the inhibitor dosing, or the metallurgy. But some locations have had eight coupons over two years, and some only one or two. Ranking them by their raw average rates would put the locations with the fewest coupons at the top and the bottom, by chance. That’s a job for a hierarchical model.

Module 5 project Week 89 of the study plan About 8–10 hours

Sixteen locations, 98 coupons and one budget. Time to partially pool! By the end, you’ll have done what a real reliability engineer would do, and you’ll know exactly how sure to be.

What you’ll make

One report, written in Quarto in your plant-study RStudio project, that Ines could take to the plant’s maintenance planning meeting. It should contain:

  1. A summary for Ines, at the top, in plain words: which locations are probably above the target, which three to act at and why, how the water temperature matters, and what to expect at the new chiller, E-401.
  2. The data and its cleaning: the corrosion rates, how you computed them, and every problem you found.
  3. The models: complete pooling, no pooling, partial pooling, and partial pooling with the water temperature, with their priors and prior predictive checks.
  4. The checks and the comparison: convergence diagnostics, posterior predictive checks, and LOO.
  5. The answers: each location’s typical corrosion rate with a credible interval, the probability that it’s above 0.125 mm/yr, and a prediction for E-401.
  6. What partial pooling changed, a cleaning log, and the limitations.

Nobody grades it, but write it as if the plant’s corrosion specialist will read it. In real life, one would.

The handover

Download the three files into a new folder in your project, data-raw/corrosion/, and don’t edit them:

  • README.txt: Ines’s note, with the corrosion-rate formula, the target, the questions, the columns and a known issue. Read it first.
  • coupons_2026.csv: every coupon since October 2024, 98 of them.
  • locations.csv: the 16 locations, with their typical water temperatures.

Then download the project template, save it in the top folder of your project as corrosion-study.qmd, and click Render. It reads the data, computes the rates, and plots them by location, with headings and code skeletons for every step. Fill it in one section at a time, and render often.

The models need brms with Stan, so follow the installation steps in Lesson 9’s practice section first. If you can’t install Stan, there’s a route without it: see the tips.

The brief, step by step

  1. Explore and clean (Module 1, Lesson 13 of this module). Compute each coupon’s exposure time from its dates, and compare it with the exposure_days column. Then compute each coupon’s corrosion rate with Ines’s formula. Plot the rates by location, on a log scale, with the target. Is any coupon’s rate impossible, or nearly so? Fix what you can justify, and log every decision.
  2. Choose the scale and the priors (Lessons 3 and 10). Corrosion rates are positive and skewed, so model the log rate. Write down weakly informative priors: a typical carbon-steel coupon in treated cooling water corrodes at roughly 0.02 to 0.2 mm/yr, and locations differ by perhaps a factor of 2. Check them with a prior predictive simulation: do they predict plausible rates?
  3. Fit four models (Lessons 9, 11 and 12):
    • complete pooling, log_rate ~ 1
    • no pooling, log_rate ~ 0 + loc_id
    • partial pooling, log_rate ~ 1 + (1 | loc_id)
    • partial pooling with the water temperature, centered at 37 °C, log_rate ~ temp_c + (1 | loc_id)
  4. Check them (Lessons 8, 10 and 13). Every R-hat below 1.01, enough ESS, no divergences (raise adapt_delta if you see any). Then posterior predictive checks with pp_check(), including one aimed at the differences between locations.
  5. Compare them (Lesson 13). Use loo() and loo_compare(). Then think about what each comparison predicts: leaving out one coupon isn’t the same as leaving out a whole location. Which matters for E-401?
  6. Answer Ines (Lessons 5, 11 and 12). With the model your checks and comparison support, report each location’s typical corrosion rate (the posterior median of its exponentiated mean log rate) with a 90% credible interval, and the probability that it’s above 0.125 mm/yr. Which three locations would you act at, and on what criterion? What does the temperature model predict for E-401’s next coupon?
  7. Show what partial pooling changed. A table or plot of the no-pooling and partial-pooling estimates side by side. Which locations moved most, and why? Did the ranking change?
  8. Write it up. Summary first, then the data, the models, the checks and comparison, the answers, and the limitations, with the cleaning log at the end.

Stretch goal. Ines’s question is really about future coupons. Fit the model with a location-level predictor that’s leave-one-location-out cross-validated: kfold(fit, group = "loc_id") in brms. Does the temperature model win now? Or take it further: corrosion rates often fall during a coupon’s exposure as a protective film forms, so add the exposure days as a coupon-level predictor, and see whether the data support it.

Tips

  • Trust the dates, not the log. The exposure time is the difference between removed and installed. If a typed column disagrees with the dates, and the dates agree with the 90-day routine, the typed column is wrong.
  • Work on the log scale, report on the rate scale. A location’s typical rate is e^{\mu_j}, the median of its coupons’ rates, and its credible interval comes from exponentiating the draws of \mu_j. Transform the draws, not the summaries.
  • Center the temperature. With temp_c = temperature minus 37 °C, the intercept is the log rate at a typical location, which is easy to put a prior on, and the sampler is happier.
  • Divergences in hierarchical models usually mean the funnel (Lesson 11). brms is already non-centered, so raise adapt_delta to 0.95 or 0.99.
  • “Probably above the target” needs a threshold you state: a posterior probability above 50%? Above 10%? With Ines’s budget of three, ranking by the probability, or by the posterior median, are both defensible. Say which, and whether the top three differ.
  • E-401 has one coupon. Predict its next coupon with posterior_predict(), which includes the coupon-to-coupon scatter. A credible interval for its typical rate is a different, narrower thing. Ines asked for a prediction.
  • Without Stan, write a Gibbs sampler for the varying-intercepts model (Module 4, Lesson 12): the conditional posteriors of the location means, the overall mean, and the two variances are all normal or scaled inverse chi-squared. Check it against lme4::lmer(log_rate ~ 1 + (1 | loc_id)), which gives similar point estimates.
  • Say what partial pooling did, and why. “The model is more conservative” isn’t enough. Name the locations that moved, and connect it to how many coupons they had.
