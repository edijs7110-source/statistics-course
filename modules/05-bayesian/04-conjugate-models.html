More conjugate models

Counts, measurements, and what comes next

Two more conjugate pairs: the gamma prior for a Poisson rate, applied to the trips of a new compressor, with a prior from the rest of the fleet; and the normal prior for a normal mean, applied to density readings of a solvent lot, with a prior from the supplier’s certificate. Use each posterior to predict: the negative binomial distribution for next quarter’s trips, and a predictive interval for the next reading.

Module 5 · Lesson 4 of 13 Week 79 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The beta-binomial model handles proportions. Two other kinds of data turn up all the time in a plant: counts of events over time, such as trips, alarms and leaks, and measurements with a known instrument error. Each has its own conjugate prior, and this lesson works through both.

It also takes prediction further. A posterior describes the parameters, but the people who run the plant care about what will happen: how many trips next quarter, or what the next reading will be. The posterior predictive distribution answers that, and it’s where Bayesian methods are often most useful.

Counting trips, weighing solvents: the everyday life of a plant! And this time we get to predict the future. Responsibly, with uncertainty.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • update a gamma prior with Poisson counts
  • compute the probability that a new machine is better than the fleet
  • predict future counts with the negative binomial distribution
  • combine a normal prior with normal measurements
  • make a predictive interval for the next measurement

The gamma-Poisson model

The plant’s new air compressor, C-7, was installed in May 2025. Unplanned trips, when it shuts down on a fault and the standby has to take over, are a real cost. Over its first 18 months, it tripped 6 times. Trips happen at random moments at some average rate, so the number in a month is a Poisson count with rate \lambda trips per month.

The conjugate prior for a Poisson rate is the gamma distribution, with shape \alpha and rate \beta:

p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta\lambda}, \qquad \text{mean} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}.

Like the beta’s a and b, the gamma’s parameters act like prior data: \alpha prior events in \beta prior months. After observing counts y_1, \ldots, y_n in n months, the posterior is

\lambda \mid y \;\sim\; \text{Gamma}\Big(\alpha + \textstyle\sum y_i,\; \beta + n\Big).

Add the trips to \alpha and the months to \beta.

The prior comes from the plant’s other compressors, which trip about 0.7 times a month. C-7 is a newer model, so the reliability engineer wanted only a modest prior: worth 6 months of data, Gamma(4.2, 6).

Your turn. Check the prior: its mean, its 95% interval, and the probability it gives to a rate above 1.5 trips a month. In R, the gamma functions take the shape and the rate: pgamma(x, shape, rate).

The rate in question is 1.5 trips a month.

alpha <- 4.2
beta <- 6
c(mean = alpha / beta, qgamma(c(0.025, 0.975), alpha, beta), p_over_1.5 = pgamma(1.5, alpha, beta, lower.tail = FALSE))

A prior mean of 0.7 trips a month, a 95% interval from 0.20 to 1.51, and a 2.6% chance of more than 1.5. A careful warning: R’s gamma functions also accept a scale, which is 1/\beta. Write pgamma(x, 4.2, scale = 6) by mistake and you get a prior with a mean of 25 trips a month. Always check a prior’s mean after writing it down.

Your turn. Update the prior with C-7’s 18 months. Find the posterior’s parameters, its mean and its 95% credible interval.

Add the number of months, length(y).

alpha_post <- 4.2 + sum(y)
beta_post <- 6 + length(y)
c(alpha_post = alpha_post, beta_post = beta_post, mean = alpha_post / beta_post, qgamma(c(0.025, 0.975), alpha_post, beta_post))

The posterior is Gamma(10.2, 24), with a mean of 0.425 trips a month and a 95% credible interval from 0.21 to 0.72. As with the beta, the mean is a compromise: the data’s own rate, 6/18 = 0.33, gets weight 18/(6 + 18) = 75\%, and the fleet’s 0.7 gets 25%.

Your turn. What’s the posterior probability that C-7 trips less often than the fleet average of 0.7 a month?

pgamma(0.7, 10.2, 24) is the posterior probability that \lambda is below 0.7.

pgamma(0.7, 10.2, 24)

97%. Even with a prior centered on the fleet’s rate, 18 months of data make it very probable that the new model trips less often. That’s useful evidence for the next compressor purchase.

Predicting next quarter

The maintenance planner wants to know how many trips to expect in the next three months. If \lambda were known, the count would be Poisson with mean 3\lambda. Averaging that Poisson over the gamma posterior gives the negative binomial distribution, with size \alpha' and probability \beta'/(\beta' + 3):

P(Y = k) = \int_0^\infty \text{Poisson}(k \mid 3\lambda)\;\text{Gamma}(\lambda \mid \alpha', \beta')\; d\lambda = \text{NegBin}\Big(k \;\Big|\; \alpha',\ \tfrac{\beta'}{\beta' + 3}\Big).

Your turn. Find the probability of no trips at all next quarter, and of 3 or more. Compare the second with the plug-in Poisson, which uses the posterior mean as if it were the true rate.

Use the same prob in both negative binomial calls.

prob <- 24 / (24 + 3)
c(none = dnbinom(0, size = 10.2, prob = prob),
  three_plus = pnbinom(2, size = 10.2, prob = prob, lower.tail = FALSE),
  plug_in = ppois(2, 3 * 0.425, lower.tail = FALSE))

A 30% chance of a trip-free quarter, and a 14.6% chance of 3 or more trips, against 13.7% from the plug-in Poisson. The difference is small here, because 18 months pin the rate down fairly well. With a few months of data, it would be much larger. The predictive distribution is always more spread out than the plug-in, because it includes the uncertainty in \lambda.

You don’t need to know the negative binomial to get these numbers. Simulation does the averaging for you: draw a rate from the posterior, then a count with that rate, and repeat. This two-step recipe works for any model, conjugate or not.

Your turn. Simulate 100,000 quarters this way, and estimate the two probabilities.

Each quarter uses its own drawn rate, lambda.

set.seed(5041)
lambda <- rgamma(100000, 10.2, 24)             # step 1: a rate from the posterior
quarter <- rpois(100000, 3 * lambda)           # step 2: a quarter's trips at that rate
c(none = mean(quarter == 0), three_plus = mean(quarter >= 3))

30.1% and 14.9%, within simulation error of the exact 30.1% and 14.6%. When rpois() gets a vector of rates, it uses a different one for each draw, which is exactly what the recipe needs.

Here’s the predictive distribution, next to the plug-in Poisson:

k <- 0:7
pred <- rbind(
  data.frame(trips = k, prob = dnbinom(k, size = 10.2, prob = 24 / 27), what = "posterior predictive"),
  data.frame(trips = k, prob = dpois(k, 3 * 0.425), what = "plug-in Poisson")
)
ggplot(pred, aes(x = factor(trips), y = prob, fill = what)) +
  geom_col(position = position_dodge(width = 0.8), width = 0.75) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c(`posterior predictive` = "#C2416C", `plug-in Poisson` = "#F4C0D1"), name = NULL) +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::percent) +
  labs(x = "Trips next quarter", y = "Probability") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) +
  theme(legend.position = "top")

The normal-normal model

A tanker of solvent has arrived, and its density is a quick check of its purity: water contamination pushes it up. The specification is 0.8700 to 0.8740 g/mL. The supplier’s certificate says 0.8720, and from experience, certificates are within about ±0.0020 of the truth (a normal prior with mean 0.8720 and SD 0.0010). The lab’s densitometer has a known measurement SD of \sigma = 0.0004 g/mL, and four readings came back: 0.8731, 0.8736, 0.8729 and 0.8734.

For a normal mean \mu with known measurement SD \sigma, the conjugate prior is normal, \mu \sim N(\mu_0, \tau_0^2). The posterior is normal too, and the cleanest way to write it uses precisions, one over the variances:

\frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu_n = \tau_n^2\left(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}\right).

Precisions add, and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the data’s mean.

Your turn. Compute the posterior mean and SD of the tanker’s density, and the weight the data get. The readings are in the setup code’s density.

The data’s term is n\bar y/\sigma^2, which is sum(density) / sigma^2.

mu0 <- 0.8720
tau0 <- 0.0010
sigma <- 0.0004
n <- length(density)
precision <- 1 / tau0^2 + n / sigma^2
mu_n <- (mu0 / tau0^2 + sum(density) / sigma^2) / precision
c(mean = mu_n, sd = sqrt(1 / precision), data_weight = (n / sigma^2) / precision)

A posterior mean of 0.87320 g/mL, with an SD of 0.00020. The four readings average 0.87325, and they get 96% of the weight: four precise readings say much more than a certificate that’s only good to ±0.0020. The posterior’s 95% credible interval, 0.87282 to 0.87359, is well inside the spec, though higher than the certificate claimed.

Your turn. The quality inspector will take one more reading from the tanker’s outlet. Its predictive distribution is normal with the posterior mean and a variance of \sigma^2 + \tau_n^2: the measurement error plus the uncertainty about \mu. Find the 95% predictive interval, and the probability that the reading comes out above the spec limit of 0.8740 (which would start an argument with the supplier).

The predictive variance is sigma^2 + tau_n^2.

mu_n <- 0.8732019
tau_n <- 0.0001961
sigma <- 0.0004
pred_sd <- sqrt(sigma^2 + tau_n^2)
c(mu_n + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(0.975) * pred_sd, p_over_spec = pnorm(0.8740, mu_n, pred_sd, lower.tail = FALSE))

The next reading will probably be between 0.8723 and 0.8741, and there’s a 3.7% chance that it’s over the limit, even though the tanker’s true density almost certainly isn’t (the posterior probability of that is 0.002%). That distinction, between the uncertainty about a parameter and the spread of the next observation, is one of the most important in applied statistics. A single reading above the limit isn’t evidence of a bad tanker. Several would be.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · New against old. C-7 replaced an older compressor in the same service, which had 22 trips in its last 30 months. With the same fleet prior for both, draw 100,000 rates from each posterior, and find the probability that C-7 trips less often, and a 95% interval for the ratio of the rates.

The old compressor was watched for 30 months.

set.seed(5042)
new <- rgamma(100000, 4.2 + 6, 6 + 18)
old <- rgamma(100000, 4.2 + 22, 6 + 30)
c(p_new_better = mean(new < old), quantile(new / old, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A 94% probability that C-7 is better. The ratio of the rates has a posterior median of 0.57 (C-7 trips about 43% less often), but its 95% interval, from 0.26 to 1.15, still includes “no better”. The posterior of any function of the parameters, such as a ratio, comes straight from the draws.

Challenge 2 · Trust, but verify. Check the gamma-Poisson formula against Lesson 1’s grid method. Compute the posterior on a grid of rates from 0.001 to 3, using the product of the 18 Poisson probabilities as the likelihood, and compare it with the Gamma(10.2, 24) density.

Inside the function, the rate is lambda.

grid <- seq(0.001, 3, by = 0.001)
lik <- sapply(grid, function(lambda) prod(dpois(y, lambda)))
post <- dgamma(grid, 4.2, 6) * lik
post <- post / sum(post)
max(abs(post / 0.001 - dgamma(grid, 10.2, 24)))

A difference of about 10^{-14}, against a peak density of 3.1: the formula is right. Only the total number of trips and months mattered, not which months the trips happened in. They’re sufficient statistics, and conjugate models always have them.

Quick quiz

1. A Gamma(2, 4) prior for a Poisson rate is updated with 10 events in 20 periods. What’s the posterior?

Add the events to the shape and the periods to the rate.

2. You type rgamma(1000, 4.2, scale = 6) for a prior with mean 0.7. What’s wrong?

Shape times scale is 25.2. Use rate = 6, or just pass the rate as the third argument.

3. Why is the negative binomial predictive distribution wider than the plug-in Poisson?

The Poisson’s variance equals its mean. Averaging over an uncertain rate adds the rate’s variance.

4. In the normal-normal model, how is the posterior precision related to the prior and the data?

1/\tau_n^2 = 1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2. Every reading adds precision.

5. The posterior SD of the tanker’s density is 0.0002. Why is the next reading’s predictive SD bigger, 0.00045?

The predictive variance is \sigma^2 + \tau_n^2: the noise in one reading plus the uncertainty about the mean.

6. What’s the general recipe for a posterior predictive simulation?

Two steps, repeated many times. It works for any model, whether or not you know a formula.

Wrap-up

You now know the three classic conjugate models. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Gamma-Poisson: a Gamma(\alpha, \beta) prior and \sum y events in n periods give a Gamma(\alpha + \sum y, \beta + n) posterior. C-7’s is Gamma(10.2, 24): 0.425 trips a month, 95% credible interval 0.21 to 0.72.
  • Comparisons: a 97% probability that C-7 trips less often than the fleet, and 94% that it’s better than its predecessor.
  • The negative binomial predicts next quarter’s trips: a 30% chance of none, and 14.6% of 3 or more.
  • Normal-normal: precisions add, and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average. Four readings outweighed the certificate 96 to 4.
  • Prediction for a measurement: the next reading’s variance is the measurement variance plus the posterior variance.
  • Simulation gives any predictive distribution: draw the parameters, then the data.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Poisson distribution
The distribution of the number of events in a fixed time when events happen at random at a constant rate.
Gamma distribution
A distribution for positive quantities, with a shape and a rate parameter. It’s the conjugate prior for a Poisson rate.
Negative binomial distribution
The distribution of a Poisson count whose rate has a gamma distribution, and so the posterior predictive distribution of the gamma-Poisson model.
Precision
One over the variance. In the normal-normal model, the posterior precision is the sum of the prior and data precisions.
Posterior predictive distribution
The distribution of future data given the observed data, averaging the model over the posterior.
Predictive interval
An interval that will contain a future observation with a stated probability. It’s wider than the credible interval for the mean.
Sufficient statistic
A summary of the data that carries all the information the likelihood needs, such as the total count and total time for Poisson data.
