Hierarchical models II

Varying intercepts, varying slopes, and counts

Catalyst activity falls over time in eight reactors, one of them only started six weeks ago. Fit a hierarchical regression with a varying intercept and slope for each reactor, and see how partial pooling turns three noisy points into a usable prediction of when the new reactor’s catalyst needs replacing. Then fit a hierarchical logistic regression to a leak survey of 18 process units, and rank them without being fooled by small samples.

Module 5 · Lesson 12 of 13 Week 87 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 11’s hierarchical model had one parameter per group. Most real problems have more: each group has its own regression line, or its own logistic curve. This lesson takes the same idea, partial pooling, to hierarchical regression.

The first problem is about catalyst. The plant’s eight hydrogenation reactors use the same catalyst, and its relative activity falls roughly linearly over time until it’s replaced, at 80% of its starting activity. The lab measures each reactor’s activity about every three weeks. Reactors R1 to R7 have eight to ten months of data. R8 was started only six weeks ago, and its three measurements fell steeply: on their own, they say it’ll need new catalyst after only 228 days, months earlier than any other reactor. Ordering catalyst takes four months, so the planner needs to decide soon.

A brand-new reactor and three data points, and a planner who needs an answer now. Partial pooling to the rescue: the other seven reactors know a thing or two!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • fit a regression line to each group separately, and see where that fails
  • read a hierarchical model with varying intercepts and slopes
  • predict a new group from three observations, with partial pooling
  • predict a group that hasn’t been seen yet
  • fit a hierarchical logistic regression to counts, and rank groups honestly

No pooling

Your turn. Fit a separate straight line to each reactor’s activity against time (in units of 100 days), and look at R8’s slope and its standard error.

The row name is the reactor’s name, "R8".

fits <- lapply(split(deact, deact$reactor), function(d) lm(activity_pct ~ day100, data = d))
coefs <- t(sapply(fits, function(f) c(coef(f), se = summary(f)$coefficients["day100", "Std. Error"])))
round(coefs, 2)
coefs["R8", c("day100", "se")]

Reactors R1 to R7 lose 3.6 to 6.8 percentage points of activity per 100 days, with standard errors from 0.25 to 0.55. R8’s three points say 10.6, with a standard error of 1.9: far steeper than any other, and far less certain. And with only three points, that standard error is itself very uncertain. No pooling ignores what the other seven reactors say about how this catalyst behaves.

Varying intercepts and slopes

The hierarchical version gives each reactor j its own starting activity a_j and decline rate b_j, and treats the pairs as draws from a common bivariate normal distribution:

y_{ij} \sim N(a_j + b_j t_{ij},\ \sigma^2), \qquad \begin{pmatrix} a_j \\ b_j \end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} \mu_a \\ \mu_b \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \tau_a^2 & \rho\,\tau_a\tau_b \\ \rho\,\tau_a\tau_b & \tau_b^2 \end{pmatrix}\right).

\mu_a and \mu_b describe the typical reactor, \tau_a and \tau_b how much reactors vary, and \rho whether reactors that start higher also decline faster. In brms, it’s one formula, the same as lmer()’s in Module 3:

deact$day100 <- deact$day / 100
fit <- brm(
  activity_pct ~ day100 + (day100 | reactor), data = deact,
  prior = c(prior(normal(95, 10), class = Intercept), prior(normal(0, 10), class = b),
            prior(exponential(0.5), class = sigma), prior(exponential(0.5), class = sd),
            prior(lkj(2), class = cor)),                 # a mild preference for small correlations
  control = list(adapt_delta = 0.99), seed = 5123
)

The (day100 | reactor) term means “an intercept and a slope for each reactor, correlated”. The LKJ prior is the standard prior for a correlation matrix: lkj(1) is flat over all correlations, and larger values prefer correlations near 0. A first run with adapt_delta raised from brms’s default of 0.8 to 0.95 still had one divergent transition, so this fit raised it to 0.99, which makes Stan take smaller steps. Here’s the summary:

The typical reactor starts at 101.2% and loses 5.65 points per 100 days. Reactors differ by about 2.2 points in starting activity (\tau_a) and 1.15 points per 100 days in decline rate (\tau_b). The setup code has the posterior draws of every reactor’s coefficients, in slopes: columns a_R1 to a_R8 and b_R1 to b_R8, and the hyperparameters mu_a, mu_b, sd_a, sd_b and cor_ab.

Your turn. Find R8’s partially pooled decline rate: its posterior mean and 95% credible interval.

R8’s slope draws are in the column b_R8.

c(mean = mean(slopes$b_R8), quantile(slopes$b_R8, c(0.025, 0.975)))

−6.3 points per 100 days (−8.9 to −4.1), pulled most of the way from R8’s own −10.6 toward the typical −5.65. R8’s three points are still in there: its posterior mean is steeper than the typical reactor’s, and its interval is three to four times as wide as the others’. That’s partial pooling working as it should, with the other reactors supplying what three points can’t.

Here are the eight reactors’ data, with the no-pooling lines (dashed) and the partially pooled ones (solid):

grid_d <- seq(0, 360, by = 10)
np <- do.call(rbind, lapply(split(deact, deact$reactor), function(d) {
  f <- lm(activity_pct ~ I(day / 100), data = d)
  data.frame(reactor = d$reactor[1], day = grid_d, activity = coef(f)[1] + coef(f)[2] * grid_d / 100, fit = "no pooling")
}))
pp <- do.call(rbind, lapply(paste0("R", 1:8), function(r) {
  data.frame(reactor = r, day = grid_d, activity = mean(slopes[[paste0("a_", r)]]) + mean(slopes[[paste0("b_", r)]]) * grid_d / 100, fit = "partial pooling")
}))
ggplot(deact, aes(x = day, y = activity_pct)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 80, color = "grey60") +
  geom_line(data = np, aes(y = activity), linetype = "dashed", color = "#2a78d6") +
  geom_line(data = pp, aes(y = activity), color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_point(size = 1, color = "#3A1F35") +
  facet_wrap(~ reactor, nrow = 2) +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(68, 106)) +
  labs(x = "Days on stream", y = "Catalyst activity (%)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The grey line is the replacement level, 80%. For R1 to R7, with plenty of data, the two methods agree. Only R8 differs.

Your turn. What will R8’s activity be at day 300? Compare the no-pooling prediction, 104.15 - 10.57 \times 3, with the partially pooled posterior, and find the posterior probability that R8 is already below 80% by then.

Time is in units of 100 days, so day 300 is 3.

at_300 <- slopes$a_R8 + slopes$b_R8 * 3                # day 300 is 3 units of 100 days
c(no_pooling = 104.1473 - 10.57007 * 3, partial = mean(at_300), quantile(at_300, c(0.025, 0.975)), p_below_80 = mean(at_300 < 80))

No pooling says R8 will be at 72% by day 300, well past replacement. Partial pooling says 84% (77% to 91%), with a 7% probability that it’s below 80%. The planner can wait for more data before ordering catalyst, and check again in a month.

Your turn. When will R8 reach 80%? For each draw, the time is (a_j - 80)/(-b_j), in units of 100 days. Find its posterior median and 95% interval, in days.

Convert from units of 100 days to days: multiply by 100.

t80 <- (slopes$a_R8 - 80) / (-slopes$b_R8) * 100        # in days
c(median = median(t80), quantile(t80, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A median of 369 days, with a 95% interval from 271 to 557. No pooling said 228 days. (Since these data are simulated, we know the truth: R8’s catalyst was set to decline by 4.0 points per 100 days, so it would reach 80% after about 550 days. Partial pooling’s interval includes that, at its upper end. No pooling missed it by almost a year.) Ordering catalyst for day 300, the lower end of the interval, would be a sensible, cautious plan.

Predicting a new reactor, and the correlation

The plant is planning a ninth reactor. Before it starts, what decline rate should the planner expect? For each draw of the hyperparameters, a new reactor’s slope is a draw from N(\mu_b, \tau_b^2).

Your turn. Simulate a new reactor’s decline rate, and find its 95% predictive interval.

The between-reactor SD of the slopes is sd_b.

set.seed(5121)
b_new <- rnorm(nrow(slopes), mean = slopes$mu_b, sd = slopes$sd_b)
c(mean = mean(b_new), quantile(b_new, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A new reactor will probably lose between 3.0 and 8.4 points per 100 days, with a mean of 5.6. That interval is the honest range for planning the ninth reactor’s catalyst budget. Like Lesson 11’s new lab, it includes the uncertainty about the hyperparameters, not just the spread between reactors.

Your turn. Do reactors whose catalyst starts more active also lose activity faster? Summarize the posterior of the correlation \rho between the intercepts and slopes, and the probability that it’s negative (a higher start goes with a more negative slope, a faster decline).

Negative means below 0.

c(mean = mean(slopes$cor_ab), p_negative = mean(slopes$cor_ab < 0))

A posterior mean of −0.48, and a 95% probability that the correlation is negative: catalyst that starts more active tends to fade faster. That’s chemically plausible, since the most active sites are often the first to be poisoned. With eight reactors, the interval is wide, from −0.86 to 0.17, so it’s a lead to follow, not a finding. The correlation also helps R8: its high starting activity, about 103%, suggests a somewhat faster decline than average, which is part of why its posterior slope is steeper than the typical reactor’s.

Hierarchical logistic regression

The second problem is about leaks. Under the plant’s leak detection and repair (LDAR) program, every valve, flange and pump seal in a process unit is checked for leaking vapor once a year. This year’s survey covered 18 units, with very different numbers of components, from 30 to 960.

Three units stand out on raw rates: U05 had 54 leaks in 520 components (10.4%), U09 had none in 400, and U11 had none in 30. The hierarchical model gives each unit a leak probability p_j on the log-odds scale, with the logits drawn from a common normal distribution: Lesson 11’s model, inside a logistic regression.

fit <- brm(
  leaks | trials(components) ~ 1 + (1 | unit), family = binomial(), data = ldar,
  prior = c(prior(normal(-4, 1.5), class = Intercept),     # about 2% leak, give or take
            prior(exponential(1), class = sd)),
  seed = 5124
)

The typical unit’s log-odds is −3.73, a leak rate of 2.4%, and units vary with an SD of 0.75 on the log-odds scale: some units leak two or three times as often as others. The setup code has the draws of each unit’s leak probability in ldar_draws, in columns U01 to U18.

Your turn. Compare the partially pooled leak probabilities of U09, U11 and U05 with their raw rates of 0%, 0% and 10.4%.

U05’s draws are in the column U05.

c(U09 = mean(ldar_draws$U09), U11 = mean(ldar_draws$U11), U05 = mean(ldar_draws$U05))

U09’s 0 out of 400 becomes 0.7%, and U11’s 0 out of 30 becomes 2.1%, almost the plant’s typical rate: 30 components say very little, while 400 say quite a lot. U05’s 10.4% barely moves, to 9.8%, because 54 leaks out of 520 is strong evidence. A raw rate of 0% would have suggested that U11 needs no attention, which no engineer would believe from 30 components.

Your turn. The regulator requires a unit with a leak rate above 5% to be resurveyed every quarter. What’s the posterior probability that U05, U03 (2 leaks in 45 components, 4.4%) and U11 are above 5%?

The limit is 5%, 0.05.

c(U05 = mean(ldar_draws$U05 > 0.05), U03 = mean(ldar_draws$U03 > 0.05), U11 = mean(ldar_draws$U11 > 0.05))

U05 is above 5% in every draw: resurvey it quarterly, and find out why it leaks so much more than the rest of the plant. U03, whose raw rate was 4.4%, has a 16% probability, and U11 4%. Neither needs quarterly surveys, but U03 deserves a closer look next year.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Which unit is the worst? League tables of raw rates are dominated by small groups, which have the most extreme rates by chance. With posterior draws, you can compute the probability that each unit has the highest leak rate: in each draw, find the unit with the largest probability, and count.

which.max gives the position of the largest value in each row.

p_matrix <- as.matrix(ldar_draws[, ldar$unit])               # one column per unit
worst <- apply(p_matrix, 1, which.max)                       # the column of the largest rate, in each draw
round(table(factor(ldar$unit[worst], levels = ldar$unit)) / nrow(p_matrix), 3)

U05 is the worst unit in 98% of the draws. U03, with only 45 components, comes second, with about 1%. Ranks are notoriously uncertain, and this is the honest way to report them: the probability of each rank, not just the ranking of point estimates.

Challenge 2 · Varying intercepts only. A simpler model gives each reactor its own intercept but one common slope: activity_pct ~ day100 + (1 | reactor). Its draws are in data/brms_deact_intercepts_draws.csv, with the same column names (every b_ column is the same common slope). What does it predict for R8 at day 300?

Use R8’s slope column, b_R8, which in this model is the common slope.

intercepts <- read_csv("data/brms_deact_intercepts_draws.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
at_300 <- intercepts$a_R8 + intercepts$b_R8 * 3
c(mean = mean(at_300), quantile(at_300, c(0.025, 0.975)), p_below_80 = mean(at_300 < 80))

85.8% (83.9% to 87.6%), and no chance at all of being below 80%. This model is too confident: it assumes that R8 declines exactly like the average reactor, when the other seven clearly differ, from 3.6 to 6.8 points per 100 days. Complete pooling of the slopes, no pooling, partial pooling: which is best? Lesson 13 compares them properly.

Quick quiz

1. What does the brms term (day100 | reactor) mean?

It’s the same notation as lme4’s lmer(). (1 | reactor) would give varying intercepts only.

2. R8’s own slope is −10.6 from three points, and its partially pooled slope −6.3. Why so much shrinkage?

As in Lesson 11, shrinkage depends on how precise the group’s own data are.

3. What does the LKJ prior describe?

lkj(1) is flat over correlations, and larger values prefer correlations near 0.

4. A unit had 0 leaks in 30 components. Why is its partially pooled leak rate 2.1%, not 0%?

Zero events in a small sample is weak evidence of a zero rate.

5. Why are league tables of raw rates misleading?

Partial pooling, and rank probabilities, correct for that.

6. How do you predict a reactor that doesn’t exist yet?

That carries both the spread between reactors and the uncertainty about the hyperparameters.

Wrap-up

You can now fit hierarchical regression models, for measurements and for counts. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • No pooling gave R8, with three points, a slope of −10.6 per 100 days, and a replacement date of day 228.
  • Varying intercepts and slopes pooled R8 with the other reactors: −6.3 (−8.9 to −4.1), and a replacement date around day 369 (271 to 557), much closer to the simulated truth.
  • A new reactor will probably lose 3.0 to 8.4 points per 100 days, and reactors that start more active tend to fade faster (95% probability).
  • Hierarchical logistic regression shrinks small units’ rates toward the plant’s typical 2.4%: U11’s 0 out of 30 became 2.1%, while U05’s 10.4% from 520 components stayed at 9.8%.
  • Rank probabilities report league tables honestly: U05 is the worst unit with 98% probability.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Hierarchical regression
A regression whose coefficients vary by group, drawn from a common distribution.
Varying intercepts
A separate intercept for each group, as in (1 | group).
Varying slopes
A separate slope for each group, usually together with varying intercepts, as in (x | group).
Bivariate normal distribution
A normal distribution for two quantities together, with two means, two SDs and a correlation.
LKJ prior
The standard prior for correlation matrices. Its parameter sets how strongly it prefers correlations near 0.
Hierarchical logistic regression
A logistic regression with group-level parameters on the log-odds scale, drawn from a common distribution.
Rank probability
The posterior probability that a group has a particular rank, such as the highest rate.
