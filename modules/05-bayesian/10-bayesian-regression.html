Bayesian linear regression

Module 2’s models, with priors and predictive checks

Refit Module 2’s Arrhenius kinetics as a Bayesian regression. Simulate the flat-prior posterior exactly and see it match least squares, add a literature prior for the activation energy and see when it matters, fit the model with brms, and predict rate constants with honest uncertainty. Then use posterior predictive checks to catch a model that fits the data badly.

Module 5 · Lesson 10 of 13 Week 85 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Module 2 fitted the Arrhenius equation to the rate constants of the plant’s main reaction, measured twice at each of eight temperatures from 140 to 210 °C:

k = A\, e^{-E_a/(RT)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log k = \log A - \frac{E_a}{1000R}\cdot\frac{1000}{T}.

A straight line of \log k against 1000/T, with T in kelvin, whose slope b gives the activation energy: E_a = -bR kJ/mol, with R = 8.314 J/(mol K). Least squares found E_a = 82 kJ/mol.

The Bayesian version of linear regression has the same model, with a prior on the coefficients and the residual SD, and a posterior instead of estimates and standard errors. This lesson shows what that adds: the prior’s say when data are scarce, predictions that carry every source of uncertainty, and a new way of checking whether the model fits, the posterior predictive check.

Arrhenius again! The equation every chemical engineer knows by heart, and now it gets a posterior. Activation energies with error bars you can actually interpret.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • fit the Arrhenius model by least squares, and recover its activation energy
  • simulate the flat-prior posterior exactly, and compare it with least squares
  • combine a literature prior with the data, and see when it matters
  • use brms’s draws to predict rate constants with honest uncertainty
  • check the model with posterior predictive checks

Least squares, and the flat-prior posterior

Your turn. Fit the line with lm(), and convert the slope and its 95% confidence interval to an activation energy.

Every slope converts to an activation energy by multiplying by -R.

fit <- lm(log_k ~ inv_T, data = kin)
c(Ea = -coef(fit)[["inv_T"]] * R, -confint(fit)["inv_T", 2:1] * R)

E_a = 82.3 kJ/mol, with a 95% confidence interval from 80.4 to 84.2. (The interval’s ends swap places because multiplying by a negative number reverses the order.)

With the noninformative prior p(\beta, \sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2, the posterior of a linear regression is Lesson 6’s normal model, generalized. With \hat\beta the least squares coefficients and s^2 the residual variance, on n - p degrees of freedom:

\sigma^2 \mid y \;\sim\; \frac{(n-p)\,s^2}{\chi^2_{n-p}}, \qquad \beta \mid \sigma^2, y \;\sim\; N\!\left(\hat\beta,\ \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\right).

So the posterior can be simulated exactly in Lesson 6’s two steps: \sigma^2, then the coefficients.

Your turn. Draw 10,000 values from the posterior, and summarize the activation energy. The multivariate normal draws use the Cholesky factor of (X^\top X)^{-1}, as in Module 4.

Each row of coefficient draws is scaled by its own \sigma: sqrt(sigma2).

fit <- lm(log_k ~ inv_T, data = kin)
X <- model.matrix(fit)
n <- nrow(X); p <- ncol(X)
s2 <- sum(resid(fit)^2) / (n - p)
set.seed(5101)
sigma2 <- (n - p) * s2 / rchisq(10000, df = n - p)
Z <- matrix(rnorm(10000 * 2), 10000, 2)
beta <- matrix(coef(fit), 10000, 2, byrow = TRUE) + (Z %*% chol(solve(crossprod(X)))) * sqrt(sigma2)
Ea <- -beta[, 2] * R
c(mean = mean(Ea), quantile(Ea, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A posterior mean of 82.3 kJ/mol, and a 95% credible interval from 80.4 to 84.2: the confidence interval, to the first decimal. As in Lesson 6, with this prior the Bayesian and classical answers are numerically identical. Everything Module 2 did with lm() was, in that sense, already a Bayesian analysis with a flat prior.

A prior from the literature

Reactions of this type, the process chemists say, have activation energies of 70 to 100 kJ/mol: a normal prior for E_a with mean 85 and SD 10 kJ/mol. On the slope’s scale, that’s a normal prior with mean -85/R = -10.22 and SD 10/R = 1.20.

When the data’s information about a coefficient is roughly normal, with estimate \hat b and standard error \text{se}, combining it with a normal prior is Lesson 4’s normal-normal model: the precisions add.

Your turn. Combine the literature prior with the slope from lm(), and find the posterior activation energy, its SD, and the weight the data get.

The data’s term is \hat b/\text{se}^2.

fit <- lm(log_k ~ inv_T, data = kin)
b_hat <- coef(fit)[["inv_T"]]
se <- summary(fit)$coefficients["inv_T", "Std. Error"]
m0 <- -85 / R; t0 <- 10 / R                                  # the prior, on the slope's scale
precision <- 1 / t0^2 + 1 / se^2
b_post <- (m0 / t0^2 + b_hat / se^2) / precision
c(Ea = -b_post * R, Ea_sd = R / sqrt(precision), data_weight = (1 / se^2) / precision)

82.3 kJ/mol, with a posterior SD of 0.87: the data get 99.2% of the weight. Sixteen good measurements over a 70 °C range say far more than the literature’s ±10 kJ/mol, and the prior hardly moves the answer.

When does the prior matter? When the data are thin. Suppose the lab had only measured at the two lowest temperatures, 140 and 150 °C: four runs over a 10-degree range.

Your turn. Repeat the combination with only those four runs.

Keep the runs at 150 °C and below.

few <- subset(kin, temp_C <= 150)
fit_few <- lm(log_k ~ inv_T, data = few)
b_hat <- coef(fit_few)[["inv_T"]]
se <- summary(fit_few)$coefficients["inv_T", "Std. Error"]
m0 <- -85 / R; t0 <- 10 / R
precision <- 1 / t0^2 + 1 / se^2
b_post <- (m0 / t0^2 + b_hat / se^2) / precision
c(data_Ea = -b_hat * R, data_se = se * R, Ea = -b_post * R, Ea_sd = R / sqrt(precision), data_weight = (1 / se^2) / precision)

The four runs alone say 87 ± 6.9 kJ/mol. The prior gets a third of the weight now, and the posterior is 86.5 ± 5.7: about 20% narrower than the data alone. That’s the typical pattern. An informative prior helps most when the data are weak, and it’s honest only if it really comes from earlier knowledge, here the chemistry of similar reactions. (With these four runs, the classical 95% interval would use a t distribution with 2 degrees of freedom, which makes it very wide. This quick calculation treats \sigma as known. A full analysis, like brms’s, would account for it.)

The full model in brms

brms fits the whole model, with priors on all three parameters, and samples them jointly:

kin$inv_T <- 1000 / (kin$temp_C + 273.15)
kin$log_k <- log(kin$k_per_min)
fit <- brm(
  log_k ~ inv_T, data = kin,
  prior = c(prior(normal(-10.2, 1.2), class = b),          # Ea about 85 kJ/mol, give or take 10
            prior(normal(-3, 2), class = Intercept),        # log k at the average temperature
            prior(exponential(10), class = sigma)),         # residual SD, about 0.1 on the log scale
  seed = 5101
)

One detail: brms’s prior on the Intercept applies to the intercept with the predictors centered, here \log k at the average 1000/T, which is much easier to have a prior belief about than \log A. The summary reports the uncentered intercept, \log A = 18.8.

Your turn. The setup code has brms’s draws in draws, with columns intercept, slope and sigma. Summarize the activation energy.

The activation energy comes from the slope.

Ea <- -draws$slope * R
c(mean = mean(Ea), quantile(Ea, c(0.025, 0.975)))

82.3 kJ/mol (80.5 to 84.2), as the quick calculation said. The interval is the same width as the flat-prior one: the prior hardly matters here.

Predictions

The plant is considering running at 230 °C, above any measured temperature. What rate constant would a new run there give? The posterior predictive distribution combines the uncertainty in the line with the run-to-run scatter: for each draw, compute the line at 230 °C, add a normal residual with that draw’s \sigma, and transform back with exp().

Your turn. Find the median and the 95% predictive interval of a new run’s rate constant at 230 °C.

Each draw’s residual has its own SD, draws$sigma.

x_new <- 1000 / (230 + 273.15)
set.seed(5102)
log_k_new <- draws$intercept + draws$slope * x_new + rnorm(nrow(draws), 0, draws$sigma)
k_new <- exp(log_k_new)
c(median = median(k_new), quantile(k_new, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A median of 0.40 per minute, with a 95% predictive interval from 0.36 to 0.45. Without the residual, the interval for the average run would be 0.38 to 0.43. Both assume that the Arrhenius line continues straight beyond 210 °C. No posterior can check that: it’s an assumption about chemistry, not statistics, and the report should say so.

Posterior predictive checks

A model can fit the data badly and still give a confident posterior. The posterior predictive check asks: if the model were right, would data like ours be typical? Simulate many replicated datasets from the posterior predictive distribution, at the same temperatures, and compare them with the real data using a test statistic T(y) chosen to probe a possible failure. The posterior predictive p-value is the share of replicated datasets with T(y^{\text{rep}}) \geq T(y). Values near 0 or 1 mean the real data are unusual for the model.

Suppose someone had fitted a straight line to k itself against the temperature in °C. The Arrhenius equation says k curves upward, so a good test statistic is the curvature: the average of k at the two ends, 140 and 210 °C, minus the average in the middle, 170 and 180 °C. For a straight line, it’s close to 0. For the real data, it’s 0.053.

Your turn. Simulate 4,000 replicated datasets from the flat-prior posterior of the straight-line model, compute the curvature of each, and find the posterior predictive p-value.

Compare with the observed data’s curvature, curvature(kin$k_per_min).

curvature <- function(k) mean(k[kin$temp_C %in% c(140, 210)]) - mean(k[kin$temp_C %in% c(170, 180)])
line <- lm(k_per_min ~ temp_C, data = kin)
X <- model.matrix(line)
n <- nrow(X)
s2 <- sum(resid(line)^2) / (n - 2)
set.seed(5103)
sigma2 <- (n - 2) * s2 / rchisq(4000, n - 2)
beta <- matrix(coef(line), 4000, 2, byrow = TRUE) + (matrix(rnorm(8000), 4000, 2) %*% chol(solve(crossprod(X)))) * sqrt(sigma2)
T_rep <- sapply(1:4000, function(s) curvature(X %*% beta[s, ] + rnorm(n, 0, sqrt(sigma2[s]))))
c(T_observed = curvature(kin$k_per_min), p_value = mean(T_rep >= curvature(kin$k_per_min)))

A posterior predictive p-value of 0.004: only 15 of 4,000 replicated datasets curve as much as the real data. The straight line can’t produce data like these, and the check says so, without reference to any other model. It has a second giveaway, too: in 97% of the posterior draws, the line predicts a negative rate constant somewhere in the range. A good test statistic targets what a model might get wrong, and that takes knowing the science.

Here are the two models’ 90% predictive bands for a new run, on the rate constant’s own scale:

grid_T <- seq(140, 210, by = 1)
set.seed(5105)
arr <- t(sapply(grid_T, function(tc) quantile(exp(draws$intercept + draws$slope * 1000 / (tc + 273.15) + rnorm(nrow(draws), 0, draws$sigma)), c(0.05, 0.95))))
line <- lm(k_per_min ~ temp_C, data = kin)
lin <- predict(line, data.frame(temp_C = grid_T), interval = "prediction", level = 0.9)
bands <- rbind(data.frame(temp = grid_T, lo = arr[, 1], hi = arr[, 2], model = "Arrhenius (log k against 1/T)"),
               data.frame(temp = grid_T, lo = lin[, "lwr"], hi = lin[, "upr"], model = "straight line (k against T)"))
ggplot(bands, aes(x = temp)) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi, fill = model), alpha = 0.45) +
  geom_point(data = kin, aes(x = temp_C, y = k_per_min), color = "#3A1F35", size = 2) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, color = "grey50") +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c(`Arrhenius (log k against 1/T)` = "#C2416C", `straight line (k against T)` = "#2a78d6"), name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Temperature (°C)", y = "Rate constant k (per minute)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) +
  theme(legend.position = "top")

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · The same check for the Arrhenius model. Use brms’s draws to simulate a replicated dataset for each draw, from the Arrhenius model, and compute the posterior predictive p-value for the curvature.

The observed statistic is curvature(kin$k_per_min).

curvature <- function(k) mean(k[kin$temp_C %in% c(140, 210)]) - mean(k[kin$temp_C %in% c(170, 180)])
X <- cbind(1, kin$inv_T)
set.seed(5104)
T_rep <- sapply(1:nrow(draws), function(s) curvature(exp(X %*% c(draws$intercept[s], draws$slope[s]) + rnorm(nrow(kin), 0, draws$sigma[s]))))
mean(T_rep >= curvature(kin$k_per_min))

0.72: the real data’s curvature is entirely typical for the Arrhenius model. A posterior predictive check can’t prove that a model is right, only find ways in which it’s wrong. This one found none. Lesson 13 adds more checks, and ways to compare models.

Challenge 2 · How long does a batch take? The reaction is first order, so the time to 99% conversion is \log(100)/k. At 200 °C, what’s its posterior median and 95% credible interval, for the average run (no residual)?

99% conversion leaves 1% of the reactant, so the time is \log(100)/k.

k_200 <- exp(draws$intercept + draws$slope * 1000 / (200 + 273.15))
t99 <- log(100) / k_200
c(median = median(t99), quantile(t99, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A median of 39.9 minutes, with a 95% credible interval from 38.4 to 41.5. Any quantity computed from the parameters, however nonlinear, gets its posterior from the draws, without the delta method or any other approximation. That’s often the most practical advantage of a Bayesian regression.

Quick quiz

1. With the prior p(\beta, \sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2, how do the posterior credible intervals of a linear regression compare with the least squares confidence intervals?

The posterior of each coefficient is a t distribution centered at the estimate, with the standard error as its scale.

2. When does an informative prior on a coefficient change the answer most?

With precisions adding, the prior’s share is large only when the data’s precision is small.

3. In brms, what does a prior on class = Intercept apply to, by default?

brms centers the predictors internally, which makes the intercept easier to have a belief about, and helps the sampler.

4. What does a posterior predictive interval include that a credible interval for the regression line doesn’t?

The line’s uncertainty plus the residual variation.

5. A posterior predictive check gives a p-value of 0.004. What does that suggest?

It points to a specific failure, here curvature that a straight line can’t produce.

6. What’s a good test statistic for a posterior predictive check?

The mean is usually fitted well by any model. Curvature, extremes, or runs of residuals are more revealing.

Wrap-up

You can now do Bayesian regression, and check the models you fit. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The flat-prior posterior of a linear regression can be simulated exactly. For the Arrhenius model, it gives E_a = 82.3 kJ/mol (80.4 to 84.2), the same as least squares.
  • Informative priors matter when the data are thin: with all 16 runs, the literature prior got 0.8% of the weight. With 4 runs over 10 degrees, it got a third.
  • brms fits the full model with priors on every parameter, and centers the predictors for its intercept prior.
  • Predictions come from the draws: a run at 230 °C would give 0.40 per minute (0.36 to 0.45), if the line continues straight.
  • Posterior predictive checks catch misfit: a straight line in k couldn’t reproduce the data’s curvature (p = 0.004), but the Arrhenius model could (p = 0.72).

NoteKey words from this lesson
Bayesian linear regression
A linear regression with a prior on the coefficients and the residual SD, giving a posterior for all of them.
Activation energy
The E_a in the Arrhenius equation, k = A e^{-E_a/(RT)}: how strongly a reaction’s rate depends on temperature.
Posterior predictive distribution
The distribution of a new observation, combining the uncertainty in the parameters with the residual variation.
Posterior predictive check
Comparing the observed data with datasets simulated from the posterior predictive distribution, to look for misfit.
Replicated data
A dataset simulated from the model with parameters drawn from the posterior, at the same predictor values as the real data.
Test statistic
A summary of a dataset, chosen to probe a possible failure of the model, compared between the real and replicated data.
Posterior predictive p-value
The share of replicated datasets whose test statistic is at least as extreme as the observed one.
