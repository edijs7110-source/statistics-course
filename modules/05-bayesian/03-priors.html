Choosing priors

Saying what you know, and only what you know

A new gasket passed 25 pressure tests without a leak: what’s its leak probability? With zero events, the prior matters, so compare informative, weakly informative and noninformative priors, including the Jeffreys prior, and see why a flat prior on one scale isn’t flat on another. Use prior predictive checks to catch priors that predict impossible data, run a sensitivity analysis, and build a robust mixture prior that knows when to stop trusting the vendor.

Module 5 · Lesson 3 of 13 Week 78 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The high-pressure steam line is getting a new kind of gasket. Before it goes into service, the maintenance team pressure-tested 25 of them on the test rig at 1.5 times the design pressure, and none leaked. The plant’s rule is that a new gasket needs a leak probability below 5% under test conditions. Does this one qualify?

Zero events is where the prior matters most. The data say the leak probability is “small”, but not how small, and different reasonable priors give different answers. So how do you choose? This lesson is about the main kinds of prior, and about two tools that keep you honest: prior predictive checks, which ask whether a prior makes sense before you see the data, and sensitivity analysis, which asks how much the conclusions depend on it.

No leaks in 25 tests! Champagne? Not so fast. “None so far” isn’t the same as “never”. Let’s see how sure we can be.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • compare informative, weakly informative and noninformative priors
  • meet the Jeffreys prior, and see why flat isn’t the same as uninformative
  • check priors by simulating the data they predict
  • run a sensitivity analysis
  • build a robust mixture prior

Noninformative priors

The natural first idea is a prior that says nothing: the flat Beta(1, 1). With 0 leaks in 25 tests, the posterior is Beta(1, 26).

Your turn. Find the flat-prior posterior mean, and the 95% upper credible bound: the value the leak probability is below with 95% probability.

The upper bound is the 95th percentile, 0.95.

a <- 1 + y
b <- 1 + n - y
c(mean = a / (a + b), upper_95 = qbeta(0.95, a, b))

A posterior mean of 3.7%, and a 95% upper bound of 10.9%. So with a flat prior, the gasket doesn’t demonstrably meet the 5% rule: leak probabilities up to 11% are still plausible after 25 clean tests. Classical statistics has a famous shortcut for zero events, the rule of three: the 95% upper confidence bound is about 3/n, here 12%. Challenge 2 compares the two.

But is “flat” really “uninformative”? Flat on the leak probability \theta says that a probability between 0% and 1% is as likely as one between 50% and 51%. On the log-odds scale, \log(\theta/(1-\theta)), which logistic regression uses, the same prior isn’t flat at all: it piles up near 0. Every prior is flat on some scale and informative on others, so “flat” isn’t a property of your knowledge, it’s a property of your parameterization.

The Jeffreys prior is a way around that. It’s built from the Fisher information (Module 4’s curvature of the log-likelihood), and it gives the same answers whichever way you parameterize the model. For a binomial probability, it’s Beta(½, ½), which puts a little more weight near 0 and 1 than the flat prior does.

Your turn. Repeat the analysis with the Jeffreys prior.

The Jeffreys prior is Beta(0.5, 0.5).

a <- 0.5 + y
b <- 0.5 + n - y
c(mean = a / (a + b), upper_95 = qbeta(0.95, a, b))

A mean of 1.9% and an upper bound of 7.3%. Two “noninformative” priors, and the posterior means differ by a factor of two. With 25 tests and no leaks, the data are too thin for the choice not to matter. (Don’t try Beta(0, 0), the Haldane prior, here. It isn’t a proper distribution, and with zero events its posterior, Beta(0, 25), isn’t either. An improper prior is sometimes fine, but you must always check that the posterior is proper.)

Your turn. The opposite trap: a “vague” prior on the log-odds scale. A normal prior with mean 0 and SD 10 looks harmless there. Draw 100,000 values from it, turn them into probabilities with plogis(), and see what it says about \theta.

The SD is 10.

set.seed(5031)
theta <- plogis(rnorm(100000, mean = 0, sd = 10))
c(below_1pct = mean(theta < 0.01), above_99pct = mean(theta > 0.99))

65% of the prior is below 1% or above 99%: 32% for “almost never leaks” and 32% for “almost always leaks”. That’s a strong, strange belief, not a vague one. With an SD of 1.5 on the log-odds scale, only 0.2% of the prior is that extreme. Lesson 10 uses priors like that for regression coefficients.

Informative priors, and prior predictive checks

The gasket’s vendor has its own test data: 2 leaks in 200 gaskets, at the same test pressure. If the plant’s conditions match the vendor’s, that’s real information, and an informative prior uses it: roughly Beta(2, 198), with a mean of 1%, worth 200 tests.

How do you check a prior? You can’t compare it with the data you’re about to analyze, but you can ask what data it predicts. That’s a prior predictive check: simulate data from the prior, before looking at yours, and see whether they’re plausible. For the gasket, the prior predictive probability of 0 leaks in 25 is the beta-binomial probability from Lesson 2, B(a, b + 25)/B(a, b).

Your turn. Compute it for the flat prior and for the vendor’s prior.

The denominator is the beta function of the vendor’s prior, beta(2, 198).

c(flat = beta(1, 1 + 25) / beta(1, 1), vendor = beta(2, 198 + 25) / beta(2, 198))

The flat prior predicted 0 leaks in 25 with probability 3.8%. It thought a clean test run was unusual, because it gave as much weight to a 60% leak rate as to a 1% one. Nobody at the plant thought that. The vendor’s prior gave it 79%: the result it expected. Neither number proves that a prior is right, but a prior that’s surprised by the data everyone expected deserves a second look.

Prior predictive checks matter even more for models with several parameters, where nobody can picture the joint prior. Take Module 2’s yield model, \text{yield} = \alpha + \beta\,(T - 180), for reactor temperatures T from 160 to 200 °C. “Vague” priors such as normal distributions with mean 0 and SD 100 for both \alpha and \beta look safe.

Your turn. Simulate 10,000 pairs (\alpha, \beta) from those priors, compute the yields they imply at the five temperatures in the setup code’s temps, and find the share of predicted yields that are impossible, below 0% or above 100%.

The model uses T - 180.

set.seed(5032)
alpha <- rnorm(10000, 0, 100)
beta <- rnorm(10000, 0, 100)
yields <- outer(alpha, rep(1, 5)) + outer(beta, temps - 180)    # a row of 5 yields for each pair
mean(yields < 0 | yields > 100)

91% of the predicted yields are impossible. The “vague” priors say the yield is most likely thousands of percent, or negative. With 40 batches of data, the likelihood will overwhelm them, so the posterior will be fine. But in bigger models with less data, priors like these can cause real trouble, and they always make the sampler’s job harder.

Weakly informative priors

Between “nothing” and “the vendor’s data” is the most useful kind of prior in practice: a weakly informative one. It rules out the absurd without pretending to know the answer. For the yield model: the yield at 180 °C is probably 60% to 100% (a normal prior for \alpha with mean 80 and SD 8), and a 1 °C change in temperature probably changes it by less than 1 percentage point (a normal prior for \beta with mean 0 and SD 0.5).

Your turn. Repeat the prior predictive check with these priors.

The SD for \beta is 0.5.

set.seed(5032)
alpha <- rnorm(10000, 80, 8)
beta <- rnorm(10000, 0, 0.5)
yields <- outer(alpha, rep(1, 5)) + outer(beta, temps - 180)
mean(yields < 0 | yields > 100)

3%. The weakly informative priors still allow a wide range, yields from about 55% to 105% at 200 °C, and only a few impossible ones. They weren’t made up from the data; they come from knowing what a yield is and roughly how chemistry responds to temperature. That’s the kind of knowledge priors are for.

Here are 40 lines from each prior, the yield against temperature that each (\alpha, \beta) pair implies:

set.seed(5033)
draw_lines <- function(a, b, label) {
  data.frame(id = rep(1:40, each = 2), temp = rep(c(160, 200), 40),
             yield = rep(a, each = 2) + rep(b, each = 2) * (rep(c(160, 200), 40) - 180), prior = label)
}
lines <- rbind(draw_lines(rnorm(40, 0, 100), rnorm(40, 0, 100), "vague: SD 100 for both"),
               draw_lines(rnorm(40, 80, 8), rnorm(40, 0, 0.5), "weakly informative"))
lines$prior <- factor(lines$prior, levels = c("vague: SD 100 for both", "weakly informative"))
ggplot(lines, aes(x = temp, y = yield, group = id)) +
  annotate("rect", xmin = 160, xmax = 200, ymin = 0, ymax = 100, fill = "#F4C0D1", alpha = 0.6) +
  geom_line(color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.4, alpha = 0.7) +
  facet_wrap(~ prior, scales = "free_y") +
  labs(x = "Temperature (°C)", y = "Predicted yield (%)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The shaded band is the possible range. Note the y-axes: the vague priors’ lines run off into thousands of percent.

Sensitivity analysis

When different reasonable priors could be argued for, analyze the data with several, and report how much the conclusions move. If they hardly move, the data have spoken. If they move a lot, that’s an important finding too: it says the data alone can’t settle the question.

Your turn. For the gasket, compute the posterior mean and the 95% upper bound with four priors: flat, Jeffreys, a weakly informative Beta(1, 9) (a leak rate of about 10% or less, worth 10 tests) and the vendor’s Beta(2, 198).

The number of tests without a leak, n - y, goes to the second parameter.

priors <- list(flat = c(1, 1), jeffreys = c(0.5, 0.5), weak = c(1, 9), vendor = c(2, 198))
t(sapply(priors, function(ab) {
  a <- ab[1] + y
  b <- ab[2] + n - y
  c(mean = a / (a + b), upper_95 = qbeta(0.95, a, b))
}))
Prior Posterior mean 95% upper bound
Flat 3.7% 10.9%
Jeffreys 1.9% 7.3%
Weakly informative 2.9% 8.4%
Vendor’s data 0.9% 2.1%

Only the vendor’s prior says the gasket meets the 5% rule. So the honest report is: “On our own 25 tests, the gasket hasn’t yet shown that its leak probability is below 5%; it will if the vendor’s tests apply to our conditions.” That puts the real question, whether the vendor’s test rig is like the plant’s, in front of the people who can answer it. About 50 tests without a leak would settle it with the weakly informative prior alone.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · A prior that knows when to back off. The risk with the vendor’s prior is that the vendor’s conditions might not match the plant’s. A mixture prior hedges: 70% weight on the vendor’s Beta(2, 198), and 30% on a flat Beta(1, 1), in case the vendor’s data don’t apply. Compute the posterior on a fine grid, and the posterior weight of the vendor’s part. Then imagine the 25 tests had found 3 leaks instead, and do it again.

The denominator adds both parts of the prior: vendor_part + flat_part.

grid <- seq(0, 1, by = 0.0001)
vendor_part <- 0.7 * dbeta(grid, 2, 198)
flat_part <- 0.3 * dbeta(grid, 1, 1)
weight_vendor <- function(leaks) {
  lik <- dbinom(leaks, 25, grid)
  sum(vendor_part * lik) / sum((vendor_part + flat_part) * lik)
}
c(no_leaks = weight_vendor(0), three_leaks = weight_vendor(3))

With no leaks, the vendor’s part gains weight, from 70% to 98%: the plant’s data agree with the vendor’s, so the posterior trusts them more, with a mean of 0.95%. With 3 leaks, its weight falls to 20%, and the posterior mean is 12%. The vendor’s prior on its own would have said 2.2%, explaining the leaks away. The mixture prior lets the data decide whether the prior applies. That’s often the right compromise when prior information comes from somewhere else.

Challenge 2 · The rule of three. Compare the classical rule of three, 3/n, with the flat-prior Bayesian 95% upper bound, qbeta(0.95, 1, n + 1), for 0 events in n = 10, 25, 100 and 1,000 tests.

With 0 events, the flat-prior posterior is Beta(1, n + 1): n_tests + 1.

n_tests <- c(10, 25, 100, 1000)
rbind(rule_of_three = 3 / n_tests, bayes_flat = qbeta(0.95, 1, n_tests + 1))

With 10 tests, 30% against 24%. With 1,000, 0.30% against 0.30%. The two agree more and more closely as n grows, and with so many tests, the prior hardly matters. The rule comes from (1 - \theta)^n = 0.05, so \theta = 1 - 0.05^{1/n} \approx -\log(0.05)/n \approx 3/n, and the flat-prior bound is 1 - 0.05^{1/(n + 1)}, almost the same formula.

Quick quiz

1. Why isn’t a flat prior on a probability “uninformative” in an absolute sense?

Flatness depends on the parameterization. The Jeffreys prior is one way to get answers that don’t.

2. What is a prior predictive check?

If a prior predicts yields of 3,000%, it doesn’t express what you know.

3. What does a weakly informative prior do?

For example, a normal prior with mean 80% and SD 8% for a yield: wide, but no impossible values to speak of.

4. Four reasonable priors give posterior 95% upper bounds from 2% to 11%. What should you report?

That’s the point of a sensitivity analysis: it shows how much the data alone can tell you.

5. You use an improper prior, such as Beta(0, 0). What must you check?

With zero events, the Beta(0, 0) prior gives a Beta(0, n) posterior, which isn’t a proper distribution.

6. What’s the advantage of a mixture prior, such as 70% vendor data and 30% flat?

A robust prior lets the data decide whether outside information applies.

Wrap-up

You can now choose priors with your eyes open. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Noninformative priors aren’t unique: with 0 leaks in 25 tests, the flat prior gives a mean of 3.7% and the Jeffreys prior 1.9%.
  • Flat depends on the scale: a normal prior with SD 10 on the log-odds puts 65% of its weight below 1% or above 99%.
  • Prior predictive checks: the “vague” yield priors predicted 91% impossible yields, the weakly informative ones 3%.
  • Informative priors carry real knowledge, such as the vendor’s tests, but only if it applies.
  • Sensitivity analysis: the gasket meets the 5% rule only with the vendor’s prior. That’s the finding to report.
  • Robust mixture priors trust outside information when the data agree with it, and drop it when they don’t.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Noninformative prior
A prior meant to add as little information as possible, such as a flat or Jeffreys prior. No prior is noninformative on every scale.
Jeffreys prior
A prior proportional to the square root of the Fisher information. It gives the same answers however the model is parameterized. For a binomial probability, it’s Beta(½, ½).
Improper prior
A “prior” that doesn’t integrate to a finite value, such as a flat prior on the whole real line. It’s acceptable only if the posterior is proper.
Informative prior
A prior that carries real knowledge, for example from earlier studies.
Weakly informative prior
A prior that rules out implausible values without claiming to know the answer.
Prior predictive check
Simulating data from the prior to see whether it predicts plausible data.
Sensitivity analysis
Repeating the analysis with different reasonable priors, and reporting how much the conclusions change.
Mixture prior
A weighted combination of priors, such as outside information and a vague prior. The posterior shifts its weight toward the part that agrees with the data.
