Statistical distance

How unusual is a batch, when it has four numbers instead of one?

Measure how far an observation is from the center of multivariate data: see why Euclidean distance fails with mixed units and correlated variables, standardize, use the Mahalanobis distance, draw ellipses of constant distance, and judge how far is far with the chi-square distribution.

Module 3 · Lesson 2 of 13 Week 48 of 160 About 5–7 hours

With one variable, “unusual” is easy: a z-score of 3 is unusual. With four, it’s harder than it looks. A batch can be perfectly ordinary on every property separately and still be very strange as a whole, because the combination doesn’t fit the way the properties usually go together. This lesson builds the right ruler for multivariate data, the Mahalanobis distance, and uses it to catch a batch that every one-at-a-time check would let through.

Picture a batch that’s a little too pure and a little too acidic. Neither is alarming on its own. But together? That’s like a cake that’s both very dry and very soggy. Something’s off!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • see why ordinary (Euclidean) distance fails with mixed units and correlated variables
  • standardize the variables, and see why that’s still not enough
  • compute the Mahalanobis distance, by hand and with mahalanobis()
  • draw ellipses of constant distance, and understand their shape
  • decide how far is far, with the chi-square distribution

Euclidean distance

The obvious way to measure how far a batch is from the average batch is the straight-line, Euclidean distance between their observation vectors:

d(\mathbf{x}, \bar{\mathbf{x}}) = \sqrt{(x_1 - \bar{x}_1)^2 + \dots + (x_p - \bar{x}_p)^2} = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})} .

It treats every direction as equally important, and that’s the problem. A difference of 10 ppm of water counts as much as a difference of 10 percentage points of purity, which would be a catastrophe. In practice, the Euclidean distance between batches is just the difference in water content, because water is measured in big numbers.

Your turn. Compute every batch’s Euclidean distance from the mean vector, and find the most distant batch.

sweep(Y, 2, xbar) subtracts the mean vector from every row. Find the batch with the largest d.

d <- sqrt(rowSums(sweep(Y, 2, xbar)^2))
names(which.max(d))

FS51, at a distance of 87. It’s the wettest batch, with 218 ppm of water. That might be genuinely unusual, but this ruler couldn’t tell us anything else: the purity, color and acid barely contribute.

Standardizing is better, but not enough

The units problem has an easy fix: convert every variable to z-scores first, so that each one counts in standard deviations. The standardized distance is

d_z = \sqrt{z_1^2 + \dots + z_p^2}, \qquad z_j = \frac{x_j - \bar{x}_j}{s_j} .

Now every property gets a fair say. But one problem remains. Look at water against acid, in standard units, with two batches picked out:

Z <- scale(Y)
r <- cor(Y)[2, 4]
theta <- seq(0, 2 * pi, length.out = 200)
e2 <- eigen(matrix(c(1, r, r, 1), 2))
ellipse <- function(c2) {
  pts <- t(e2$vectors %*% diag(sqrt(c2 * e2$values)) %*% rbind(cos(theta), sin(theta)))
  tibble(x = pts[, 1], y = pts[, 2])
}
circle <- tibble(x = sqrt(qchisq(0.99, 2)) * cos(theta), y = sqrt(qchisq(0.99, 2)) * sin(theta))
pts <- tibble(batch = rownames(Z), x = Z[, "water_ppm"], y = Z[, "acid_mgKOH"]) |>
  mutate(type = case_when(batch == "FS73" ~ "FS73", batch == "FS51" ~ "FS51", TRUE ~ "other"))
ggplot(pts, aes(x = x, y = y)) +
  geom_path(data = circle, color = "grey65", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_path(data = ellipse(qchisq(0.99, 2)), color = "#2a78d6", linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_path(data = ellipse(qchisq(0.5, 2)), color = "#2a78d6", linewidth = 0.4) +
  geom_path(data = ellipse(13.3), color = "#C2416C", linetype = "dotted", linewidth = 0.7) +
  geom_point(aes(color = type, size = type)) +
  geom_text(data = filter(pts, type != "other"), aes(label = batch), nudge_x = 0.45, size = 3.5) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c(FS73 = "#C2416C", FS51 = "#1baf7a", other = "grey55")) +
  scale_size_manual(values = c(FS73 = 3, FS51 = 3, other = 1.6)) +
  coord_equal() +
  labs(x = "Water (z-score)", y = "Acid (z-score)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) + theme(legend.position = "none")

The dashed circle is a set of points at the same standardized distance from the center. But the data don’t fill a circle. Water and acid are correlated (r = 0.58), so the cloud is stretched along the diagonal, an ellipse, not a circle. FS51 (green) sits at the far end of the cloud: extreme, but in a direction the data often go. FS73 (rose) is closer to the center by standardized distance, but it sits in a direction where the data almost never go: low water with high acid. Water makes acid, so that combination needs explaining.

Your turn. Compute FS73’s standardized distance from the center, using all four properties.

Square the z-scores and add them up across each row: rowSums(Z^2).

Z <- scale(Y)
dz <- sqrt(rowSums(Z^2))
dz["FS73"]

2.62. That’s only the 16th largest of the 80 batches: by this ruler, FS73 is unremarkable. Its z-scores are 1.1 for purity, −1.6 for water, −0.3 for color and 1.7 for acid. None of them would raise an eyebrow on its own.

The Mahalanobis distance

The right ruler accounts for the correlations as well as the spreads. It’s the Mahalanobis distance, introduced by the Indian statistician P. C. Mahalanobis in 1936:

D^2(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top S^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) .

It’s the squared Euclidean distance with S^{-1} in the middle, and the inverse covariance matrix is what does the work. Remember the end of last lesson: S^{-1} magnifies the directions in which the data hardly vary. So a step in a direction the data often take counts for little, and the same step in a direction the data rarely take counts for a lot. With uncorrelated variables, D^2 is exactly the sum of the squared z-scores, and with one variable it’s just z^2.

R’s mahalanobis(x, center, cov) returns D^2, already squared, for every row:

d2 <- mahalanobis(Y, colMeans(Y), cov(Y))
head(sort(d2, decreasing = TRUE), 4)
#>      FS73      FS51      FS08      FS24 
#> 34.616060 12.354437  9.574946  9.462807

FS73’s squared distance is 34.6. The next most unusual batch is at 12.4. FS73 is by far the strangest batch of the quarter, and no single property says so.

NoteGo deeper: Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance after “whitening”

Use the spectral decomposition, S = P\Lambda P^\top, so that S^{-1} = P\Lambda^{-1}P^\top. Then

D^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top P \Lambda^{-1} P^\top (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) = \sum_{j=1}^{p} \frac{y_j^2}{\lambda_j}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = P^\top(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) .

In words: rotate the data onto the principal axes (the eigenvectors), divide each coordinate by its standard deviation along that axis, \sqrt{\lambda_j}, and measure ordinary distance. That turns the elliptical cloud into a round one, with every direction equally variable and no correlations left. It’s called whitening, and the matrix that does it is S^{-1/2} = P\Lambda^{-1/2}P^\top. The second challenge has you check it.

A small eigenvalue \lambda_j is a direction where the data barely spread, so dividing by it makes even a modest step there count a lot. FS73 stepped out in one of those directions.

Your turn. Find the batch with the largest Mahalanobis distance.

The covariance matrix is S.

d2 <- mahalanobis(Y, center = xbar, cov = S)
sort(d2, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3]

FS73 with 34.6, then FS51 with 12.4 and FS08 with 9.6. FS51, the “most distant” batch by the Euclidean ruler, is second here: it’s extreme, but in a typical direction.

Your turn. Compute FS73’s squared distance by hand, from the formula.

solve(S) is the inverse of the covariance matrix.

dev <- Y["FS73", ] - xbar
t(dev) %*% solve(S) %*% dev

34.6, the same as mahalanobis(). FS73’s deviations from the mean are +0.41 points of purity, −47 ppm of water, −1.2 APHA and +0.051 mg KOH/g of acid. Purer than average but more acidic, and drier but more acidic: both clash with how the properties usually move together.

Ellipses of constant distance

All the points at the same Mahalanobis distance from the center form an ellipse (in more dimensions, an ellipsoid). In the plot above, the blue curves are two such ellipses, for water and acid: the inner one at the distance that half the batches fall within, the outer one at the 99% mark. Their shape comes straight from the eigen-decomposition:

  • the axes point along the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix;
  • the half-length of each axis is \sqrt{c\,\lambda_j}, for an ellipse at squared distance c.

So the ellipse is long in the direction of the biggest eigenvalue, where the data spread most, and short where they spread least. In standard units, the two eigenvalues of a 2 \times 2 correlation matrix are 1 + r and 1 - r (Lesson 0.14), so the stronger the correlation, the longer and thinner the ellipse. FS51 and FS73 both lie outside the 99% ellipse. But FS51 is far out along the ellipse’s long axis, 4.1 standard units from the center, while FS73 is only 2.4 standard units out, across the short axis. Measured with the ellipse, FS73 is the more unusual of the two: the dotted rose ellipse passes through FS73, at a squared distance of 13.3 for this pair, and FS51, at 10.6, lies inside it.

How far is far?

If the data come from a multivariate normal distribution (next lesson), the squared Mahalanobis distances of the observations follow, approximately, a chi-square distribution with p degrees of freedom: one for each variable. That’s the multivariate version of “a squared z-score is chi-square with one degree of freedom”. It gives a yardstick:

qchisq(c(0.95, 0.99, 0.999), df = 4)
#> [1]  9.487729 13.276704 18.466827

About 1 batch in 100 should have D^2 above 13.3. Among the 80 batches, three are above the 95% mark, about the four you’d expect by chance, and only FS73 is above the 99% mark. Plotting every batch’s D^2 in production order gives a multivariate control chart, also called a Hotelling T^2 chart, which many chemical plants run alongside their single-variable charts precisely to catch batches like FS73:

tibble(order = seq_along(d2), d2 = d2, batch = names(d2)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = order, y = d2)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = qchisq(0.99, 4), color = "#C2416C", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_line(color = "grey75") +
  geom_point(color = "#2a78d6", size = 1.8) +
  annotate("text", x = 73, y = 34.6, label = "FS73", hjust = 1.3, size = 3.5) +
  labs(x = "Batch, in production order", y = "Squared Mahalanobis distance") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Two cautions. First, the chi-square yardstick is only approximate: the mean and covariance are estimated from the same data, and the outlier itself inflates S, which makes it look a little less extreme than it is. With 80 batches and 4 variables, the approximation is reasonable. Second, with 80 batches checked, the most extreme one is always somewhat large, so judge the largest distance with a Bonferroni-style correction, as in Lesson 2.10.

So what happened to FS73? The production records show that its reactor had been cleaned with a dilute acid wash just before the batch, and it seems not all of the wash was rinsed out. No single test flagged the batch, and it passed its specification. The customer, whose process is sensitive to acid, noticed.

Your turn. Compute the chi-square p-value for FS73’s squared distance, and its Bonferroni-adjusted value for having checked 80 batches.

The degrees of freedom are the number of variables, p = 4.

d2 <- mahalanobis(Y, xbar, S)
p <- pchisq(d2["FS73"], df = 4, lower.tail = FALSE)
c(p = unname(p), bonferroni = min(1, 80 * unname(p)))

p = 5.6 \times 10^{-7}, and 4.5 \times 10^{-5} even after the correction. FS73 doesn’t belong with the others.

Your turn. Which pair of properties makes FS73 unusual? Compute its squared Mahalanobis distance using water and acid only.

The second column is "acid_mgKOH".

W <- Y[, c("water_ppm", "acid_mgKOH")]
mahalanobis(W, colMeans(W), cov(W))["FS73"]

13.3, the largest of all 80 batches for this pair, and beyond the 99.8% point of a chi-square with 2 degrees of freedom. Purity with acid gives 11.5. Every other pair gives about 4 or less. FS73 is unusual because its acid doesn’t match its water, or its purity.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Whitening. Build S^{-1/2} = P\Lambda^{-1/2}P^\top from the eigen-decomposition of S, transform the centered data with it, and compute FS73’s squared Euclidean distance in the transformed (“whitened”) space.

Take the square roots of the eigenvalues, e$values.

e <- eigen(S)
S_inv_half <- e$vectors %*% diag(1 / sqrt(e$values)) %*% t(e$vectors)
white <- sweep(Y, 2, xbar) %*% S_inv_half
sum(white[which(rownames(Y) == "FS73"), ]^2)

34.6 again. In the whitened space, ordinary Euclidean distance is the Mahalanobis distance. Check round(cov(white), 10): it’s the identity matrix, so the whitened variables are uncorrelated, with variance 1.

Challenge 2 · The ellipse’s axes. For water and acid in standard units, the correlation matrix has eigenvalues 1 + r and 1 - r. Compute the half-lengths of the axes of the 95% ellipse, \sqrt{c\,\lambda_j} with c the 95% point of the chi-square distribution with 2 degrees of freedom.

The second eigenvalue is 1 - r.

r <- cor(Y[, "water_ppm"], Y[, "acid_mgKOH"])
c95 <- qchisq(0.95, df = 2)
sqrt(c95 * c(1 + r, 1 - r))

3.08 along the diagonal “wetter and more acid” direction, and 1.58 across it. The ellipse is twice as long as it is wide. With r = 0.9, it would be more than four times as long: \sqrt{1.9/0.1} = 4.4.

Quick quiz

1. Why is the Euclidean distance a poor measure of how unusual a batch is?

In ppm, water dominates it. Standardizing fixes the units, but not the correlations.

2. Two variables are strongly positively correlated. Which of these points is more unusual, at the same standardized distance from the center?

High-high is a direction the data often take. High-low goes against the correlation, so its Mahalanobis distance is larger.

3. What does the inverse covariance matrix do in D^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top S^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})?

S^{-1} divides by the variance along each principal axis, so rare directions count more.

4. With uncorrelated variables, the squared Mahalanobis distance equals…

With a diagonal S, S^{-1} just divides each squared deviation by its variance.

5. What shape is a set of points at the same Mahalanobis distance from the center of two correlated variables?

The half-length of each axis is \sqrt{c\lambda_j}, so the ellipse is longest in the direction of greatest spread.

6. For multivariate normal data with p = 4 variables, what distribution do the squared Mahalanobis distances approximately follow?

One degree of freedom per variable. The 99% point, 13.3, makes a convenient flag for unusual observations.

Wrap-up

You now have the right ruler for multivariate data! Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Euclidean distance treats every direction alike. With mixed units, the variable with the biggest numbers dominates it.
  • Standardized distance fixes the units, but still ignores the correlations, so it misses unusual combinations.
  • The Mahalanobis distance, D^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top S^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}), accounts for both. mahalanobis() computes it. It’s Euclidean distance after whitening the data with S^{-1/2}.
  • Ellipses of constant distance have axes along the eigenvectors, with half-lengths \sqrt{c\lambda_j}.
  • How far is far: for multivariate normal data, D^2 is approximately chi-square with p degrees of freedom. Plotted over time, it’s a multivariate control chart.
  • FS73 was ordinary on every property, but its acid didn’t fit its water or its purity: D^2 = 34.6, with a Bonferroni-adjusted p = 4.5 \times 10^{-5}. An acid wash residue was the culprit.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Euclidean distance
The straight-line distance between two vectors, \sqrt{(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})^\top(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})}.
Standardized distance
The Euclidean distance between the z-scores.
Mahalanobis distance
A distance that accounts for the variances and correlations of the variables: D^2 = (\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top S^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}).
Whitening
Transforming data with S^{-1/2} so that the variables become uncorrelated with variance 1. Euclidean distance after whitening is the Mahalanobis distance.
Ellipse (ellipsoid) of constant distance
The set of points at a given Mahalanobis distance from the center, with axes along the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.
Multivariate control chart
A plot of each observation’s squared Mahalanobis distance in time order, with a control limit, used to monitor several variables at once. Also called a Hotelling T^2 chart.
