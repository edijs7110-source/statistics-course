Nested designs

Analysts within labs, repeats within analysts: where does the variation come from?

Analyze an interlaboratory study with nested factors: fit a nested ANOVA and test each factor against the right error term, estimate variance components for labs, analysts and repeats, fit the same model as a mixed model with lme4, and run a nested MANOVA of two properties.

Module 3 · Lesson 10 of 13 Week 56 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The plant and its biggest customer keep disagreeing about the product’s water content: the customer’s lab finds more water than the plant’s lab does. Is the product changing in transit, or are the labs measuring differently? To find out, the quality department organized an interlaboratory study. One well-mixed reference sample went to four labs: the plant’s, the customer’s, and two contract labs. In each lab, three analysts each measured it three times, for purity and water.

It’s the same sample everywhere, so every difference in the 36 results is a measurement difference. The questions: do the labs disagree? Do the analysts within a lab? And how much of the variation comes from each source?

Same sample, four labs, twelve analysts, thirty-six results. If they all agreed, this lesson would be very short!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • tell nested factors from crossed ones
  • fit a nested ANOVA, and test each factor against the right error term
  • estimate variance components: how much variation comes from labs, analysts and repeats
  • fit the same model as a mixed model with lme4
  • run a nested MANOVA of both properties

The interlaboratory study

Here are the 36 results, one panel per lab, with each analyst’s mean as a bar:

interlab |>

  mutate(property = factor(property, labels = c("Purity (%)", "Water (ppm)"))) |>

  theme(strip.placement = "outside", axis.text.x = element_text(angle = 45, hjust = 1))

Two things stand out. The customer lab’s purity results are higher than everyone else’s, and contract lab 2’s water results are well above everyone else’s. Within each lab, the analysts’ means also differ, though by less.

Group by lab.

interlab |>

The plant lab finds 137.7 ppm of water and the customer lab 149.5 ppm, so the complaint is real. Contract lab 2 finds even more, 162.3 ppm. For purity, the customer lab is the odd one out, at 99.25% against 99.00% to 99.07% elsewhere.

Nested, not crossed

In Lesson 8, the factors were crossed: every catalyst was run at every temperature. Here, the analysts are nested in the labs: each analyst works in only one lab, so “analyst 1” at the plant lab has nothing to do with “analyst 1” at the customer lab. There’s no lab × analyst interaction to estimate, since no analyst appears in two labs. (That’s why the data give the analysts lab-specific codes, PL-A1 and so on.)

The model for repeat k by analyst j in lab i is

y_{ijk} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_{j(i)} + e_{ijk},

where \beta_{j(i)} is the effect of analyst j within lab i. In R, lab / analyst means “lab, and analyst within lab”, and expands to lab + lab:analyst. The degrees of freedom are 3 for the labs, 4 \times (3 - 1) = 8 for the analysts within the labs, and 12 \times (3 - 1) = 24 for the repeats.

Your turn. Fit the nested ANOVA of the purity.

The nesting operator is /.

summary(aov(purity_pct ~ lab / analyst, data = interlab))

Three rows: the labs (3 degrees of freedom), the analysts within the labs (8) and the residuals, the repeats (24). The analysts’ F-test is right: F = 4.69, p = 0.0015, so the analysts within a lab do differ. But the labs’ F-test, F = 89.4, is wrong, as the next section explains.

The right error term

By default, aov() tests every term against the residuals, the variation between repeats by the same analyst. For the analysts, that’s the right comparison. For the labs, it isn’t.

A lab’s mean is the average of its three analysts’ results, so it carries those analysts’ quirks along with the lab’s own bias. Even if the four labs were identical, their means would differ because their analysts differ. So the labs must be compared with the variation between analysts within the labs:

F = \frac{\text{MS}_{\text{lab}}}{\text{MS}_{\text{analyst(lab)}}} \quad \text{on 3 and 8 degrees of freedom.}

The rule: test a factor against the mean square of the random factor nested directly below it. The analysts are random here: they stand for all the analysts who might ever run the test, not just these twelve.

Your turn. The setup code has stored the nested ANOVA table as tab. Compute the right F-test for the labs.

The analysts’ mean square is in the second row of the table.

F_lab <- tab[["Mean Sq"]][1] / tab[["Mean Sq"]][2]
c(F = F_lab, p = pf(F_lab, 3, 8, lower.tail = FALSE))

F = 19.1 on 3 and 8 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0005. The labs really do disagree, but the evidence is much weaker than the wrong test’s F = 89.4 on 3 and 24 suggested. With only 3 analysts per lab, there are only 8 degrees of freedom for the error.

aov() can do this itself, if you tell it the error structure: aov(purity_pct ~ lab + Error(analyst), data = interlab) tests the labs in the analyst stratum. Try it!

Variance components

The labs disagree. But the more useful question for a measurement method is: how much variation does each source add? Treat the labs as random too, a sample of all the labs that might measure the product. Then the model has three variance components: \sigma^2_L between labs, \sigma^2_A between analysts within a lab, and \sigma^2_e between repeats. A single result from a random lab and analyst has variance \sigma^2_L + \sigma^2_A + \sigma^2_e.

Each mean square estimates a combination of the components. With 3 analysts per lab and 3 repeats per analyst,

E(\text{MS}_e) = \sigma^2_e, \qquad E(\text{MS}_{A(L)}) = \sigma^2_e + 3\sigma^2_A, \qquad E(\text{MS}_L) = \sigma^2_e + 3\sigma^2_A + 9\sigma^2_L .

Solving gives the estimates: \hat\sigma^2_e = \text{MS}_e, \hat\sigma^2_A = (\text{MS}_{A(L)} - \text{MS}_e)/3 and \hat\sigma^2_L = (\text{MS}_L - \text{MS}_{A(L)})/9.

Your turn. Estimate the three variance components of the purity.

Each analyst made 3 repeats, so divide by 3.

ms <- tab[["Mean Sq"]]
c(lab = (ms[1] - ms[2]) / 9,
  analyst = (ms[2] - ms[3]) / 3,
  repeat_ = ms[3])

In standard deviations: 0.103 percentage points between labs, 0.037 between analysts, and 0.034 between repeats. The labs contribute 81% of the total variance: the analysts are fine, and the labs are the problem.

Measurement scientists summarize such a study with two standard deviations:

  • The repeatability standard deviation, s_r = \hat\sigma_e = 0.034: how much repeats by the same analyst, in the same lab, scatter.
  • The reproducibility standard deviation, s_R = \sqrt{\hat\sigma^2_L + \hat\sigma^2_A + \hat\sigma^2_e} = 0.115: how much results from different labs scatter.

Here, s_R is more than three times s_r. A purity result is very repeatable within a lab, but results from two labs can easily differ by 0.2 or 0.3 percentage points.

A first look at mixed models

The ANOVA formulas need a balanced design, and they can even give negative variance estimates when a mean square comes out smaller than the one below it. A mixed model estimates the variance components directly, by REML (restricted maximum likelihood), and copes with unbalanced data. The lme4 package fits them. In its formula, (1 | lab/analyst) means a random effect for each lab and for each analyst within a lab:

m_purity <- lme4::lmer(purity_pct ~ 1 + (1 | lab / analyst), data = interlab)
lme4::VarCorr(m_purity)
#>  Groups      Name        Std.Dev.
#>  analyst:lab (Intercept) 0.037382
#>  lab         (Intercept) 0.103457
#>  Residual                0.033718

The standard deviations are the same as the ANOVA estimates: for balanced data with positive estimates, the two methods agree. Module 5 comes back to models like this one as Bayesian hierarchical models.

Your turn. Fit the same mixed model for the water content, and look at its variance components.

The water’s column is water_ppm.

m_water <- lme4::lmer(water_ppm ~ 1 + (1 | lab / analyst), data = interlab)
lme4::VarCorr(m_water)

Standard deviations of 10.5 ppm between labs, 3.4 ppm between analysts, and 4.5 ppm between repeats. So s_r = 4.5 ppm and s_R = 11.9 ppm. Again, the labs dominate.

Nested MANOVA

To test the labs and the analysts on both properties at once, fit a nested MANOVA. The same rule about error terms holds, with SSCP matrices in place of mean squares. manova() tests the analysts within the labs against the repeats, which is right. Its test of the labs uses the same error, which is wrong again: the labs must be tested against the analysts’ SSCP matrix, H_{A(L)}.

With a balanced design, there’s an easy way to get that test. Average each analyst’s three repeats, and run a one-way MANOVA of the 12 analyst means on the labs. The variation of the analyst means within a lab is exactly the analyst-level variation, so the test has the right error, with 8 degrees of freedom.

Your turn. Fit the nested MANOVA of purity and water.

Nest the analysts in the labs: lab / analyst.

fit <- manova(cbind(purity_pct, water_ppm) ~ lab / analyst, data = interlab)
summary(fit)

The analysts within the labs differ: Pillai’s trace 1.11, F = 3.74 on 16 and 48 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0002. Ignore the lab line, which uses the wrong error.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · The labs, tested right. The setup code has stored the 12 analysts’ means as analyst_means, with columns lab, analyst, purity and water. Test the labs on both properties with a one-way MANOVA of the analyst means.

Use analyst_means, not interlab.

summary(manova(cbind(purity, water) ~ lab, data = analyst_means))

Pillai’s trace 1.74, F = 17.7 on 6 and 16 degrees of freedom, p = 3 \times 10^{-6}. The labs disagree, on the pair of properties, even when tested against the analyst-to-analyst variation. You’d get the same Wilks’ lambda, 0.016, from \det(H_{A(L)})/\det(H_{A(L)} + H_L) with the SSCP matrices of the nested MANOVA.

Challenge 2 · Settling the dispute. Does the customer’s lab find more water than the plant’s lab, by more than the analysts’ variation explains? Fit the water of the analyst means on the lab. The plant lab is the first level, so each coefficient compares a lab with the plant lab.

The analyst means’ water column is water.

water_fit <- lm(water ~ lab, data = analyst_means)
summary(water_fit)$coefficients

The customer lab reads 11.8 ppm more water than the plant lab (standard error 3.5, p = 0.01), and contract lab 2 reads 24.6 ppm more (p = 0.0001). So the dispute is about measurement, not the product. Every lab measured the same sample.

The study can’t say which lab is right, though. For that, the labs would measure a certified reference material whose water content is known. Then each lab’s bias could be estimated, and corrected.

Quick quiz

1. Why are the analysts nested in the labs, and not crossed with them?

Crossed factors appear in every combination. Here, “analyst 1” means a different person in each lab.

2. What should the labs’ F-test use as its denominator?

A lab’s mean carries its analysts’ differences, so the labs are compared with the analyst-to-analyst variation.

3. With 3 analysts per lab and 3 repeats per analyst, what does \text{MS}_{A(L)} estimate?

Each analyst’s mean averages 3 repeats, so it varies by \sigma^2_A + \sigma^2_e/3, and the mean square multiplies that by 3.

4. The repeatability standard deviation is 4.5 ppm and the reproducibility standard deviation 11.9 ppm. What does that tell you?

Most of the reproducibility variance comes from the labs. (A badly mixed sample would show up as large repeat-to-repeat scatter.)

5. What does (1 | lab / analyst) mean in an lme4 formula?

It expands to (1 | lab) + (1 | lab:analyst).

6. Why does a one-way MANOVA of the 12 analyst means test the labs correctly?

With a balanced design, it gives exactly the test of H_L against H_{A(L)}, with 8 error degrees of freedom.

Wrap-up

You can now analyze nested designs! Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Nested factors appear within only one level of another factor, like analysts within labs. In R, lab / analyst expands to lab + lab:analyst, with no interaction.
  • The right error term: test a factor against the mean square of the random factor nested directly below it. Error() terms in aov() do that automatically.
  • Variance components come from the expected mean squares, or directly from a mixed model, lme4::lmer() with (1 | lab / analyst).
  • Repeatability and reproducibility summarize a measurement method: here, the labs contribute most of the variance in both properties.
  • Nested MANOVA uses the same rules with SSCP matrices. With a balanced design, a MANOVA of the analyst means tests the labs correctly.
  • The dispute: the customer’s lab reads 11.8 ppm more water than the plant’s lab. The labs measure differently, and a certified reference material would show which one is right.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Nested factor
A factor whose levels appear within only one level of another factor, such as analysts within labs.
Crossed factors
Factors whose levels appear in every combination, such as catalysts and temperatures.
Random factor
A factor whose levels are a sample from a larger population of levels, such as analysts. Its effects are described by a variance.
Variance components
The variances contributed by each random factor and by the residual error.
Repeatability and reproducibility
The scatter of repeated results within one lab (s_r), and of results from different labs (s_R).
Mixed model
A model with both fixed and random effects, fitted by REML in lme4::lmer() or nlme::lme().
