Text, dates and categories

Cleaning strings with stringr, times with lubridate, and categories with forcats

Clean and match text with stringr and regular expressions, parse and calculate with dates, times and time zones using lubridate, and control categorical variables with factors and forcats.

Module 1 · Lesson 10 of 14 Week 25 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Meet the lab’s spreadsheet, as it looked before anybody cleaned it: lab_results_raw.csv. Different people typed it over three weeks, and it shows. Batch 1 appears as B01, B1 and " b01 ". Purity is called Purity, purity % and PURITY. And the times are written in three different styles. None of this is unusual: text typed by humans is always messy. This lesson gives you the three packages that tidy it up: stringr for text, lubridate for dates and times, and forcats for categories.

Cleaning text is like tidying a lab bench: a little satisfying, a little tedious, and it makes everything afterwards so much easier. Let’s get scrubbing!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • tidy text with stringr: trimming, changing case, padding and combining
  • find and fix patterns with regular expressions
  • parse dates and times, even when they’re written in several styles
  • calculate with times, and understand time zones
  • create, reorder and combine categories with forcats

Strings: the basics

Here’s the raw file. Reading every column as text, with trim_ws = FALSE, shows exactly what was typed, stray spaces and all. (By default, readr trims spaces from the edges of each value. Base R’s read.csv() doesn’t.)

raw <- read_csv("data/lab_results_raw.csv",
                col_types = cols(.default = col_character()), trim_ws = FALSE)
n_distinct(raw$batch)
#> [1] 82
head(unique(raw$batch), 15)
#>  [1] "B02"   "B2"    " b01 " "B1"    "B01"   "B03"   "b03"   "B04"   "b06"  
#> [10] "B06"   "b05"   "B05"   "B-08"  " B08 " "B08"

There are 40 batches, spelled in 82 different ways! The stringr functions all start with str_, and they all take the text as their first argument, so they work nicely in pipes:

library(stringr)
x <- c(" b01 ", "B-07", "B 12", "B5", "b33")
str_trim(x)                   # remove spaces at the start and end
#> [1] "b01"  "B-07" "B 12" "B5"   "b33"
str_to_upper(x)               # also str_to_lower() and str_to_title()
#> [1] " B01 " "B-07"  "B 12"  "B5"    "B33"
str_length(x)                 # the number of characters
#> [1] 5 4 4 2 3
str_sub("L26-006", 1, 3)      # characters 1 to 3
#> [1] "L26"
str_pad("5", 2, pad = "0")    # pad to 2 characters, on the left
#> [1] "05"
str_c("B", "07")              # join strings together
#> [1] "B07"

A few more that you’ll use all the time: str_squish() removes spaces at the edges and squeezes repeated spaces inside, and str_glue() fills values into a template:

str_squish("  too   many    spaces  ")
#> [1] "too many spaces"
str_glue("Batch {c('B01', 'B02')} ran on reactor R{1:2}")
#> Batch B01 ran on reactor R1
#> Batch B02 ran on reactor R2

Your turn. Trim the spaces off the batch codes and make them all upper case. How many different spellings are left? (raw is ready in every exercise.)

str_trim() removes the spaces at the start and end of each value.

n_distinct(str_to_upper(str_trim(raw$batch)))

66, down from 82. That’s progress, but B-08, B 07 and B9 are still spelled their own way. You need a cleverer tool.

Regular expressions

A regular expression (regex for short) is a little language for describing patterns in text. Instead of saying “the text B07”, a regex can say “a B, then some digits”. Here are the pieces you’ll use most:

Regex Matches
B, 07 those exact characters
. any one character
\d a digit, 0 to 9 (and \D is anything but a digit)
[A-Z], [a-z] one character from a range
[- ] one character from a set: here, a dash or a space
+, *, ? the thing before it: one or more times, zero or more times, or optionally
{2} the thing before it, exactly twice
^, $ the start and the end of the text
a|b either a or b

In an R string, a backslash has to be written twice, so the regex \d is typed "\\d". (A real dot is "\\.", as in Lesson 1’s file names.) These stringr functions all take a pattern:

str_detect(x, "\\d")            # does it contain a digit?
#> [1] TRUE TRUE TRUE TRUE TRUE
str_extract(x, "\\d+")          # the first run of digits
#> [1] "01" "07" "12" "5"  "33"
str_remove_all(x, "[- ]")       # delete every dash and space
#> [1] "b01" "B07" "B12" "B5"  "b33"
str_replace(x, "^b", "B")       # replace a lower-case b at the start
#> [1] " b01 " "B-07"  "B 12"  "B5"    "B33"
str_detect(c("B07", "B7", "B-07"), "^B\\d{2}$")   # exactly B and two digits?
#> [1]  TRUE FALSE FALSE

The last line describes a clean batch code completely: ^ the start, B, \d{2} exactly two digits, $ the end. str_view() is great for testing a pattern, because it marks every match with <>:

str_view(c("Purity", "purity %", "PURITY"), regex("pur", ignore_case = TRUE))
#> [1] │ <Pur>ity
#> [2] │ <pur>ity %
#> [3] │ <PUR>ITY

regex(..., ignore_case = TRUE) makes the match ignore upper and lower case.

Your turn. Every spelling of a batch code contains the batch number. Extract the digits with a regex, pad them to two characters, and put a B in front. How many distinct batch codes do you get?

“One or more digits” is "\\d+".

raw |>
  mutate(batch_id = str_c("B", str_pad(str_extract(batch, "\\d+"), 2, pad = "0"))) |>
  count(batch_id)

Exactly 40 codes, from B01 to B40. Padding matters for sorting, too: as text, "B10" comes before "B9", but "B09" comes before "B10".

Matching text

The test names need the same treatment. They come in nine spellings for three analytes:

count(raw, test, sort = TRUE)
#> # A tibble: 9 × 2
#>   test            n
#>   <chr>       <int>
#> 1 Purity         25
#> 2 APHA           16
#> 3 H2O ppm        15
#> 4 water          15
#> 5 color          13
#> 6 purity %       13
#> 7 Colour APHA    12
#> 8 PURITY          9

str_detect() inside case_when() sorts them out: each line tests for a pattern, and the first one that matches wins. In a regex, colou?r matches both the American “color” and the British “colour”, because u? means “an optional u”.

Your turn. Complete the patterns for water and color. The water tests are called “Water (KF)”, “water” and “H2O ppm”, and the color tests “Colour APHA”, “color” and “APHA”.

Use | for “or”: "water|h2o" matches either word.

For color: "colou?r|apha".

raw |>
  mutate(analyte = case_when(
    str_detect(test, regex("pur", ignore_case = TRUE)) ~ "purity",
    str_detect(test, regex("water|h2o", ignore_case = TRUE)) ~ "water",
    str_detect(test, regex("colou?r|apha", ignore_case = TRUE)) ~ "color"
  )) |>
  count(analyte)

No NA row, so every one of the 127 rows found its analyte. Always check for a leftover NA group after a case_when() like this one.

Dates and times

R has two main types for time. A Date is a day on the calendar, and a date-time (POSIXct) is an exact moment, down to the second. lubridate’s parsing functions are named after the order of the parts:

library(lubridate)
#> 
#> Attaching package: 'lubridate'
#> The following objects are masked from 'package:base':
#> 
#>     date, intersect, setdiff, union
ymd("2026-03-02")
#> [1] "2026-03-02"
dmy("02.03.2026")
#> [1] "2026-03-02"
mdy("March 2, 2026")
#> [1] "2026-03-02"
ymd_hms("2026-03-10T07:30:00Z")
#> [1] "2026-03-10 07:30:00 UTC"

All four understand many separators, so dmy() reads 02.03.2026, 02/03/2026 and 2 March 2026 alike. Once you have a date-time, you can take it apart:

t <- ymd_hms("2026-03-02 14:59:00")
year(t)
#> [1] 2026
month(t, label = TRUE)
#> [1] Mar
#> 12 Levels: Jan < Feb < Mar < Apr < May < Jun < Jul < Aug < Sep < ... < Dec
wday(t, label = TRUE)
#> [1] Mon
#> Levels: Sun < Mon < Tue < Wed < Thu < Fri < Sat
hour(t)
#> [1] 14
floor_date(t, "hour")
#> [1] "2026-03-02 14:00:00 UTC"

But what’s 02/03/2026? In Europe, it’s the 2nd of March. In the US, it’s February 3rd! A computer can’t know which, so you have to:

dmy("02/03/2026")
#> [1] "2026-03-02"
mdy("02/03/2026")
#> [1] "2026-02-03"

The lab’s campaign ran in March, so its dates are day first. The raw file mixes three styles: 2026-03-02 14:55, 02/03/2026 14:55 and Mar 03 2026 02:41 PM. parse_date_time() accepts a list of possible orders, and tries each one:

parse_date_time(c("2026-03-02 14:55", "02/03/2026 14:55", "Mar 03 2026 02:41 PM"),
                orders = c("Ymd HM", "dmY HM", "bdY IMp"), tz = "UTC")
#> [1] "2026-03-02 14:55:00 UTC" "2026-03-02 14:55:00 UTC"
#> [3] "2026-03-03 14:41:00 UTC"

In the orders, Y is a four-digit year, m a month number, b a month name, d a day, H an hour from 0 to 23, I an hour from 1 to 12, M the minutes and p AM or PM.

Your turn. Parse all of the measured times in the raw file, and find the first and last measurement.

The other two styles are day-month-year with a 24-hour time, and month name, day, year with a 12-hour time and AM or PM.

orders = c("Ymd HM", "dmY HM", "bdY IMp")

measured_at <- parse_date_time(raw$measured, orders = c("Ymd HM", "dmY HM", "bdY IMp"), tz = "UTC")
range(measured_at)

The first test was at 14:55 on 2 March, and the last at 11:48 on 22 March. If even one time had failed to parse, range() would have returned NA, and lubridate would have warned you.

Time spans and time zones

Subtracting two date-times gives a difftime, a time span with units. difftime() lets you choose the units, and as.numeric() turns the result into a plain number:

batches <- read_csv("data/batches.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
batches$end_time[1] - batches$start_time[1]
#> Time difference of 1.516667 hours
difftime(batches$end_time[1], batches$start_time[1], units = "mins")
#> Time difference of 91 mins

lubridate can also add time. It has two kinds of spans: a duration is an exact number of seconds (ddays(1) is always 86,400 seconds), while a period follows the calendar (days(1) means “the same time tomorrow”). Usually they agree. They disagree when the clocks change, and that’s where time zones come in.

Every date-time has a time zone. The plant’s batch system records UTC, Coordinated Universal Time, and with_tz() shows the same moment on another clock. Imagine that the plant is in Ohio, on US Eastern time. On the night of 8 March 2026, the clocks there jumped from 2:00 straight to 3:00, for daylight saving time. Here’s a batch that ran across that night:

start <- ymd_hm("2026-03-08 06:30", tz = "UTC")
end <- ymd_hm("2026-03-08 08:30", tz = "UTC")
end - start
#> Time difference of 2 hours
with_tz(c(start, end), "America/New_York")
#> [1] "2026-03-08 01:30:00 EST" "2026-03-08 04:30:00 EDT"

The batch ran for 2 hours, but a wall clock in Ohio went from 1:30 to 4:30, which looks like 3 hours. If the system had stored local times, as plain text without a time zone, every batch that crossed that night would seem an hour too long. In November, when the clocks fall back, durations would come out an hour too short, and some times would even happen twice. UTC never jumps. That’s why the plant, like almost every database, stores times in UTC and converts them to local time only for display, as Lesson 6 promised.

with_tz() changes the clock that a moment is shown on. force_tz() keeps the clock time and changes the zone, so it makes a different moment. Use it only to fix times that were recorded with the wrong zone.

Your turn. Compute each batch’s duration in minutes, and find the longest batch of the campaign.

The units for minutes are written "mins".

batches |>
  mutate(duration_min = as.numeric(difftime(end_time, start_time, units = "mins"))) |>
  slice_max(duration_min, n = 1) |>
  select(batch_id, start_time, end_time, duration_min)

B29 ran for 113 minutes. It also had the longest residence time of the campaign, 71.7 minutes.

Factors with forcats

A factor is R’s type for categorical data: a variable with a fixed set of possible values, called levels, in a chosen order. The levels exist even when no row uses them, which is just what you want in a table:

analyte <- factor(c("water", "purity", "water"), levels = c("purity", "water", "color"))
table(analyte)
#> analyte
#> purity  water  color 
#>      1      2      0

Unless you say otherwise, the levels are sorted alphabetically, and that order decides the order of tables, legends and the bars in a plot. The forcats functions (all starting with fct_) change the levels without touching the data:

Function Does
fct_relevel(f, "C") moves level C to the front
fct_infreq(f) orders the levels from the most common to the least
fct_reorder(f, x) orders the levels by the median of x in each group
fct_recode(f, new = "old") renames levels
fct_collapse(f, AB = c("A", "B")) merges levels into one
fct_lump_n(f, n) keeps the n most common levels, and lumps the rest into “Other” 
library(forcats)
table(fct_infreq(batches$catalyst))
#> 
#>  B  A  C  D 
#> 12 10  9  9
levels(fct_reorder(batches$catalyst, batches$yield_pct))
#> [1] "B" "A" "D" "C"

Ordering bars by their value, rather than alphabetically, makes plots far easier to read. That’s fct_reorder()’s favorite job.

Some categories have a natural order, like the workload scale in Lesson 9’s survey. An ordered factor knows that “low” comes before “high”, so comparisons work:

workload <- factor(c("low", "high", "about right"),
                   levels = c("very low", "low", "about right", "high", "very high"),
                   ordered = TRUE)
workload > "low"
#> [1] FALSE  TRUE  TRUE

Your turn. Order the catalysts by their median yield, from lowest to highest.

Reorder by the yields: batches$yield_pct. fct_reorder() uses the median unless you choose another function.

levels(fct_reorder(batches$catalyst, batches$yield_pct))

B, A, D and then C. But try .fun = mean: then C comes first! Catalyst C has the highest median but the lowest mean, because a few very poor batches pull its mean down. Which summary you choose matters.

n = 3 keeps the three most common levels.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · The night the clocks changed. An operator’s handwritten log says that a batch started at 01:30 and ended at 04:30, local time in New York, on 8 March 2026. How long did the batch really run?

Parse both times with ymd_hm(), and give the time zone with tz = "America/New_York". Without it, R assumes UTC, which has no daylight saving time.

start <- ymd_hm("2026-03-08 01:30", tz = "America/New_York")

start <- ymd_hm("2026-03-08 01:30", tz = "America/New_York")
end <- ymd_hm("2026-03-08 04:30", tz = "America/New_York")
end - start

2 hours, not 3: the hour from 2:00 to 3:00 never happened that night.

Challenge 2 · How long did the lab wait? For each batch, find the time of its first lab test in the raw file, and how many minutes after the end of the batch that was. What’s the median wait? You’ll need everything from this lesson: clean batch codes, parsed times, and a time span.

Reuse your answers: the batch code from the regex exercise, and the times from parse_date_time(). The wait is a difftime() from end_time to first_test, in minutes.

batch_id = str_c("B", str_pad(str_extract(batch, "\\d+"), 2, pad = "0")),
measured_at = parse_date_time(measured, orders = c("Ymd HM", "dmY HM", "bdY IMp"), tz = "UTC")
...
wait_min = as.numeric(difftime(first_test, end_time, units = "mins"))
raw |>
  mutate(batch_id = str_c("B", str_pad(str_extract(batch, "\\d+"), 2, pad = "0")),
         measured_at = parse_date_time(measured, orders = c("Ymd HM", "dmY HM", "bdY IMp"), tz = "UTC")) |>
  group_by(batch_id) |>
  summarise(first_test = min(measured_at)) |>
  left_join(batches, by = "batch_id") |>
  mutate(wait_min = as.numeric(difftime(first_test, end_time, units = "mins"))) |>
  summarise(median_wait = median(wait_min))

A median of 232 minutes: almost four hours between the end of a batch and its first test. Every wait is positive, too, so no sample was tested before its batch had finished. That’s a useful check that the times are right!

Quick quiz

1. Which of these does the regex "^B\\d{2}$" match?

It needs a B at the start, then exactly two digits, and then the end of the text. "B7" has only one digit, and "B-07" has a dash.

2. Which function removes the spaces at the start and end of " B08 "?

str_trim() removes the spaces at the edges. str_squish() also squeezes repeated spaces in the middle.

3. A European lab writes 02/03/2026 for the 2nd of March. Which function reads it correctly?

Day, month, year: dmy(). mdy() would read it as February 3rd.

4. Why does the plant’s database store times in UTC?

Local clocks skip an hour in spring and repeat one in autumn. UTC does neither. Convert to local time only for display, with with_tz().

5. In what order are the levels of factor(c("low", "high", "medium"))?

Without levels = ..., R sorts the levels alphabetically. Give the levels yourself whenever the order matters.

6. What does fct_reorder(catalyst, yield_pct) do?

Only the order of the levels changes, not the data. It’s perfect for ordering the bars of a plot.

Wrap-up

Messy text holds no fear for you now! Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • stringr basics: str_trim(), str_squish(), str_to_upper(), str_pad(), str_sub(), str_c() and str_glue().
  • Regular expressions: \d, [...], +, ?, {2}, ^, $ and |, typed with double backslashes in R, and used by str_detect(), str_extract(), str_replace() and str_remove(). Test them with str_view().
  • Dates and times: ymd(), dmy(), mdy() and friends, parse_date_time() for mixed styles, and year(), month(), wday() and hour() to take them apart.
  • Spans and zones: difftime() with units, durations versus periods, with_tz() to display a moment on another clock, and UTC for storage.
  • Factors: levels decide the order. fct_relevel(), fct_infreq(), fct_reorder(), fct_recode(), fct_collapse() and fct_lump_n() change them, and ordered factors know which level is bigger.

NoteKey words from this lesson
String
A piece of text. R calls its type character.
Regular expression (regex)
A pattern that describes text, like "^B\\d{2}$" for “a B and two digits, and nothing else”.
Date and date-time
A day on the calendar, and an exact moment in time. In R, Date and POSIXct.
Time zone
The rules that turn a moment into a clock time in some place, including daylight saving time.
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time, the world’s reference clock, which never changes for daylight saving time.
Duration and period
An exact number of seconds, and a span on the calendar, like “one day later”.
Factor and levels
R’s type for categories, and its set of allowed values, in order.
Ordered factor
A factor whose levels have a natural order, so that comparisons like > make sense.
