Areal data and spatial weights

Sixty districts and a worried town

A local newspaper’s map shows respiratory admissions highest near the plant. Compute standardized admission ratios for the county’s 60 districts, decide which districts are neighbours (queen and rook contiguity), build spatial weights, and test for spatial autocorrelation with Moran’s I and a permutation test. Then meet the small-numbers problem, smooth the ratios with empirical Bayes, and see how much a choropleth map depends on its class breaks.

Module 9 · Lesson 6 of 13 Week 138 of 160 About 5–7 hours

In March, the local newspaper printed a map of respiratory hospital admissions by district, from public health data. The darkest districts were around the plant, and the headline asked whether the plant was making people ill. Hana has been asked to look at the numbers properly, with the county’s public health officer. This lesson and the next are that analysis.

The data are areal: one number per district, for a partition of the county into 60 districts. There’s nothing to interpolate between districts; the questions are whether the pattern is real, and what explains it.

A choropleth map is the most persuasive and most misleading chart in statistics. Today you’ll learn both why it persuades and how it misleads.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • compute standardized admission ratios, and see why raw counts and rates aren’t enough
  • define neighbours by shared borders (queen and rook contiguity) and build spatial weights
  • test the ratios for spatial autocorrelation with Moran’s I, analytically and by permutation
  • see the small-numbers problem, and smooth the ratios with empirical Bayes
  • see how the class breaks of a choropleth map change its message

Standardized ratios

district_health.csv has, for each district, its population, the share aged 65 or more, a deprivation index (0 is the national average, in standard deviations), the density of main roads, the distance from the plant, and the number of respiratory hospital admissions over five years, 2021 to 2025. Raw counts mostly measure population, and older people are admitted more often, so the public health office has also computed the number expected if the district had the national admission rates for each age group. The standardized admission ratio (SAR; for deaths, the standardized mortality ratio, SMR) is

\text{SMR}_i = \frac{O_i}{E_i} \quad \text{(observed over expected)}.

1 means “as expected for its age structure”, 1.5 means 50% more admissions than expected.

The setup code reads the districts, transforms them to UTM, attaches the health data, and loads spdep.

Your turn. Compute each district’s ratio, the county’s overall ratio (\sum O / \sum E), and list the five highest.

Divide by expected.

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
print(sum(dis$admissions) / sum(dis$expected))
st_drop_geometry(dis[order(-dis$smr), c("name", "expected", "admissions", "smr", "dist_plant_km")])[1:5, ]

The county as a whole has 5,023 admissions against 4,892 expected, a ratio of 1.03, about the national average. The districts range from 0.55 to 1.80. The highest, Ashby (1.80) and North Park (1.76), are 8.4 and 5.6 km from the plant, with only 44.5 and 40.9 admissions expected: small districts, whose ratios are noisy. Riverside, St Anne’s and Market Hill, in the town, follow.

Neighbours by contiguity

For areas, the natural neighbours are those that share a border: contiguity. Two versions, named after chess pieces: queen contiguity counts any shared boundary point, even a single corner; rook contiguity needs a shared edge of positive length. spdep::poly2nb() finds them from the polygons.

Your turn. Find the queen neighbours, and summarize how many each district has.

queen = TRUE (the default).

nb <- poly2nb(dis, queen = TRUE)
print(nb)
c(links = sum(card(nb)), mean = mean(card(nb)), min = min(card(nb)), max = max(card(nb)))

310 links: on average, a district has 5.2 neighbours, from 2 (three districts on the county’s edge) to 10 (Canal Side, the plant’s own district, a large district that touches many town districts). Contiguity neighbours are symmetric by construction: if i borders j, j borders i.

Your turn. Now rook contiguity. How many links differ?

Rook is queen = FALSE.

nb_q <- poly2nb(dis, queen = TRUE)
nb_r <- poly2nb(dis, queen = FALSE)
c(queen = sum(card(nb_q)), rook = sum(card(nb_r)))

None: 310 both ways. These districts are irregular polygons (they were drawn as Voronoi cells), and in such a tiling, borders meet three at a time, so two districts never touch at a corner only. On a regular grid of squares, like a raster, every cell has 4 rook neighbours and 8 queen neighbours, and the choice matters a lot. Real administrative boundaries are in between.

Spatial weights and Moran’s I

Your turn. Build row-standardized weights, compute the spatial lag of the ratios (the average of each district’s neighbours), and correlate it with the ratios.

The lag of smr.

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
lw <- nb2listw(poly2nb(dis), style = "W")
lag <- lag.listw(lw, dis$smr)
cor(dis$smr, lag)

0.73: districts with high ratios tend to have neighbours with high ratios. Something that varies smoothly across the county is raising or lowering admissions.

Your turn. Compute Moran’s I with row-standardized ("W") and binary ("B", every link weighs 1) weights.

Binary weights are style "B".

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
nb <- poly2nb(dis)
c(W = unname(moran.test(dis$smr, nb2listw(nb, style = "W"))$estimate[1]),
  B = unname(moran.test(dis$smr, nb2listw(nb, style = "B"))$estimate[1]))

0.51 with row-standardized weights and 0.49 with binary ones; both far above the expected -1/59 = -0.017 (the row-standardized test’s standard deviate is 6.6). Row standardization gives each district’s neighbours a total weight of 1, so a district with 10 neighbours counts each one less; binary weights let districts with many neighbours (Canal Side, the town) weigh more in the total. The choice is a modelling decision, and it should be reported.

Your turn. With only 60 districts, the normal approximation is shaky. Run a permutation test with 999 shuffles.

The function is moran.mc.

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
lw <- nb2listw(poly2nb(dis), style = "W")
set.seed(9061)
mc <- moran.mc(dis$smr, lw, nsim = 999)
c(I = unname(mc$statistic), p = mc$p.value)

p = 0.001: none of the 999 random rearrangements of the 60 ratios over the map gave an I as large as the real one. The clustering is real. What causes it is another question (Lesson 7).

The small-numbers problem

A ratio from a district expecting 30 admissions is much noisier than one from a district expecting 300. If admissions are Poisson, \text{SD}(O_i/E_i) \approx \sqrt{\text{RR}_i / E_i}: with no real differences at all, a district expecting 25 would have ratios scattered with an SD of 0.2, one expecting 250 with 0.06. So the extremes of a map of ratios are dominated by the smallest districts, which is why Ashby and North Park topped the list.

Your turn. Compare the spread of the ratios in districts expecting fewer than 40 admissions with those expecting 100 or more.

100 or more.

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
small <- dis$expected < 40
big <- dis$expected >= 100
c(n_small = sum(small), sd_small = sd(dis$smr[small]), n_big = sum(big), sd_big = sd(dis$smr[big]))

The 19 small districts’ ratios have an SD of 0.31, the 16 large ones’ 0.17: nearly twice the spread, for the same kind of places. Part of every small district’s ratio is Poisson noise, and a map can’t show the difference.

Empirical Bayes smoothing shrinks each district’s ratio toward the county’s mean, more for small districts. Suppose the true relative risks \theta_i vary around a mean b with variance a, and O_i \mid \theta_i \sim \text{Poisson}(E_i\theta_i). The best estimate of \theta_i is then a weighted average,

\hat\theta_i = w_i \, \frac{O_i}{E_i} + (1 - w_i)\, b, \qquad w_i = \frac{a}{a + b/E_i},

with a and b estimated from all the districts by the method of moments (Marshall, 1991). It’s Module 5’s partial pooling, done quickly.

Your turn. Smooth the ratios with EBest(), and compare the ranges before and after.

The counts and the expected counts: dis$expected.

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
eb <- EBest(dis$admissions, dis$expected)
print(attr(eb, "parameters"))
dis$eb <- eb$estmm
c(range(dis$smr), range(dis$eb))

The estimated mean is b = 1.03 and the variance of the true risks a = 0.058 (an SD of 0.24: the districts really do differ). The range shrinks from 0.55–1.80 to 0.63–1.58. Ashby, expecting 44.5, keeps a weight of 0.058/(0.058 + 1.03/44.5) = 0.72 on its own ratio, and moves from 1.80 to 1.58; a district expecting 300 would keep 94%. The ranking changes too: the smoothed map’s highest districts are those with high ratios and enough data to believe them.

Your turn. Is the clustering only noise? Assunção and Reis’s empirical Bayes Moran’s I standardizes each ratio by its own Poisson uncertainty before computing I. Run it.

999 permutations.

lw <- nb2listw(poly2nb(dis), style = "W")
set.seed(9062)
ebm <- EBImoran.mc(dis$admissions, dis$expected, lw, nsim = 999)
c(I = unname(ebm$statistic), p = ebm$p.value)

0.51, p = 0.001: the same answer. With these district sizes, the clustering isn’t an artefact of unequal noise. The pattern on the newspaper’s map is real; the next question is what explains it.

Choropleth maps and their class breaks

A choropleth map colours each area by its value in classes. The classes’ breaks decide what the eye sees. Quantile breaks put the same number of areas in each class (every class appears, whatever the values); equal-interval breaks split the range evenly (honest about the values, but one outlier can push everything else into a couple of classes). The classInt package computes both, and several others.

Your turn. Compute five quantile classes and five equal-interval classes of the raw ratios, and count the districts in each equal-interval class.

Equal intervals are style "equal".

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
q <- classIntervals(dis$smr, n = 5, style = "quantile")
e <- classIntervals(dis$smr, n = 5, style = "equal")
print(round(q$brks, 2)); print(round(e$brks, 2))
table(cut(dis$smr, e$brks, include.lowest = TRUE))

The quantile breaks are 0.55, 0.83, 0.96, 1.14, 1.37 and 1.80: the darkest class starts at 1.37, so 12 districts look alarming. With equal intervals (0.55, 0.80, 1.05, 1.30, 1.55, 1.80), only 6 districts are in the darkest class, and 21 are in the second-lightest. Same data, two different stories. A careful map states its breaks, uses ones with a meaning when it can (1 = as expected, 1.25, 1.5), and maps the smoothed estimates, or the probability that the risk exceeds 1, rather than raw ratios.

NoteGo deeper: the modifiable areal unit problem

Areal data come from aggregating individuals into areas whose boundaries someone chose. Draw the boundaries differently, at the same scale (the zoning effect) or at a different scale (the scale effect), and correlations, regression slopes and Moran’s I can all change, sometimes even sign. This is the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), named by Stan Openshaw in 1984. A related trap is the ecological fallacy: a correlation between district averages (deprivation and admissions) doesn’t say that the deprived individuals are the ones admitted. Neither problem has a general solution; the defences are to analyze at the finest scale available, to check whether conclusions survive a change of units, and to phrase conclusions about areas as conclusions about areas. Openshaw’s short book The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (Geo Books, 1984) is still worth reading.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Before blaming the plant. Correlate the ratios with the distance from the plant, the ratios with deprivation, and deprivation with the distance from the plant.

The distance column is dist_plant_km.

dis$smr <- dis$admissions / dis$expected
c(smr_distance = cor(dis$smr, dis$dist_plant_km),
  smr_deprivation = cor(dis$smr, dis$deprivation),
  deprivation_distance = cor(dis$deprivation, dis$dist_plant_km))

-0.62: the ratios fall with the distance from the plant, as the newspaper said. But they rise with deprivation even more strongly (+0.73), and deprivation itself falls with the distance from the plant (-0.68): the old industrial housing around the plant and in the town is the most deprived. The distance could be standing in for deprivation, or the other way round. Correlations can’t separate them; a regression with both can, and since the residuals will be spatially correlated, it has to be a spatial regression. That’s Lesson 7. (And the plant’s own district, Canal Side? A ratio of 0.95.)

It’s 0.54, close to the contiguity version’s 0.51. When would the two definitions disagree badly? (Think of a county with one huge rural district and many small town districts.)

Wrap-up

You’ve checked the newspaper’s map. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Standardized ratios (observed over expected) compare districts with different age structures; the county overall is at 1.03, the districts between 0.55 and 1.80.
  • Contiguity neighbours (queen or rook) and row-standardized or binary weights; here, 310 links and 5.2 neighbours per district.
  • The clustering is real: Moran’s I 0.51 (p = 0.001 by permutation, and the same with the empirical Bayes version for rates).
  • The small-numbers problem: small districts’ ratios are twice as variable. Empirical Bayes shrinks them (Ashby from 1.80 to 1.58).
  • Class breaks change a map’s message: 12 alarming districts with quantiles, 6 with equal intervals.
  • Before blaming the plant: distance and deprivation are entangled (r = -0.68).

NoteKey words from this lesson
Standardized ratio (SMR, SAR)
Observed over expected cases, where the expected count uses reference rates for each age group.
Contiguity (queen, rook)
Neighbours defined by a shared boundary: any shared point (queen) or a shared edge (rook).
Row-standardized and binary weights
Each area’s neighbour weights sum to 1, or every link has weight 1.
Small-numbers problem
The extra variability of rates from small populations, which dominates the extremes of a map.
Empirical Bayes smoothing
Shrinking each area’s rate toward an overall (or local) mean, by an amount that depends on its expected count.
Choropleth map
A map with areas coloured by classes of a value.
Modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP)
The dependence of results for areal data on how the areas were drawn.
Ecological fallacy
Concluding about individuals from relationships between area averages.
