Models for areal data

Is it the plant?

Answer the newspaper’s question. Fit Poisson regressions for the admissions with an offset for the expected counts, see the plant’s apparent effect disappear once deprivation and traffic are in, and deal with overdispersion. Test the residuals for spatial autocorrelation, use Lagrange multiplier tests to choose between spatial error and spatial lag models, fit both and a CAR model with spatialreg, and put an honest confidence interval on the plant’s effect.

Module 9 · Lesson 7 of 13 Week 139 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 6 showed that the newspaper’s pattern is real: admission ratios cluster, and they’re higher near the plant. It also showed that the districts near the plant are the most deprived. Deprivation is one of the strongest predictors of respiratory illness everywhere (through smoking, damp housing, occupations and access to care), and so is traffic. So the question for this lesson is precise: after accounting for deprivation and traffic, is there any association left with the distance from the plant?

Two complications make it a spatial-statistics question. The counts vary more than a Poisson model allows, and whatever the covariates miss is spatially correlated, so ordinary regression standard errors are too small, exactly as in Lesson 5.

This is the kind of analysis that ends up in a council meeting, so every number needs its uncertainty. Let’s be careful.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • fit Poisson regressions for counts, with the expected counts as an offset
  • see a confounder at work, and deal with overdispersion
  • test regression residuals for spatial autocorrelation
  • choose between spatial error and spatial lag models with Lagrange multiplier tests
  • fit spatial error, spatial lag and CAR models with spatialreg, and compare them
  • put a confidence interval on the plant’s effect, and say what it means

Poisson regression with an offset

For counts O_i with expected counts E_i, the natural model is

O_i \sim \text{Poisson}(E_i \,\theta_i), \qquad \log\theta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots,

so \log \mathrm{E}[O_i] = \log E_i + \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots: the log of the expected count enters with a coefficient fixed at 1, as an offset. Each e^{\beta_j} is a relative risk per unit of x_j.

The setup code reads and merges the data, and builds the row-standardized contiguity weights from Lesson 6.

Your turn. Fit the newspaper’s model: admissions on the distance from the plant only.

The offset is the log of the expected counts.

g1 <- glm(admissions ~ offset(log(expected)) + dist_plant_km, family = poisson, data = dis)
print(coef(summary(g1)))
exp(coef(g1)[["dist_plant_km"]])                               # relative risk per km

A relative risk of 0.967 per km: 3.3% fewer admissions for every kilometre from the plant, with z = -10. Over 10 km, that’s a factor of 0.967^{10} = 0.72. Taken at face value, a district next to the plant has 40% more admissions than one 10 km away. Don’t take it at face value yet.

Your turn. Add deprivation and traffic.

Traffic is traffic_km_per_km2.

g2 <- glm(admissions ~ offset(log(expected)) + deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km,
          family = poisson, data = dis)
print(coef(summary(g2)))
exp(coef(g2))

Deprivation: a relative risk of 1.21 per standard deviation. Traffic: 1.07 per km of main road per km². Distance from the plant: 0.998 per km, with p = 0.65. The plant’s apparent effect has vanished; it was deprivation and traffic, which are both higher near the plant (the old industrial housing, the motorway). That’s confounding, and the reason no study of a local health complaint should stop at the map.

Your turn. A Poisson model’s residual deviance should be about its degrees of freedom. Check, and refit as quasi-Poisson, which scales the standard errors by the estimated dispersion.

family = quasipoisson.

g2 <- glm(admissions ~ offset(log(expected)) + deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km,
          family = poisson, data = dis)
q2 <- glm(admissions ~ offset(log(expected)) + deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km,
          family = quasipoisson, data = dis)
c(deviance_per_df = deviance(g2) / df.residual(g2), dispersion = summary(q2)$dispersion,
  p_distance = coef(summary(q2))["dist_plant_km", 4])

A deviance of 2.2 per degree of freedom, and an estimated dispersion of 2.2: the counts vary more than Poisson, because districts differ in ways the covariates don’t capture. The quasi-Poisson standard errors are \sqrt{2.2} = 1.49 times larger, and the distance’s p-value rises to 0.76. Overdispersion is the rule, not the exception, for area counts.

Are the residuals spatially correlated?

The extra variation might be spatially structured: neighbouring districts sharing whatever the covariates miss (housing stock, a GP practice’s referral habits, a valley with poor air). If it is, the districts aren’t independent pieces of evidence, and even the quasi-Poisson standard errors are too small.

Your turn. Compute Moran’s I of the Poisson model’s Pearson residuals.

type = "pearson".

g2 <- glm(admissions ~ offset(log(expected)) + deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km,
          family = poisson, data = dis)
res <- residuals(g2, type = "pearson")
moran.test(res, lw)$estimate

I = 0.39, against an expected -0.017: strong positive autocorrelation in the residuals. The covariates explain the plant-distance pattern, but they leave a smooth spatial pattern behind. A model for that pattern is needed, both for honest standard errors and for better estimates of each district’s risk.

Spatial regression models

The spatialreg package fits the two classical spatial regression models, for a continuous response y (here the log standardized ratio, which is roughly normal with expected counts this size):

  • the spatial error model (SEM): y = X\boldsymbol\beta + \mathbf u, \mathbf u = \lambda W \mathbf u + \boldsymbol\varepsilon. The errors are spatially correlated: something unmeasured and smooth. The coefficients mean what they mean in an ordinary regression.
  • the spatial lag model (SLM, or SAR lag): y = \rho W y + X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon. The response itself spills over between neighbours (house prices, contagious behaviour). A covariate change in one district then affects its neighbours too, so the coefficients aren’t the full effects.

Both are simultaneous autoregressive (SAR) models. The conditional autoregressive (CAR) model specifies instead each district’s error given its neighbours’, \mathrm{E}[u_i \mid u_{-i}] = \lambda \sum_j w_{ij} u_j, and is the basis of most disease-mapping models.

First, the ordinary regression on the log ratio.

Your turn. Fit it, and test its residuals with lm.morantest().

lm.morantest.

m0 <- lm(lsmr ~ deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km, data = dis)
print(coef(summary(m0)))
lm.morantest(m0, lw)$estimate

The same story as the Poisson model (deprivation and traffic strong, distance not), and the residuals’ I is 0.32 (standard deviate 4.9, p < 10^{-6}). Note the expected value under no autocorrelation: -0.047, not -0.017, because regression residuals are correlated with each other through the fitted coefficients, which lm.morantest() accounts for.

Which spatial model? Lagrange multiplier (Rao score) tests, computed from the ordinary regression alone, test for a spatial error term and for a spatial lag. Their robust (adjusted) versions test each one allowing for the other. The usual rule (Anselin, 1996): if only one robust test is significant, choose that model.

Your turn. Run all five tests with lm.RStests().

test = "all".

m0 <- lm(lsmr ~ deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km, data = dis)
rs <- lm.RStests(m0, lw, test = "all")
sapply(rs, function(t) c(statistic = unname(t$statistic), p = t$p.value))

Both simple tests are significant (error: p = 0.0001; lag: p = 0.009), but of the robust versions, only the error test is (adjusted error: p = 0.004; adjusted lag: p = 0.53). The lag test was only significant because it picks up error autocorrelation. So: a spatial error model. That also makes sense: respiratory admissions don’t “spill over” between districts, but unmeasured risk factors vary smoothly.

Your turn. Fit the spatial error model with errorsarlm().

The function is errorsarlm.

sem <- errorsarlm(lsmr ~ deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km, data = dis, listw = lw)
print(summary(sem)$Coef)
c(lambda = unname(sem$lambda), lambda_se = unname(sem$lambda.se))

\lambda = 0.61 (standard error 0.12): strong spatial correlation in the errors. Deprivation’s coefficient is 0.20 (a 22% higher risk per standard deviation) and traffic’s 0.066, both still clear; the distance from the plant has a coefficient of +0.0024 per km, standard error 0.0089, p = 0.79. Compared with the ordinary regression, the standard errors are larger (distance: 0.0089 against 0.0066), as they should be. The residuals of this model have a Moran’s I of 0.006: the error model has absorbed the spatial pattern.

Your turn. Fit the spatial lag model with lagsarlm() too, and compare the three models’ AIC.

The lag model is lagsarlm.

f <- lsmr ~ deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km
m0 <- lm(f, data = dis)
sem <- errorsarlm(f, data = dis, listw = lw)
slm <- lagsarlm(f, data = dis, listw = lw)
c(ols = AIC(m0), error = AIC(sem), lag = AIC(slm), rho = unname(slm$rho))

AICs of -38.6 for the ordinary regression, -50.2 for the error model and -42.6 for the lag model (\rho = 0.34). The error model wins clearly, as the robust tests said. In the lag model, a coefficient isn’t the whole effect: a change in one district’s deprivation also raises its neighbours’ predicted ratios through \rho W y. impacts(slm, listw = lw) splits the effects into direct and indirect (spillover) parts; for deprivation, the total effect is 0.24, of which 0.08 is spillover.

Your turn. Fit a CAR model with spautolm(..., family = "CAR"). CAR models need symmetric weights, so use binary weights (style "B").

Binary weights: style "B".

lwB <- nb2listw(poly2nb(dis), style = "B")
car <- spautolm(lsmr ~ deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km, data = dis, listw = lwB, family = "CAR")
print(summary(car)$Coef)
c(lambda = unname(car$lambda), AIC = AIC(car))

\lambda = 0.166 with binary weights, close to the largest value the model allows, 1/5.88 = 0.170 (one over the largest eigenvalue of the weights matrix; \lambda’s scale depends on the weights): strong spatial correlation, as in the error model. An AIC of -48.6, close to the error model’s -50.2, and the same conclusions: deprivation and traffic matter, distance doesn’t. SAR and CAR are two ways to write down a smooth unmeasured pattern; they rarely disagree on the substance.

What can we say about the plant?

“Not significant” isn’t “no effect”. The useful answer is a confidence interval: how large an effect is still compatible with the data?

Your turn. From the spatial error model, compute a 95% confidence interval for the distance coefficient, and turn it into a relative risk for a district 5 km closer to the plant.

The standard error is the second element of est.

sem <- errorsarlm(lsmr ~ deprivation + traffic_km_per_km2 + dist_plant_km, data = dis, listw = lw)
est <- summary(sem)$Coef["dist_plant_km", ]
ci <- est[1] + c(-1.96, 1.96) * est[2]
c(ci_per_km = ci, rr_5km_closer = rev(exp(-5 * ci)))

The distance coefficient lies between -0.015 and +0.020 per km. For a district 5 km closer to the plant, the relative risk lies between 0.91 and 1.08. So the data are compatible with the plant raising admissions in nearby districts by up to about 8% (or lowering them by up to 10%), and not with the 18% that the newspaper’s model implied (0.967^{-5}). That’s what Hana can tell the council: the pattern on the map is explained by deprivation and traffic; any remaining effect of the plant on respiratory admissions, at the district level, is small, at most a few percent. It’s not proof that the plant is harmless (district data can’t see effects concentrated in a few streets, Lesson 6’s ecological fallacy), but it’s what these data can say.

NoteGo deeper: disease mapping with BYM models

The log ratio works here because every district expects at least 24 admissions. With small counts (a rare cancer, a small area), the log of O/E is unstable or undefined at zero, and the model should stay on the count scale: O_i \sim \text{Poisson}(E_i\theta_i) with \log\theta_i = \mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta + u_i + v_i, where u_i is a spatially structured CAR effect and v_i an unstructured one. That’s the Besag-York-Mollié (BYM) model (1991), the workhorse of disease mapping, fitted by MCMC (the CARBayes package, or Stan, as in Module 5) or by INLA. Its maps of \theta_i borrow strength both from the whole county (like Lesson 6’s empirical Bayes) and from each district’s neighbours. Riebler and others’ “BYM2” reparametrization (2016) makes its priors easier to set. Waller and Gotway’s Applied Spatial Statistics for Public Health Data (Wiley, 2004), chapter 9, explains it from the ground up.

Quick quiz

1. Why is \log E_i an offset rather than a covariate?

Then the other coefficients describe the relative risk, O/E.

2. The distance’s effect vanished when deprivation and traffic were added. That’s:

Deprivation and traffic are correlated with the distance and with admissions.

3. A deviance of 2.2 per degree of freedom in a Poisson model means:

Overdispersion; quasi-Poisson multiplies the standard errors by \sqrt{2.2}.

4. Only the robust error test was significant. Which model?

The simple lag test was significant only because it picks up error autocorrelation.

5. In a spatial lag model, why isn’t a coefficient the full effect of its covariate?

Direct plus indirect (spillover) impacts make the total.

6. What’s the best summary of the analysis for the council?

A confidence interval, with the limits of district-level data stated.

Wrap-up

You’ve answered the newspaper’s question, carefully. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Poisson regression with an offset models relative risks. On distance alone, admissions fell 3.3% per km; with deprivation (1.21 per SD) and traffic (1.07 per unit) in the model, the distance’s effect was 0.998 per km, p = 0.65.
  • Overdispersion (2.2) and residual autocorrelation (Moran’s I 0.39) both make ordinary standard errors too small.
  • Lagrange multiplier tests chose the spatial error model (\lambda = 0.61, AIC -50.2 against -38.6 for ordinary regression and -42.6 for the lag model); a CAR model agreed.
  • The honest answer is an interval: for a district 5 km closer to the plant, a relative risk between 0.91 and 1.08.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Offset
A term with a coefficient fixed at 1, such as the log of the expected count in a Poisson model for rates.
Relative risk
The ratio of the rate in one group to the rate in another; in a log-linear model, e^\beta per unit of the covariate.
Overdispersion
More variation in counts than the Poisson model allows.
Spatial error model (SEM)
A regression whose errors follow a simultaneous autoregression, \mathbf u = \lambda W\mathbf u + \boldsymbol\varepsilon.
Spatial lag model (SLM)
A regression with the neighbours’ average response as a predictor, y = \rho W y + X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon.
Conditional autoregressive (CAR) model
A model specifying each area’s value given its neighbours’; the basis of most disease-mapping models.
Lagrange multiplier (Rao score) tests
Tests for spatial error and spatial lag dependence computed from the ordinary regression; their robust versions separate the two.
Direct and indirect impacts
In a spatial lag model, the effect of a covariate change on the same area and the spillover effect on others.
