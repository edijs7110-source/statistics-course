Module 9 project: an environmental spatio-temporal analysis

Is the benzene plume going away?

The Module 9 project brief: eight years of quarterly benzene measurements in 40 groundwater wells around an old tank farm. Clean the data (a well in the wrong coordinate system, results below the detection limit, a round in the wrong units, a duplicate and a dry well), map today’s plume with its uncertainty and the probability of exceeding 10 µg/L, find out whether the plume is shrinking and moving, and judge whether benzene will reach the town’s drinking-water well.

In 1994, a buried pipe at the plant’s old tank farm leaked petrol-grade benzene into the ground. The free product was removed in 1996, but a dissolved plume remains in the shallow aquifer, and the groundwater flows east, toward the town’s drinking-water well, PW-1, 1.8 km away. Hana has persuaded the regulator to accept monitored natural attenuation (letting bacteria break the benzene down, and proving it with data) instead of pumping and treating the groundwater for decades. The regulator agreed, on three conditions, and wants a report on all three.

That’s an environmental spatio-temporal analysis: data at fixed places, measured again and again, with a map of today, a trend over time, a prediction, and an honest account of the uncertainty in each.

Module 9 project Week 146 of the study plan About 8–10 hours

Your last project of the spatial block! Everything from this module meets here: coordinate systems, variograms, kriging, exceedance probabilities and trends over time. Hana’s waiting, and so is the regulator.

What you’ll make

One report, written in Quarto in your plant-study RStudio project, that Hana could send to the regulator. It should contain:

  1. A summary for the regulator, at the top, in plain words: where benzene is above 10 µg/L today (with a map and its uncertainty), whether the plume is shrinking (how fast) and moving (how fast, which way), and whether the sentinel wells, between the plume and PW-1, are at risk.
  2. The data and its problems: what you found, and what you did about each.
  3. The map of today: a kriged map of the 2026 Q3 round, a map of its standard error, and a map of the probability of exceeding 10 µg/L, with the variogram and the cross-validation behind them.
  4. The trend: how the plume’s size, mass and position have changed since 2019.
  5. The sentinel wells: what the data say about MW-36 and MW-37, and what they can’t say yet.
  6. Limitations, and a recommendation.

Nobody grades it, but write it as if the regulator’s hydrogeologist will check every number. In real life, they would.

The handover

Download the three files into a new folder in your project, data-raw/groundwater/, and don’t edit them:

  • README.txt: Hana’s note, with the three conditions, the questions, the columns and a known issue. Read it first.
  • wells.csv: the 40 monitoring wells, with their coordinates and screen depths.
  • groundwater_benzene.csv: every result since February 2019, as the labs reported it.

Then download the project template, save it in the top folder of your project as groundwater-benzene.qmd, and click Render. It reads the data and summarizes it, with headings and code skeletons for every step. Fill it in one section at a time, and render often. It uses the tidyverse, sf and gstat.

The brief, step by step

  1. Explore (Lessons 1 and 10). Map the wells. How many results per well and per round? How do the values range, and what does a time series look like at the source and at the edge?
  2. Clean (Lesson 1, and Modules 1 and 4). Check every well’s coordinates against the others (is one in a different CRS?). Look for duplicated results, for values that can’t be numbers, and for a round whose values look a thousand times too small. Decide how to treat results below the detection limit: they’re information, not missing values (Module 4, Lesson 11).
  3. Map today (Lessons 3 to 5). Work on the log scale. Compute and fit a variogram for the 2026 Q3 round (consider a trend, or the distance from the tank farm), krige a grid, map the prediction and its standard error, cross-validate, and map the probability of exceeding 10 µg/L.
  4. The trend (Lessons 10 and 11, and Module 6). For each round, estimate the plume’s total “mass” (the sum of kriged concentrations over the grid, which is proportional to the mass if porosity and thickness are constant), its area above 10 µg/L, and its centre of mass. Is the mass falling, and at what half-life? Is the centre moving?
  5. The sentinels (Module 4, Lesson 11, and Module 6). MW-36 and MW-37 are mostly below the detection limit. What can you say about their trend? If it continues, when would they reach 1 µg/L?
  6. Write it up. Summary first. Then the data, the cleaning, today’s map, the trend, the sentinels, the limitations and a recommendation, and the cleaning log at the end.

Stretch goal. Fit a spatio-temporal model to all the rounds at once: for example, universal kriging with a trend in time and in the distance downstream of the tank farm, or a space-time variogram (Lesson 11) of the residuals. Does it give a sharper map of today than the 2026 Q3 round alone? Does it let you predict at the sentinel wells with an uncertainty?

Tips

  • Coordinates first. One well with coordinates near (8.76, 51.46) is in longitude and latitude. Convert it with st_transform() from EPSG:4326 to EPSG:32632; don’t drop it.
  • Censored values. Substituting half the detection limit is a common start (Module 4 showed when it’s good enough). With the log scale and kriging, a value at half the limit is a reasonable plug-in for a map; for the sentinel trends, where most values are censored, consider a censored regression (survival::survreg() with type = "left"), as in Module 4.
  • The unit error. Compare the median of each round and each lab. A round from one lab a thousand times lower than the rounds before and after isn’t a sudden clean-up.
  • The variogram of one round. With only 40 wells and a strong trend, the raw variogram may not level off. Try a trend in the distance from the tank farm, or in the distance downstream (x), and fit the residuals’ variogram.
  • Mass and centroid. Krige every round on the same grid with the same variogram model, then sum e^{\hat z} over the grid (the mass proxy) and weight the coordinates by it (the centroid). Regress the log of the mass on time for a half-life.
  • Don’t over-claim. The sentinel wells’ trend is estimated from a few small numbers near the detection limit. Say what it suggests, how uncertain it is, and what more data would settle it (more frequent sampling at MW-36 and MW-37, a lower detection limit).

When it’s done, you’ll have carried out a complete environmental spatio-temporal analysis, from a messy monitoring database to maps, trends and a recommendation with its uncertainty.
