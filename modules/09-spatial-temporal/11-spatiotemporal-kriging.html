Spatio-temporal covariance and kriging

Borrowing strength from neighbours in space and in time

Model how the air-quality network’s day-to-day anomalies are correlated across space and time together. Estimate spatial and temporal covariances by hand, check whether they’re separable, compute a spatio-temporal variogram with gstat, fit separable and metric models, and use them to fill in hidden measurements with space-time kriging. Then validate it honestly: it helps where a station’s spatial neighbours are far away, and fails where the plant’s plume breaks the model’s assumptions.

Module 9 · Lesson 11 of 13 Week 143 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 10 found that the network’s space-time interaction (what’s left after the station and day effects) is correlated over a few kilometres and a few days. Geostatistics in Lessons 3 to 5 used correlation in space to predict where nobody measured. With time, there’s a second source of information: a station’s own yesterday and tomorrow. Spatio-temporal kriging uses both, through a covariance function of the distance in space and the lag in time.

Tomás has a practical reason to care. Analyzers fail, and the EU’s air-quality rules require 90% data capture for a station’s annual mean to count; good gap-filling, with honest uncertainty, is part of running a network.

Today’s lesson ends with a method that works brilliantly at some stations and badly at others. Finding out which, and why, is the real skill.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • estimate spatial and temporal covariances of the anomalies by hand
  • check whether the covariance is separable (space times time)
  • compute a spatio-temporal variogram with gstat, and fit separable and metric models
  • predict hidden measurements by space-time kriging, and compare with simpler methods
  • validate the predictions by station type, and diagnose where the model fails

Covariance in space and time

For a field Z(s, t) with mean zero, the spatio-temporal covariance is C(h, u) = \mathrm{Cov}(Z(s, t), Z(s + h, t + u)): a function of the spatial distance h and the time lag u, if the field is stationary in both. It’s separable if it factors into a spatial and a temporal part,

C(h, u) = \frac{C(h, 0)\, C(0, u)}{C(0, 0)},

which means that the spatial pattern of correlation is the same at every time lag, only scaled down. Separable models are convenient (their covariance matrices are Kronecker products, cheap to invert), but physical processes like wind-driven transport usually aren’t separable.

The setup code computes the interaction from Lesson 10 (log NO₂ minus station and day effects), as a 365 × 24 matrix R, and the station coordinates in kilometres.

Your turn. Compute the covariance between every pair of stations at lag 0, and average it in distance bands.

Average by band.

C0 <- cov(R, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
pair <- lower.tri(D)
band <- cut(D[pair], c(0, 3, 6, 10, 15, 30))
c(variance = mean(diag(C0)), round(tapply(C0[pair], band, mean), 4))

The anomalies’ variance is 0.036 (a standard deviation of 0.19 on the log scale, roughly ±20%). Stations within 3 km of each other share a covariance of 0.013, about 37% of the variance; from 3 km on, the covariances are slightly negative (-0.005 to -0.006) and close to zero beyond 15 km. The negative values are a side effect of removing the day effect: the anomalies of all stations average zero every day, so a positive cluster of stations must be balanced by negative ones elsewhere.

Your turn. Now in time: the covariance of each station with itself one day later, averaged over the stations, and its ratio to the variance.

Tomorrow drops the first day: R[-1, ].

today <- R[-365, ]; tomorrow <- R[-1, ]
C1 <- cov(tomorrow, today, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")      # C1[i, j]: station i tomorrow, station j today
C0 <- cov(R, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
c(lag0 = mean(diag(C0)), lag1 = mean(diag(C1)), ratio = mean(diag(C1)) / mean(diag(C0)))

A lag-1 covariance of 0.012, 34% of the variance: a station’s anomaly today has a correlation of about 0.34 with its anomaly yesterday (Lesson 10’s average autocorrelation, 0.38, weighed every station equally).

Your turn. Is the covariance separable? Compare the observed lag-1 covariance between different stations, by distance band, with the separable prediction C(h, 0) \times C(0, 1) / C(0, 0).

Multiply by the temporal ratio, ratio.

C0 <- cov(R, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
C1 <- cov(R[-1, ], R[-365, ], use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
C1s <- (C1 + t(C1)) / 2                                          # average the two directions
pair <- lower.tri(D); band <- cut(D[pair], c(0, 3, 6, 10, 15, 30))
ratio <- mean(diag(C1)) / mean(diag(C0))
round(rbind(lag0 = tapply(C0[pair], band, mean), lag1 = tapply(C1s[pair], band, mean),
            separable = tapply(C0[pair], band, mean) * ratio), 4)

Within 3 km, the observed lag-1 covariance between different stations is 0.0039, and the separable prediction 0.0046, a little higher; beyond 3 km both are near zero. They’re close enough that a separable model is a reasonable first approximation. A clear mismatch, such as a lag-1 covariance between two stations that’s larger than their lag-0 covariance (which a plume moving from one to the other would produce), would rule it out.

The spatio-temporal variogram

gstat works with the spacetime package’s STFDF class (Lesson 10’s “full grid”): a set of locations, a set of times, and a data frame with one row per location-time, space varying fastest (all stations for day 1, then all for day 2, …). variogramST() then computes \gamma(h, u) for distance bins h and time lags u. To keep it quick, the lesson uses the first quarter of 2025.

Your turn. Build the STFDF for January to March, and check its dimensions.

Use the logical vector q1 to keep the rows for January to March, and leave the column index empty to keep all the stations: R[q1, ]. t() makes the stations vary fastest.

q1 <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-04-01")
days <- as.Date(rownames(R))[q1]
stf <- STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), days, data.frame(r = as.vector(t(R[q1, ]))))
dim(stf)

24 locations × 90 days × 1 variable. Transposing the days × stations matrix before as.vector() is what puts the stations fastest; getting this order wrong silently scrambles the data, so check a value or two (stf[3, 5]$r should be R[5, 3]).

Your turn. Compute the spatio-temporal variogram for time lags 0 to 3 days, up to 20 km in 2 km bins.

Lags 0 to 3: tlags = 0:3.

q1 <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-04-01")
stf <- STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), as.Date(rownames(R))[q1], data.frame(r = as.vector(t(R[q1, ]))))
vst <- variogramST(r ~ 1, stf, tlags = 0:3, cutoff = 20, width = 2, na.omit = TRUE, progress = FALSE)
vst[1:6, c("np", "dist", "timelag", "gamma")]

At lag 0, the semivariance rises from 0.011 at 1.4 km to 0.041 at 5 km, then levels off (the missing first row, distance 0 at lag 0, would be each station with itself on the same day). The plot below shows all four lags: at short distances, the curves for longer lags start higher, and at large distances they level off together, just below the lag-0 curve.

Fitting models

A separable model in gstat has a spatial variogram, a temporal variogram (each with sill 1) and a joint sill. A metric model treats time as a third coordinate, with a space-time anisotropy factor converting days into kilometres: \gamma(\sqrt{h^2 + (\kappa u)^2}). There are others (product-sum, sum-metric), with more parameters. fit.StVariogram() fits them by weighted least squares, like fit.variogram(), and works much better when the distances are in kilometres than in metres (the optimizer’s step sizes don’t adapt to the scale of each parameter).

Your turn. Fit a separable model with exponential spatial and temporal parts.

The model type is "separable".

q1 <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-04-01")
stf <- STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), as.Date(rownames(R))[q1], data.frame(r = as.vector(t(R[q1, ]))))
vst <- variogramST(r ~ 1, stf, tlags = 0:3, cutoff = 20, width = 2, na.omit = TRUE, progress = FALSE)
start <- vgmST("separable", space = vgm(0.8, "Exp", 3, 0.2), time = vgm(0.8, "Exp", 1.5, 0.2), sill = 0.04)
fs <- fit.StVariogram(vst, start, method = "L-BFGS-B", lower = c(0.1, 0, 0.1, 0, 0.001), upper = c(50, 1, 20, 1, 1))
c(space_range_km = fs$space$range[2], time_range_days = fs$time$range[2], sill = fs$sill,
  lag1_correlation = exp(-1 / fs$time$range[2]), MSE = attr(fs, "MSE"))

A spatial range parameter of 2.3 km (a practical range of about 7 km), a temporal range of 3.1 days, and a joint sill of 0.036, matching the variance. The temporal range implies a correlation of 0.72 between consecutive days, much higher than the 0.34 you computed by hand. The difference matters: the fitted model has essentially no nugget, so it treats all of the variance as smooth and persistent, while part of it (measurement noise, the plume’s jumps from day to day) is neither. Remember that for the validation.

Your turn. Fit a metric model, and compare its fit.

Start the anisotropy at 3 km per day.

q1 <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-04-01")
stf <- STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), as.Date(rownames(R))[q1], data.frame(r = as.vector(t(R[q1, ]))))
vst <- variogramST(r ~ 1, stf, tlags = 0:3, cutoff = 20, width = 2, na.omit = TRUE, progress = FALSE)
start <- vgmST("metric", joint = vgm(0.04, "Exp", 3, 0.01), stAni = 3)
fm <- fit.StVariogram(vst, start, method = "L-BFGS-B", lower = c(0.001, 0.1, 0, 0.1))
c(range_km = fm$joint$range[2], km_per_day = fm$stAni, MSE = attr(fm, "MSE"))

A joint range of 2.5 km and an anisotropy of 0.97 km per day: one day apart counts like about a kilometre apart. Its MSE, 1.35 \times 10^{-5}, is practically the same as the separable model’s 1.37 \times 10^{-5}: the empirical variogram can’t tell the two apart.

Space-time kriging, and an honest test

To test the method, hide a random 15% of January’s measurements, predict them with krigeST() from everything else, and compare with two simpler methods: kriging each day from that day’s other stations (space only), and averaging the station’s own previous and next day (time only).

krigeST() takes the observations as an STSDF (sparse: only the observed combinations) and the targets as another STSDF, whose index lists each target’s station and day.

Your turn. Run the test.

The fitted model is fs.

q1 <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-04-01")
stf <- STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), as.Date(rownames(R))[q1], data.frame(r = as.vector(t(R[q1, ]))))
vst <- variogramST(r ~ 1, stf, tlags = 0:3, cutoff = 20, width = 2, na.omit = TRUE, progress = FALSE)
fs <- fit.StVariogram(vst, vgmST("separable", space = vgm(0.8, "Exp", 3, 0.2), time = vgm(0.8, "Exp", 1.5, 0.2), sill = 0.04),
                      method = "L-BFGS-B", lower = c(0.1, 0, 0.1, 0, 0.001), upper = c(50, 1, 20, 1, 1))
jan <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-02-01"); dj <- as.Date(rownames(R))[jan]
Rj <- R[jan, ]
set.seed(9111)
hide <- matrix(runif(length(Rj)) < 0.15, nrow(Rj)) & !is.na(Rj)          # 15% of the measurements
obs <- Rj; obs[hide] <- NA
sts <- as(STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), dj, data.frame(r = as.vector(t(obs)))), "STSDF")
sts <- sts[!is.na(sts$r), ]
h <- which(t(hide))                                                        # stations fastest
s_i <- (h - 1) %% ncol(Rj) + 1; t_i <- (h - 1) %/% ncol(Rj) + 1
target <- STSDF(SpatialPoints(xy), dj, data.frame(id = seq_along(h)), index = cbind(s_i, t_i))
k <- krigeST(r ~ 1, data = sts, newdata = target, modelList = fs, computeVar = TRUE)
truth <- Rj[cbind(t_i, s_i)]
sm <- fs$space; sm$psill <- sm$psill * fs$sill                             # the spatial part alone
space_only <- sapply(seq_along(h), function(i) {
  d <- data.frame(xy, r = obs[t_i[i], ]); d <- d[!is.na(d$r), ]
  krige(r ~ 1, ~x + y, d, newdata = xy[s_i[i], , drop = FALSE], model = sm, debug.level = 0)$var1.pred })
time_only <- sapply(seq_along(h), function(i) {
  z <- obs[, s_i[i]]; nb <- z[c(t_i[i] - 1, t_i[i] + 1)[c(t_i[i] > 1, t_i[i] < nrow(obs))]]
  if (all(is.na(nb))) 0 else mean(nb, na.rm = TRUE) })
rmse <- function(p) sqrt(mean((p - truth)^2))
c(hidden = length(h), spacetime = rmse(k$var1.pred), space = rmse(space_only), time = rmse(time_only), zero = rmse(0))

116 values hidden. Space-time kriging’s RMSE is 0.162, exactly as good as kriging each day in space alone (0.162). The station’s own neighbouring days alone give 0.183, and predicting zero (only the station and day effects) 0.204. So, on average, adding time didn’t help at all. Is that the whole story?

Your turn. An average hides a lot. Break the space-time and space-only errors down by station type.

Each hidden value’s station is s_i.

  krige(r ~ 1, ~x + y, d, newdata = xy[s_i[i], , drop = FALSE], model = sm, debug.level = 0)$var1.pred })
type <- stations$type[match(colnames(Rj), stations$station)][s_i]
round(rbind(spacetime = tapply((k$var1.pred - truth)^2, type, function(e) sqrt(mean(e))),
            space = tapply((space_only - truth)^2, type, function(e) sqrt(mean(e)))), 3)

No: it depends entirely on the station. At the roadside stations, strung along the motorway 5 to 6.5 km apart, space-time kriging cuts the error from 0.158 to 0.118, and at the rural stations, far from everything, from 0.171 to 0.148: there, the station’s own previous and next days are the best information available. In the town, where the urban stations are 1 to 2 km apart, it’s worse (0.129 against 0.106): with its too-strong temporal correlation, the model gives the station’s own neighbouring days too much weight compared with its close neighbours on the same day. At the industrial stations it’s worse too (0.220 against 0.196): their anomalies are driven by the plume, which switches on and off with the wind, and no stationary covariance can represent that. The average of 0.162 against 0.162 was hiding a gain in one place and a loss in another.

NoteGo deeper: non-separable covariances and physics

Separable covariances can’t represent transport: if a pollution episode moves east at 10 km a day, then station B, 10 km east of A, is most correlated with A one day earlier, not on the same day, and the correlation’s peak shifts with the lag. Non-separable families handle this: Gneiting’s class (2002) lets the spatial range change with the time lag; Lagrangian (frozen-field) models C(h - vu, 0) move a spatial covariance with a velocity v; and models from stochastic partial differential equations (advection-diffusion) build the physics in. With a dominant wind direction, the covariance is also asymmetric in space-time, which none of gstat’s built-in models allow. Wikle, Zammit-Mangion and Cressie’s Spatio-Temporal Statistics with R, chapter 4, and Gneiting’s “Nonseparable, Stationary Covariance Functions for Space-Time Data” (Journal of the American Statistical Association 97, 2002) are the places to start.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Calibration. Are the kriging variances honest? Compute the mean squared z-score, overall and by station type.

Divide by the kriging variance, k$var1.var.

q1 <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-04-01")
stf <- STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), as.Date(rownames(R))[q1], data.frame(r = as.vector(t(R[q1, ]))))
vst <- variogramST(r ~ 1, stf, tlags = 0:3, cutoff = 20, width = 2, na.omit = TRUE, progress = FALSE)
fs <- fit.StVariogram(vst, vgmST("separable", space = vgm(0.8, "Exp", 3, 0.2), time = vgm(0.8, "Exp", 1.5, 0.2), sill = 0.04),
                      method = "L-BFGS-B", lower = c(0.1, 0, 0.1, 0, 0.001), upper = c(50, 1, 20, 1, 1))
jan <- as.Date(rownames(R)) < as.Date("2025-02-01"); dj <- as.Date(rownames(R))[jan]
Rj <- R[jan, ]
set.seed(9111)
hide <- matrix(runif(length(Rj)) < 0.15, nrow(Rj)) & !is.na(Rj)
obs <- Rj; obs[hide] <- NA
sts <- as(STFDF(SpatialPoints(xy), dj, data.frame(r = as.vector(t(obs)))), "STSDF"); sts <- sts[!is.na(sts$r), ]
h <- which(t(hide)); s_i <- (h - 1) %% ncol(Rj) + 1; t_i <- (h - 1) %/% ncol(Rj) + 1
target <- STSDF(SpatialPoints(xy), dj, data.frame(id = seq_along(h)), index = cbind(s_i, t_i))
k <- krigeST(r ~ 1, data = sts, newdata = target, modelList = fs, computeVar = TRUE)
z2 <- (k$var1.pred - Rj[cbind(t_i, s_i)])^2 / k$var1.var
type <- stations$type[match(colnames(Rj), stations$station)][s_i]
round(c(all = mean(z2), tapply(z2, type, mean)), 2)

A mean squared z-score of 3.19 overall: the errors are, on average, \sqrt{3.19} = 1.8 times as large as the kriging standard errors claim. At the roadside stations (1.17) the variances are about right, at the rural ones (1.70) somewhat optimistic, and in the town (2.92) and at the industrial stations (6.44) badly so. That’s the missing nugget and the too-strong temporal correlation, plus the plume. A model can predict reasonably and still misstate its uncertainty, and only validation reveals it. Compare Lesson 4’s soil kriging, whose z-scores were honest (1.03).

Wrap-up

You’ve modelled correlation in space and time together. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Spatio-temporal covariance C(h, u), estimated by hand from the stations’ series; separable if it factors as space times time.
  • gstat’s tools: STFDF objects (space fastest), variogramST(), and fit.StVariogram() for separable and metric models (in kilometres).
  • Space-time kriging fills in hidden measurements from both the neighbouring stations and the neighbouring days.
  • Validation by station type showed where it helps (stations far from their neighbours) and where it fails (the plume’s non-stationarity), and that its variances were overconfident there.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Spatio-temporal covariance
C(h, u), the covariance between values a distance h and a time lag u apart.
Separable covariance
A covariance that factors into a purely spatial and a purely temporal part.
Spatio-temporal variogram
\gamma(h, u) = \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}[Z(s+h, t+u) - Z(s, t)], estimated for distance bins and time lags.
Metric model
A spatio-temporal variogram that treats time as a scaled third dimension.
Space-time anisotropy
The factor converting time lags into equivalent distances (km per day).
Space-time kriging
Kriging with a spatio-temporal covariance, using neighbours in both space and time.
Non-stationarity
Statistical properties that change with location or time, such as a plume that appears only with certain winds.
