Kriging

The best guess between the samples, and how good it is

Predict mercury where nobody sampled. Start with inverse distance weighting, then solve the ordinary kriging equations by hand for one garden, and check them against gstat. Krige a map of the whole survey area with a map of its standard errors, judge it by leave-one-out cross-validation, and turn it into what the regulator needs: the probability that each 50 m square exceeds 1 mg/kg.

Module 9 · Lesson 4 of 13 Week 136 of 160 About 5–7 hours

A family on the north-eastern edge of the plant wants to know whether their vegetable garden is safe. Nobody took a sample in it: the nearest one is 97 m away. Hana has to give them a number, and an honest idea of how sure that number is. That’s the problem kriging solves: the best linear prediction of the value at an unsampled place, with its prediction variance, using the variogram to decide how much each sample should count.

The method is named after Danie Krige, a South African mining engineer who in the 1950s estimated gold grades in new blocks of ore from nearby boreholes; Georges Matheron, in Fontainebleau, turned his practice into theory in the 1960s.

Kriging is regression where the “inputs” are the neighbouring samples, and the variogram says how much to trust each one. Its party trick is that it knows how wrong it might be.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • predict with inverse distance weighting, the simple baseline
  • set up and solve the ordinary kriging equations by hand, and compute the kriging variance
  • krige with gstat, at one point and over a whole grid
  • map the prediction and its standard error, and see where the uncertainty comes from
  • compare methods by leave-one-out cross-validation
  • turn the predictions into probabilities of exceeding a threshold

The variogram, once more

Kriging uses the variogram at the distances between the target and its nearby samples, mostly a few hundred metres. So we fit it up to 1 km, where the trend matters less, and to log mercury itself (Lesson 5 brings the trend back in).

The setup code reads the data and fits that model. Its coordinates for the garden, and a 50 m prediction grid over the survey square, come with it.

Your turn. Print the fitted model, and evaluate it at 100, 500 and 1,000 m with variogramLine().

Add 1000.

print(m)
variogramLine(m, dist_vector = c(100, 500, 1000))

A tiny nugget (0.006), and an exponential with a partial sill of 4.6 and a range parameter of 3,887 m, far beyond the survey: over the first kilometre, this model is almost a straight line, \gamma(100) = 0.12, \gamma(500) = 0.57, \gamma(1000) = 1.06. That’s the trend’s doing (the variogram doesn’t level off, Lesson 3), and the fitted sill means nothing on its own. It doesn’t matter much here: ordinary kriging only uses the variogram’s shape over the distances to the neighbours, and that’s well estimated.

A baseline: inverse distance weighting

The simplest spatial predictor is a weighted average of the nearby samples, with weights proportional to 1/d^p: inverse distance weighting (IDW), usually with p = 2. It’s intuitive and quick, but its weights ignore the variogram, ignore how the samples cluster, and give no measure of uncertainty.

Your turn. Predict log mercury in the garden by IDW from its 10 nearest samples, and back-transform to mg/kg.

The usual power is 2.

d <- sqrt((soil$x - garden$x)^2 + (soil$y - garden$y)^2)
nn <- order(d)[1:10]                                          # the 10 nearest samples
w <- 1 / d[nn]^2
w <- w / sum(w)
c(nearest_m = d[nn[1]], prediction = sum(w * soil$z[nn]), mg_kg = exp(sum(w * soil$z[nn])))

The nearest sample is 97 m away. IDW predicts a log value of 0.048, about 1.05 mg/kg: right at the 1 mg/kg screening value. The 10 neighbours range from 0.39 to 2.16 mg/kg, so the answer depends a lot on how they’re weighted.

Ordinary kriging by hand

Kriging also predicts with a weighted sum, \hat Z(s_0) = \sum_i \lambda_i Z(s_i), but chooses the weights to minimize the expected squared error, subject to \sum_i \lambda_i = 1 (so the prediction is unbiased whatever the mean, which is what makes it ordinary kriging; simple kriging assumes the mean is known). With a variogram, the minimization gives a linear system: for n neighbours, with \Gamma_{ij} = \gamma(s_i - s_j), \gamma_{0i} = \gamma(s_0 - s_i) and a Lagrange multiplier \mu for the constraint,

\begin{pmatrix} \Gamma & \mathbf 1 \\ \mathbf 1^\top & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol\lambda \\ \mu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol\gamma_0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{OK}} = \boldsymbol\lambda^\top \boldsymbol\gamma_0 + \mu .

\sigma^2_{\text{OK}} is the kriging variance, the expected squared prediction error. It depends on where the samples are and on the variogram, but not on the values measured.

Your turn. Build the system for the garden’s 10 nearest samples, solve it, and predict.

The last equation says the weights sum to 1.

d <- sqrt((soil$x - garden$x)^2 + (soil$y - garden$y)^2)
nn <- order(d)[1:10]
gam <- function(h) variogramLine(m, dist_vector = h)$gamma
Gamma <- matrix(gam(as.vector(as.matrix(dist(soil[nn, c("x", "y")])))), 10)
A <- rbind(cbind(Gamma, 1), c(rep(1, 10), 0))                 # the 11 x 11 system
b <- c(gam(d[nn]), 1)
sol <- solve(A, b)
lambda <- sol[1:10]
c(sum_weights = sum(lambda), prediction = sum(lambda * soil$z[nn]))

A prediction of 0.0135 on the log scale, 1.01 mg/kg. Look at the weights (round(lambda, 3)): 0.50 for the nearest sample, 0.40 for the second, and only 0.06 or less for the other eight, some of them slightly negative. Kriging notices that the farther samples are screened by the two near ones: once you know the values 97 and 123 m away, a sample 400 m away in the same direction adds little. IDW can’t do that: it gave the eight farther samples 32% of the weight between them, kriging only 10%.

Your turn. Compute the kriging variance, \boldsymbol\lambda^\top\boldsymbol\gamma_0 + \mu, and the standard error.

Add the Lagrange multiplier, mu.

d <- sqrt((soil$x - garden$x)^2 + (soil$y - garden$y)^2)
nn <- order(d)[1:10]
gam <- function(h) variogramLine(m, dist_vector = h)$gamma
Gamma <- matrix(gam(as.vector(as.matrix(dist(soil[nn, c("x", "y")])))), 10)
A <- rbind(cbind(Gamma, 1), c(rep(1, 10), 0))
b <- c(gam(d[nn]), 1)
sol <- solve(A, b)
lambda <- sol[1:10]; mu <- sol[11]
ok_var <- sum(lambda * gam(d[nn])) + mu
c(variance = ok_var, se = sqrt(ok_var))

A variance of 0.136, a standard error of 0.37 on the log scale. A 95% prediction interval for log mercury is 0.014 \pm 1.96 \times 0.37, which back-transforms to 0.49 to 2.1 mg/kg. So the honest answer for the family is: “probably about 1 mg/kg, possibly as low as 0.5 or as high as 2; we should sample the garden.” That’s what the kriging variance is for.

Kriging with gstat

Your turn. krige() does all of this. Krige the garden from its 10 nearest samples (nmax = 10), and compare.

nmax = 10.

k <- krige(z ~ 1, locations = ~x + y, data = soil, newdata = garden, model = m, nmax = 10)
k

The same 0.01354 and 0.1356. z ~ 1 asks for ordinary kriging (a constant but unknown mean); nmax limits it to the nearest samples, a local neighbourhood, which is faster and lets the mean drift slowly across the map. Without nmax, gstat uses all 200 samples.

Your turn. Krige the whole 60 × 60 grid from all the samples, and find the area (each cell is 0.0025 km²) where the predicted log value is above 0, that is, where the predicted median is above 1 mg/kg.

Predict at the grid.

kg <- krige(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, newdata = grid, model = m, debug.level = 0)
print(summary(kg$var1.pred))
sum(kg$var1.pred > 0) * 0.0025                                 # km2

The predictions run from -3.7 (0.025 mg/kg) to 1.29 (3.6 mg/kg), and 1.21 km² of the 9 km² square has a predicted median above 1 mg/kg. Kriging is an exact interpolator with no nugget: at a sampled location, the prediction equals the measurement. With this tiny nugget, it nearly does.

Your turn. The standard-error map (right, above) looks like a map of the samples. Correlate each cell’s kriging standard error with its distance to the nearest sample.

The kriging variance is kg$var1.var.

kg <- krige(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, newdata = grid, model = m, debug.level = 0)
nearest <- sapply(seq_len(nrow(grid)), function(i) min(sqrt((soil$x - grid$x[i])^2 + (soil$y - grid$y[i])^2)))
c(max_se = max(sqrt(kg$var1.var)), correlation = cor(sqrt(kg$var1.var), nearest))

A correlation of 0.90 with the distance to the nearest sample; the largest standard error, 0.69, is in a corner of the square, where there are samples on one side only. The kriging variance depends on the geometry of the samples and on the variogram, not on the data values, so it can be computed before sampling. That’s how sampling campaigns are designed: put the next samples where the kriging variance is largest, or where it matters most.

Cross-validation

Did kriging get it right? Leave-one-out cross-validation removes each sample in turn, predicts it from the other 199, and compares. Three numbers matter: the mean error (bias, should be near 0), the root mean squared error (accuracy), and the mean squared z-score, the squared error divided by the kriging variance (calibration: near 1 if the kriging variances are honest).

Your turn. Run krige.cv(), and compute the three.

The z-scores are in cv$zscore.

cv <- krige.cv(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, model = m, debug.level = 0)
c(mean_error = mean(cv$residual), rmse = sqrt(mean(cv$residual^2)),
  mean_sq_z = mean(cv$zscore^2))

A mean error of -0.0002, an RMSE of 0.443 on the log scale (predictions typically within a factor of e^{0.44} = 1.6), and a mean squared z-score of 1.03: the kriging variances are almost exactly as large as the real errors, and 5.5% of the z-scores fall outside \pm 1.96, close to the 5% they should. The model’s uncertainty is honest.

Your turn. Cross-validate IDW too (krige.cv() without a model does IDW; set = list(idp = 2) sets the power), and compare with predicting every sample by the mean of the others.

idp = 2.

cvi <- krige.cv(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, debug.level = 0, set = list(idp = 2))
loo_mean <- (sum(soil$z) - soil$z) / (nrow(soil) - 1)
c(idw = sqrt(mean(cvi$residual^2)), mean_only = sqrt(mean((soil$z - loo_mean)^2)))

IDW (from all the samples) has an RMSE of 0.630, and the mean alone 1.161, against kriging’s 0.443. Kriging’s errors are 30% smaller than IDW’s. IDW is a reasonable quick look; for decisions, krige.

From predictions to decisions

On the log scale, the kriging prediction and its standard error describe an approximately normal distribution for the true value in each cell. So the probability that a cell exceeds 1 mg/kg (\log 1 = 0) is

P(Z(s_0) > \log 1) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{\log 1 - \hat Z(s_0)}{\sigma_{\text{OK}}(s_0)}\right).

A map of that probability is what a regulator can act on: clean up where it’s high, sample more where it’s in between.

Your turn. Compute the exceedance probability for every cell, and the area where it’s above 0.9, 0.5 and 0.1.

The third threshold is 0.1.

kg <- krige(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, newdata = grid, model = m, debug.level = 0)
p1 <- 1 - pnorm((log(1) - kg$var1.pred) / sqrt(kg$var1.var))
sapply(c(p90 = 0.9, p50 = 0.5, p10 = 0.1), function(p) sum(p1 > p) * 0.0025)

0.49 km² is almost certainly above 1 mg/kg (probability over 90%), 1.21 km² probably (over 50%, the same as the predicted-median area), and 2.53 km² possibly (over 10%). In the residential area north-east of the plant, 0.24 km² has a probability above 10%: that’s where Hana would recommend garden sampling. The gap between the 90% and 10% areas is the price of having only 200 samples; more samples would shrink it.

NoteGo deeper: simple kriging, and why kriging is “BLUP”

If the mean m is known, simple kriging predicts \hat Z(s_0) = m + \mathbf c_0^\top C^{-1}(\mathbf z - m\mathbf 1), with covariances C(h) = C(0) - \gamma(h) instead of the variogram, and no constraint on the weights; its variance C(0) - \mathbf c_0^\top C^{-1}\mathbf c_0 is smaller than ordinary kriging’s, because it doesn’t have to estimate the mean. (With gstat, give the mean as beta in krige().) That formula is exactly the conditional mean of a multivariate normal distribution (Module 3), which is the Gaussian-process view of Lesson 5. Ordinary kriging is the best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP) when the mean is unknown, the same BLUP that appears in mixed models (Module 3, Lesson 11), where the “random effects” are predicted by the same algebra. Cressie’s Statistics for Spatial Data, chapter 3, derives all of them.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · The garden, in mg/kg. The kriging prediction on the log scale back-transforms to the median, e^{\hat Z}. If the log value is normal, the mean is e^{\hat Z + \sigma^2/2}. For the garden, compute both, and the probability of exceeding 1 mg/kg.

The mean adds half the variance: divide by 2.

k <- krige(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, newdata = garden, model = m, nmax = 10, debug.level = 0)
pr <- k$var1.pred; s2 <- k$var1.var
c(median = exp(pr), mean = exp(pr + s2 / 2), p_above_1 = 1 - pnorm((0 - pr) / sqrt(s2)))

A median of 1.01 mg/kg, a mean of 1.08, and a probability of 51% of exceeding 1 mg/kg: a coin toss. For a health question, the mean matters (exposure adds up over the garden), and so does the probability. Both say the same thing: take a sample. Back-transforming a kriged log value with e^{\hat Z} alone underestimates the mean everywhere, more where the uncertainty is larger.

Then try the cell with the exceedance probability closest to 0.5 instead. Which is the better place to sample, and for whom?

Wrap-up

You’ve predicted where nobody measured, and said how sure you are. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • IDW is a quick weighted average; ordinary kriging chooses the weights from the variogram to minimize the error, with weights summing to 1, and gives a kriging variance.
  • By hand and with gstat: the garden’s prediction is 1.01 mg/kg (log 0.014, standard error 0.37), with half the weight on the nearest sample and screening of the far ones.
  • The standard-error map follows the sampling gaps (correlation 0.90 with the distance to the nearest sample).
  • Cross-validation: RMSE 0.443 for kriging against 0.630 for IDW, with honest variances (mean squared z-score 1.03).
  • Decisions: 0.49 km² is almost certainly above 1 mg/kg, 2.53 km² possibly, and the garden is a coin toss: sample it.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Inverse distance weighting (IDW)
Prediction by a weighted average with weights proportional to 1/d^p.
Ordinary kriging
The best linear unbiased prediction with an unknown constant mean, from a variogram; its weights sum to 1.
Simple kriging
Kriging with a known mean, using covariances.
Kriging variance
The expected squared prediction error of kriging; it depends on the sampling geometry and the variogram, not on the values.
Screening
The reduction of a sample’s kriging weight when nearer samples lie between it and the target.
Local neighbourhood
Kriging from only the nearest samples (nmax).
Leave-one-out cross-validation
Predicting each sample from all the others, to measure bias, accuracy and calibration.
Exceedance probability
The probability that the true value at a location exceeds a threshold, from the prediction and its standard error.
