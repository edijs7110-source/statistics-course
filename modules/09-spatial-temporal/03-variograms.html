Variograms

How fast does resemblance fade?

The variogram is the geostatistician’s main tool: half the average squared difference between two measurements, as a function of their distance. Compute it by hand from the variogram cloud, then with gstat; see what a trend does to it, and remove it; fit exponential, spherical, Gaussian and Matérn models and read their nugget, sill and range; and find the anisotropy that the wind left in the soil.

Module 9 · Lesson 3 of 13 Week 135 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 2 measured spatial dependence with correlations. Geostatistics, which grew up in the 1950s and 1960s in South African gold mines and French mining schools, uses a different tool: the variogram. It asks how different two measurements are, on average, as a function of the distance between them. Close together, they’re similar; far apart, as different as two random samples. How fast the difference grows, and where it stops growing, is what kriging (Lesson 4) needs to predict between the samples.

The variogram is one plot, but it answers three questions at once: how noisy are the measurements, how variable is the soil, and how far does a sample’s influence reach?

In this lesson you’ll:

  • compute semivariances by hand, from the variogram cloud
  • compute an empirical variogram with gstat::variogram()
  • recognize what a trend does to a variogram, and remove it
  • fit exponential, spherical, Gaussian and Matérn models, and read their nugget, sill and range
  • look for anisotropy with directional variograms

The semivariance

For a quantity Z measured at locations s, the semivariogram (usually just “variogram”) is

\gamma(h) = \tfrac{1}{2} \, \mathrm{E}\big[(Z(s + h) - Z(s))^2\big],

half the expected squared difference between two values a distance h apart. (The half makes \gamma equal to the variance for far-apart, uncorrelated pairs.) That only makes sense if \gamma depends on the separation h and not on where you are: the assumption of stationarity. If it also depends only on the length of h, not its direction, the field is isotropic.

For two specific samples i and j, \frac{1}{2}(z_i - z_j)^2 is a single noisy estimate of \gamma(h_{ij}). All of them together form the variogram cloud: 200 \times 199 / 2 = 19{,}900 pairs here. The empirical variogram averages the cloud within distance bins.

The setup code reads the soil data, takes logs, and computes the distance to the cell room in kilometres.

Your turn. Build the variogram cloud: every pair’s distance and half squared difference. Then average the semivariance over the pairs less than 100 m apart.

Squared: ^2.

D <- as.matrix(dist(soil[, c("x", "y")]))                  # distances, m
G <- 0.5 * outer(soil$z, soil$z, "-")^2                     # half squared differences
keep <- lower.tri(D)                                        # each pair once
h <- D[keep]; g <- G[keep]
c(pairs = length(h), close_pairs = sum(h < 100), gamma_close = mean(g[h < 100]))

19,900 pairs, of which 79 are within 100 m, with an average semivariance of 0.081. The variance of all 200 log values is 1.34, so samples less than 100 m apart differ by only a small fraction of the overall variation. Each single pair’s value is very noisy (a squared difference is a chi-squared-like quantity with one degree of freedom), which is why the cloud itself is hard to read and is always averaged.

Your turn. Now average the semivariance for pairs 1,400 to 1,500 m apart, and compare it with the variance of the log values.

Up to 1,500 m.

D <- as.matrix(dist(soil[, c("x", "y")]))
G <- 0.5 * outer(soil$z, soil$z, "-")^2
keep <- lower.tri(D); h <- D[keep]; g <- G[keep]
c(gamma_far = mean(g[h > 1400 & h < 1500]), variance = var(soil$z))

2.09, more than the variance of 1.34. For a stationary field that can’t happen: the semivariance of far-apart pairs should level off at the variance (the sill). Here it keeps growing, because pairs 1.4 km apart are typically one sample near the cell room and one far away. That’s the trend from Lesson 2: the mean isn’t constant, so the field isn’t stationary, and the raw variogram mixes the trend with the dependence.

The empirical variogram

gstat’s variogram() does the binning: cutoff is the largest distance considered (a common rule is up to half the largest distance in the data), and width the bin width. The formula’s left side is the variable, its right side the trend (~ 1 for a constant mean), and locations = ~x + y names the coordinates.

Your turn. Compute the empirical variogram of log mercury up to 1.5 km in 100 m bins, and check its first bin against your hand calculation.

Bins 100 m wide.

v <- variogram(z ~ 1, locations = ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
print(v[, c("np", "dist", "gamma")])
v$gamma[1]

The first bin has the same 79 pairs and the same 0.081. np is the number of pairs in each bin and dist their average distance. The semivariance climbs steadily, from 0.08 to 2.09, without levelling off: the trend again.

Your turn. Put the trend in the formula: z ~ dist_km computes the variogram of the residuals from a regression on the distance to the cell room. Compare its first and last bins with the raw variogram’s.

The trend is the distance, dist_km.

vr <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, locations = ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
print(vr[, c("np", "dist", "gamma")])
vr$gamma[c(1, 15)]

0.074 at the shortest distances, much as before, but only 0.71 at 1.45 km instead of 2.09. The residual variogram rises quickly to about 0.4 at 500 m, flattens, and rises slowly again beyond a kilometre, toward the residuals’ variance of 0.64 and a bit beyond. The slow rise at large distances is real: as you’ll see below, the dependence reaches much farther in one direction than in the other.

Variogram models

Kriging needs \gamma(h) at every distance, not just at the bin centres, and it needs a function that’s guaranteed to give valid (non-negative) variances for every combination of locations. So we fit a model from a family known to be valid. Every model has three features:

  • the nugget c_0: the jump at the origin, \gamma(0^+). Measurement error plus variation at scales smaller than the closest samples (the name comes from gold nuggets: two samples a centimetre apart can differ completely).
  • the sill c_0 + c_1: the plateau, the variance of far-apart values; c_1 is the partial sill.
  • the range a: how far the dependence reaches.

The common models, with c_1 and a:

Model \gamma(h) - c_0 for h > 0 Reaches 95% of the sill at
Spherical c_1\left(\frac{3h}{2a} - \frac{h^3}{2a^3}\right) for h \le a, c_1 beyond exactly a (and stops)
Exponential c_1(1 - e^{-h/a}) 3a (the practical range)
Gaussian c_1(1 - e^{-h^2/a^2}) \sqrt{3}\,a

The Matérn family adds a smoothness parameter \kappa: \kappa = 0.5 is the exponential, \kappa \to \infty the Gaussian. Rough fields (soil, ore grades) have small \kappa and rise linearly from the nugget; smooth ones (groundwater levels, temperature) have large \kappa and rise like a parabola.

fit.variogram() fits a model to the empirical variogram by weighted least squares (by default, each bin’s weight is its number of pairs divided by its distance squared, so the short distances that matter most for kriging count most). You give it a starting model with vgm(psill, model, range, nugget).

Your turn. Fit an exponential model to the residual variogram, and compute its practical range.

The exponential model is "Exp".

vr <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
fe <- fit.variogram(vr, vgm(psill = 0.6, model = "Exp", range = 300, nugget = 0.05))
print(fe)
c(nugget = fe$psill[1], partial_sill = fe$psill[2], range = fe$range[2], practical_range = 3 * fe$range[2])

A nugget of 0.018, a partial sill of 0.692 (so a sill of 0.71), and a range parameter of 693 m, which means a practical range of 2.1 km. The nugget is only 2.6% of the sill: two samples a few metres apart would differ very little, so the laboratory’s measurement error and the soil’s very-small-scale patchiness are small. The starting values only need to be roughly right; the fit adjusts them.

Your turn. Fit a spherical model, which reaches its sill at a finite range, and compare.

The spherical model is "Sph".

vr <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
fs <- fit.variogram(vr, vgm(0.6, "Sph", 900, 0.05))
c(nugget = fs$psill[1], partial_sill = fs$psill[2], range = fs$range[2])

A nugget of 0.059, a partial sill of 0.587 and a range of 1,440 m. To fit the steep rise at short distances and the slow rise beyond a kilometre with a curve that must stop at its range, the spherical model compromises: a bigger nugget, a lower sill. Different models can describe the same points quite differently near the origin, and the origin is what matters for kriging.

Your turn. Compare the weighted sums of squared errors (attr(fit, "SSErr")) of the exponential, spherical and Gaussian fits.

The attribute is "SSErr".

vr <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
fits <- list(Exp = fit.variogram(vr, vgm(0.6, "Exp", 300, 0.05)),
             Sph = fit.variogram(vr, vgm(0.6, "Sph", 900, 0.05)),
             Gau = fit.variogram(vr, vgm(0.6, "Gau", 500, 0.05)))
sapply(fits, attr, "SSErr")

The exponential fits best (5.6 \times 10^{-5}), then the spherical (9.0 \times 10^{-5}), and the Gaussian worst (1.4 \times 10^{-4}): soil contamination is rough, and the Gaussian model, which describes very smooth fields, rises too slowly at short distances. The SSErr is a weighted least-squares criterion, useful for comparing models fitted to the same variogram, but not a measure of predictive skill; Lesson 4’s cross-validation is the better judge.

Anisotropy

Isotropy means the dependence is the same in every direction. A wind that blew mostly from the south-west for fifty years, though, would spread mercury further along the wind than across it. A directional variogram uses only pairs whose direction is within a tolerance (by default ±22.5°) of a given angle, measured clockwise from north: 45° is north-east (along the wind), 135° south-east (across it).

Your turn. Compute the residual variogram in the two directions, and average each direction’s semivariance over the bins below 600 m.

Across the wind: 135°.

vd <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1200, width = 150, alpha = c(45, 135))
print(vd[, c("np", "dist", "gamma", "dir.hor")])
short <- vd$dist < 600
tapply(vd$gamma[short], vd$dir.hor[short], mean)

Below 600 m, the average semivariance is 0.21 along the wind and 0.33 across it. At 380 m, for example, it’s 0.23 north-east and 0.48 south-east: samples 380 m apart along the wind are as alike as samples about 200 m apart across it. The dependence reaches roughly twice as far along the wind. That’s geometric anisotropy: the same variogram shape, with a range that depends on direction, which a stretch of the coordinates turns back into isotropy. Lesson 5 puts it into kriging.

NoteGo deeper: why not just any curve?

A variogram model must be conditionally negative definite: for any locations s_1, \dots, s_n and weights \lambda_i that sum to zero, \sum_i \sum_j \lambda_i \lambda_j \gamma(s_i - s_j) \le 0. That’s equivalent to saying that the variance of every linear combination \sum \lambda_i Z(s_i) with weights summing to zero, -\sum_i\sum_j \lambda_i\lambda_j\gamma(s_i - s_j), is non-negative. A curve drawn through the empirical points by hand, or a polynomial, can violate it, and then kriging can give negative variances. Bochner’s theorem characterizes the valid covariance functions as the Fourier transforms of non-negative measures, which is how families like the Matérn are proven valid. Also note: a model valid in one dimension may not be valid in two or three; the spherical model is valid only up to three dimensions. Noel Cressie’s Statistics for Spatial Data (Wiley, 1993), chapter 2, has the details.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Matérn. Fit a Matérn model with its smoothness \kappa estimated too (fit.kappa = TRUE), starting from \kappa = 1. What \kappa does it choose, and what does that say about the field?

fit.kappa = TRUE.

vr <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, data = soil, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
fm <- suppressWarnings(fit.variogram(vr, vgm(0.6, "Mat", 300, 0.05, kappa = 1), fit.kappa = TRUE))
print(fm)
fm$kappa[2]

\kappa = 0.4, a little rougher than the exponential’s 0.5, with no nugget at all: the rough Matérn absorbs the small nugget into its steep start. gstat searches \kappa over a grid (0.3, 0.4, …, up to 5) and keeps the best, which is why it’s a round number. The fit (SSErr 5.2 \times 10^{-5}) is barely better than the exponential’s: the data say “rough, about exponential”, and the simpler model is the usual choice.

Challenge 2 · Only the grid. The 56 samples of the 2018 campaign cluster near the cell room. Fit the exponential model to the 144 grid samples only. What happens to the shortest-distance bins, and to the fitted nugget?

The grid campaign is "grid 2019".

grid <- soil[soil$campaign == "grid 2019", ]
vg <- variogram(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, data = grid, cutoff = 1500, width = 100)
print(vg[, c("np", "dist", "gamma")])
fg <- fit.variogram(vg, vgm(0.6, "Exp", 300, 0.05))
c(nugget = fg$psill[1], partial_sill = fg$psill[2], range = fg$range[2])

With the grid alone, no two samples are closer than 100 m (they’re 250 m apart, jittered), so the first bin is at 176 m, with 57 pairs. The fitted nugget jumps to 0.107, six times the 0.018 from all the samples, and the range parameter grows to 1,117 m: below 175 m, the model has no data and has to extrapolate. That’s why survey designers add some close pairs to a grid: without them, the nugget, the most important number for kriging at short range, is a guess.

Quick quiz

1. What is \gamma(h)?

For a stationary field, \gamma(h) = \sigma^2 - C(h), the variance minus the covariance.

2. The raw variogram kept rising above the variance. Why?

A stationary variogram levels off at the variance; a trend makes it keep climbing.

3. The nugget represents:

It’s the jump of the variogram at the origin.

4. An exponential model with range parameter 693 m reaches 95% of its sill at about:

The practical range of the exponential model is three times its range parameter.

5. Why fit a model instead of using the empirical variogram directly?

Only conditionally negative definite functions are safe.

6. The semivariance at 380 m was 0.23 toward the north-east and 0.48 toward the south-east. That’s:

Geometric anisotropy, from fifty years of south-westerly wind.

Wrap-up

You’ve built the geostatistician’s main tool. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The semivariance \frac{1}{2}(z_i - z_j)^2, averaged over pairs in distance bins, is the empirical variogram: 0.081 for pairs within 100 m.
  • A trend makes the variogram climb without limit (2.09 at 1.45 km, above the variance of 1.34). Model the trend (z ~ dist_km) and look at the residuals’ variogram.
  • Models have a nugget, a sill and a range. The exponential fits best here: nugget 0.018, sill 0.71, practical range 2.1 km.
  • Anisotropy: the dependence reaches about twice as far along the wind (north-east) as across it.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Semivariogram (variogram)
\gamma(h) = \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{E}[(Z(s+h) - Z(s))^2], half the expected squared difference at separation h.
Variogram cloud
The half squared differences of all pairs, against their distances.
Stationarity
The statistical properties don’t depend on location; for the variogram, a constant mean and a \gamma that depends only on the separation.
Isotropy and anisotropy
Dependence that’s the same in every direction, or that depends on direction.
Nugget, sill, range
The variogram’s jump at the origin, its plateau, and the distance at which it reaches the plateau.
Practical range
The distance at which a model reaches 95% of its sill: 3a for the exponential model.
Matérn model
A family of variogram models with a smoothness parameter \kappa; \kappa = 0.5 is the exponential.
