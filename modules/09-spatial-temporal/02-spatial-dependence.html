Spatial dependence

Near things are more alike

Measure how much each soil sample resembles its neighbours. Build a neighbour list and spatial weights, compute the spatial lag, Moran’s I by hand and with spdep, test it by permutation, meet Geary’s C, see how much of the dependence is just the trend away from the cell room, draw a correlogram, and find the local hot spots with LISA.

Module 9 · Lesson 2 of 13 Week 134 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Waldo Tobler’s “first law of geography” says that everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things. For the soil survey, it means a sample tells you something about the soil 100 m away, less about the soil 1 km away, and nothing about the soil 3 km away. This lesson measures that resemblance, the spatial autocorrelation, the same way Module 6 measured how much today resembles yesterday.

Autocorrelation was a nuisance in time series and a feature in forecasting. It’s the same here: it breaks the usual standard errors, and it’s what makes spatial prediction possible.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • define each sample’s neighbours, and turn them into spatial weights
  • compute the spatial lag, the average of each sample’s neighbours
  • compute Moran’s I by hand, then test it with moran.test() and by permutation
  • compare Moran’s I with Geary’s C
  • separate the trend from the dependence, and draw a correlogram
  • find local clusters with LISA, and see why their p-values need care

Neighbours and weights

Every measure of spatial dependence compares each observation with its neighbours, so the first step is to decide who they are. For points, the usual choices are the k nearest points, or all points within a distance d. The neighbours are then turned into a spatial weights matrix W, with w_{ij} > 0 if j is a neighbour of i and 0 otherwise. Most often the rows are standardized to sum to 1 (style "W" in spdep), so that \sum_j w_{ij} z_j is the average of i’s neighbours.

The setup code reads the soil data, takes the logarithm of mercury (it’s skewed, and the log is what the module works with from here on), and loads spdep.

Your turn. Find each sample’s 6 nearest neighbours with knearneigh(), convert them to a neighbour list with knn2nb(), and count the links. Is the relation symmetric (is every sample a neighbour of its neighbours)?

Six neighbours: k = 6.

nb <- knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6))
c(links = sum(card(nb)), symmetric = is.symmetric.nb(nb))

1,200 links, six for each of the 200 samples, and the relation isn’t symmetric: a sample on the edge of the survey has neighbours that are closer to other samples than to it. That’s fine for Moran’s I; card(nb) counts each sample’s neighbours.

Your turn. Make row-standardized weights with nb2listw(), compute the spatial lag (each sample’s neighbour average) with lag.listw(), and correlate it with the samples’ own values.

Row-standardized is style "W".

nb <- knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6))
lw <- nb2listw(nb, style = "W")
lag <- lag.listw(lw, z)
cor(z, lag)

0.915: a sample’s log mercury is very close to its neighbours’ average. That’s the spatial version of the lag-1 autocorrelation of Module 6. Plotted against each other (lag against value), the samples form the Moran scatterplot, whose slope is Moran’s I.

Moran’s I

Moran’s I is the most used measure of spatial autocorrelation. With z_i centred on their mean,

I = \frac{n}{S_0} \, \frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij} z_i z_j}{\sum_i z_i^2}, \qquad S_0 = \sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}.

It’s a correlation-like number: near 0 when neighbours are unrelated (its expected value under no autocorrelation is -1/(n-1), slightly negative), positive when neighbours are alike, negative when they differ (a checkerboard). With row-standardized weights, S_0 = n and I is the slope of the Moran scatterplot.

Your turn. Compute Moran’s I by hand, with the weights as a matrix from listw2mat().

W z, with the centred values: W %*% zc.

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
W <- listw2mat(lw)                          # 200 x 200, rows sum to 1
zc <- z - mean(z)
(length(z) / sum(W)) * sum(zc * (W %*% zc)) / sum(zc^2)

I = 0.822, very strong positive autocorrelation. It’s close to, but not the same as, the correlation of 0.915 with the lag: Moran’s I is a regression slope rather than a correlation, and it uses the overall mean for both the values and their lags.

Your turn. moran.test() computes the same I, and its variance under the hypothesis of no autocorrelation, for a z-test. Run it.

Give it the values and the weights, lw.

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
mt <- moran.test(z, lw)
print(mt)
mt$statistic

I = 0.822 against an expected -0.005, with a standard deviate of 21.7: overwhelming evidence that nearby samples are alike, which nobody doubted. The test’s real use is on residuals, where the answer matters: if a regression’s residuals are autocorrelated, its standard errors are wrong (Lesson 7).

The z-test relies on approximations. A permutation test needs none: shuffle the values among the locations many times, recompute I each time, and see where the real I falls among the shuffled ones. A shuffle destroys any spatial pattern while keeping the values, so the shuffled I’s show what I looks like by chance.

Your turn. Run moran.mc() with 999 permutations, and find the largest of the 999 permuted values.

nsim = 999.

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
set.seed(9021)
mc <- moran.mc(z, lw, nsim = 999)
c(I = unname(mc$statistic), p = mc$p.value, max_permuted = max(mc$res[1:999]))

None of the 999 shuffles came anywhere near: the largest permuted I was 0.160, against the real 0.822. The p-value, (0 + 1)/(999 + 1) = 0.001, is the smallest 999 permutations can give. (mc$res holds the permuted values, plus the observed one at the end.)

Geary’s C looks at squared differences between neighbours instead of products:

C = \frac{(n-1)}{2 S_0} \, \frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij} (z_i - z_j)^2}{\sum_i (z_i - \bar z)^2}.

It’s 1 with no autocorrelation, below 1 when neighbours are alike, and above 1 when they differ. It’s more sensitive to local differences, Moran’s I to the global pattern.

Your turn. Run geary.test().

The function is geary.test.

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
geary.test(z, lw)$estimate

C = 0.19, far below its expected value of 1: neighbours differ much less than random pairs. Moran’s I and Geary’s C almost always agree on the direction; they differ in what they weigh most.

Trend or dependence?

Some of that resemblance is easy to explain: samples near the cell room are high, and their neighbours are near the cell room too. That’s a trend, a large-scale change in the mean. What’s left after the trend is removed is the small-scale dependence, and the two need different treatment (Lessons 3 to 5).

Your turn. Fit a straight-line trend of log mercury on the distance to the cell room, and compute Moran’s I of the residuals with lm.morantest(), which accounts for the fitted coefficients.

Regress on d_cell.

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
fit <- lm(z ~ d_cell)
print(coef(fit))
lm.morantest(fit, lw)$estimate[1]

Log mercury falls by about 0.0017 per metre (a factor of 5.2 per kilometre), and the residuals’ Moran’s I is 0.620: lower than 0.822, but still very strong. The distance trend explains part of the resemblance, and the rest is genuine small-scale dependence, from the patchy way mercury settled and the soil holds it. That’s what kriging will use.

Your turn. A correlogram shows how the autocorrelation fades with distance: Moran’s I computed with neighbours in distance bands, 0 to 250 m, 250 to 500 m, and so on. Compute it for the trend’s residuals.

Name each value by its band’s upper limit: bands[-1].

r <- residuals(lm(z ~ d_cell))
bands <- seq(0, 2000, by = 250)
I_band <- sapply(1:8, function(i) {
  nb_i <- dnearneigh(xy, bands[i], bands[i + 1])             # neighbours in this band
  moran.test(r, nb2listw(nb_i, style = "W", zero.policy = TRUE), zero.policy = TRUE)$estimate[1]
})
round(setNames(I_band, bands[-1]), 3)

0.666 for pairs up to 250 m apart, 0.437 for 250 to 500 m, 0.209 at 500 to 750 m, then about 0 beyond 1 km (slightly negative at the largest distances, as residuals that average to zero must be). The dependence reaches about a kilometre. The variogram, next lesson, says the same thing in a form that’s easier to model. (zero.policy = TRUE allows samples with no neighbours in a band, which happens at the short distances near the edges.)

Without the trend, Moran’s I of log mercury itself turns strongly negative at large distances: samples 1.5 km apart are typically one near the cell room and one far away, so they differ. That’s what a trend does to a correlogram, and why you look at the residuals.

Local clusters: LISA

Moran’s I is one number for the whole survey. Its local version (Anselin’s LISA, local indicators of spatial association) gives one number per sample, I_i = z_i \sum_j w_{ij} z_j / m_2 (with m_2 = \sum z_i^2 / n), whose average is the global I. A large positive I_i with a high value is a high-high cluster, a hot spot surrounded by hot neighbours; low-low is a cold spot; negative I_i marks an outlier unlike its neighbours.

Your turn. Compute the local Moran’s I with localmoran(), and count the samples that are significant high-high clusters at the 5% level.

Significant at 5%: p < 0.05.

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
loc <- localmoran(z, lw)
p <- loc[, 5]                                            # the p-values
lag <- lag.listw(lw, z)
high_high <- z > mean(z) & lag > mean(z) & p < 0.05
c(high_high = sum(high_high), median_distance = median(d_cell[high_high]))

51 high-high samples, with a median distance of 368 m from the cell room and none farther than 840 m: 46 on the plant site, 3 on farmland and 2 in residential areas. There are also 34 low-low samples, far out. The average of the 200 local I’s is exactly the global I, 0.822.

NoteGo deeper: why local p-values need care

Each local test uses a sample and its neighbours, so neighbouring tests share data and aren’t independent, and there are 200 of them. At the 5% level, about 10 would be “significant” by chance even with no pattern at all. The local p-values are also computed under randomization assumptions that the strong global autocorrelation violates. Anselin (1995) recommended treating them as exploratory, and a correction for multiple testing (Bonferroni, which is very strict here, or a false discovery rate) as the minimum. Even then, a LISA map is a map of where to look, not a set of findings. Luc Anselin, “Local Indicators of Spatial Association: LISA”, Geographical Analysis 27(2), 1995, introduced them.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Fewer false alarms. Adjust the local p-values for multiple testing with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (p.adjust(..., method = "BH")), and count the high-high samples that survive.

method = "BH".

lw <- nb2listw(knn2nb(knearneigh(xy, k = 6)), style = "W")
p_adj <- p.adjust(localmoran(z, lw)[, 5], method = "BH")
lag <- lag.listw(lw, z)
sum(z > mean(z) & lag > mean(z) & p_adj < 0.05)

30 of the 51 survive: the core of the hot spot, around the cell room. The others are on its fringes, where the evidence is weaker. A false discovery rate of 5% means that, of the samples flagged, about 5% are expected to be false alarms, which is a sensible standard for a map of where to look next.

Challenge 2 · Raw or log? In RStudio, compute Moran’s I for the raw mercury values (not the log) with the same weights. It’s 0.79, slightly lower than the log’s 0.82. Which would you report, and why? (Think about what a few very high values do to a statistic built from products.)

Quick quiz

1. What does a row-standardized weights matrix do to the spatial lag?

Rows that sum to 1 turn \sum_j w_{ij} z_j into an average.

2. Moran’s I is near -1/(n-1) when:

That’s its expected value under independence; it’s slightly negative because the values are centred.

3. Geary’s C below 1 means:

Geary’s C measures squared differences, so it’s small when neighbours are alike.

4. Why does a permutation test shuffle the values among the locations?

Each shuffle keeps the values but destroys their arrangement.

5. Moran’s I fell from 0.82 to 0.62 after removing the distance trend. What does that show?

Trend and small-scale dependence both make neighbours alike; models treat them differently.

6. Why are LISA p-values treated as exploratory?

200 tests at 5% give about 10 false alarms even with no pattern.

Wrap-up

You’ve measured the resemblance between neighbours. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Neighbours and weights: six nearest neighbours, row-standardized, so the spatial lag is the neighbours’ average (correlation 0.915 with the values).
  • Moran’s I is 0.822 for log mercury (standard deviate 21.7; no permutation out of 999 came close), and Geary’s C is 0.19.
  • Trend versus dependence: after a distance trend, the residuals’ I is 0.62, and the correlogram shows the dependence fading over about a kilometre.
  • LISA finds 51 high-high samples around the cell room (30 after a false-discovery-rate adjustment): a map of where to look.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Spatial autocorrelation
The resemblance between observations at nearby locations.
Neighbour list
For each observation, the observations counted as its neighbours (k nearest, within a distance, or sharing a border).
Spatial weights matrix
The matrix W of weights w_{ij} given to neighbour j of observation i, often row-standardized.
Spatial lag
The weighted average of an observation’s neighbours, \sum_j w_{ij} z_j.
Moran’s I
A global measure of spatial autocorrelation built from products of centred neighbouring values.
Geary’s C
A global measure built from squared differences between neighbours; below 1 for positive autocorrelation.
Correlogram
Moran’s I (or another autocorrelation) as a function of the distance between pairs.
LISA
Local indicators of spatial association: a local Moran’s I for each observation, to find clusters and outliers.
