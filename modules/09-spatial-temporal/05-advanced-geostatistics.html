Beyond ordinary kriging

Trends, directions and the Gaussian-process view

Put the distance trend into the kriging model (universal kriging), compare it with regression kriging, and see what a trend does far outside the data. Add the wind’s anisotropy and choose its ratio by cross-validation. Then look at geostatistics as a Gaussian process: fit the trend and the covariance together by maximum likelihood with nlme::gls(), and see how much an ordinary regression understates the trend’s uncertainty.

Module 9 · Lesson 5 of 13 Week 137 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 4’s ordinary kriging assumed a constant mean, at least within each neighbourhood. But we know the mean isn’t constant: mercury falls with the distance from the cell room. And we know the dependence isn’t the same in every direction: the wind stretched it toward the north-east. This lesson puts both into the model, and then steps back to see kriging as what it is underneath: prediction from a Gaussian process.

Three upgrades today: a trend, a direction, and a likelihood. Each one changes the map a little, and one of them changes a standard error a lot.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • krige with a trend (universal kriging), and compare it with regression kriging
  • see what a trend does when you predict far from the data
  • add geometric anisotropy, and choose it by cross-validation
  • fit the trend and the covariance together by maximum likelihood, as a Gaussian process
  • see how much an ordinary regression understates uncertainty when the residuals are correlated

Universal kriging

Universal kriging (also called kriging with a trend) models the value as a regression on covariates plus a spatially correlated residual:

Z(s) = \mathbf x(s)^\top \boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon(s), \qquad \varepsilon \text{ with variogram } \gamma .

The kriging weights are now constrained so that the prediction is unbiased for any \boldsymbol\beta, and the variogram is the residuals’ variogram (Lesson 3). In gstat, it’s one change: the formula’s right side.

The setup code reads the data, computes the distance in kilometres, and fits both variograms: m, Lesson 4’s model for log mercury, and mr, Lesson 3’s model for the residuals from the distance trend.

Your turn. First, the trend on its own: an ordinary regression of log mercury on the distance.

Regress on dist_km.

fit <- lm(z ~ dist_km, data = soil)
c(coef(fit), r_squared = summary(fit)$r.squared)

An intercept of 0.83 (2.3 mg/kg at the cell room) and a slope of -1.66 per km: mercury falls by a factor of e^{1.66} = 5.2 per kilometre. The distance alone explains 52% of the variation in log mercury; the residuals carry the rest, and they’re spatially correlated (Lesson 2’s Moran’s I of 0.62).

Your turn. Krige the garden with the trend, using the residuals’ variogram mr. The new location needs the covariate too: garden has its dist_km.

The same trend as the regression: z ~ dist_km.

krige(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, soil, newdata = garden, model = mr, debug.level = 0)

0.0115 with a variance of 0.131, against ordinary kriging’s 0.0135 and 0.136 in Lesson 4: practically the same. The garden is surrounded by samples, so the neighbours tell kriging almost everything, with or without a trend. Trends matter most where the samples are sparse.

Your turn. Cross-validate universal kriging and compare it with ordinary kriging.

Universal kriging uses the residuals’ variogram, mr.

cv_ok <- krige.cv(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, model = m, debug.level = 0)
cv_uk <- krige.cv(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, soil, model = mr, debug.level = 0)
score <- function(cv) c(rmse = sqrt(mean(cv$residual^2)), mean_sq_z = mean(cv$zscore^2))
rbind(ordinary = score(cv_ok), universal = score(cv_uk))

An RMSE of 0.440 for universal kriging against 0.443 for ordinary kriging: a tiny gain. Its variances are a little too small (mean squared z-score 1.10). With 200 well-spread samples, the data already “know” about the trend locally, and ordinary kriging with a nearly linear variogram follows it.

Regression kriging does the same in two steps: fit the trend by ordinary least squares, krige the residuals (with a known mean of zero, simple kriging), and add the two.

Your turn. Predict the garden by regression kriging.

Simple kriging of the residuals, whose mean is 0: beta = 0.

fit <- lm(z ~ dist_km, data = soil)
soil$res <- residuals(fit)
k_res <- krige(res ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, newdata = garden, model = mr, beta = 0, debug.level = 0)
c(trend = unname(predict(fit, garden)), residual = k_res$var1.pred,
  prediction = unname(predict(fit, garden)) + k_res$var1.pred)

The trend says -0.69 (0.50 mg/kg) (the garden is 0.92 km from the cell room), and the kriged residual adds +0.70, because the nearby samples are higher than the trend predicts: that’s the north-east plume. Total: 0.0124, within 0.001 of universal kriging. The difference is that universal kriging estimates the trend accounting for the correlation (by generalized least squares) and includes the trend’s uncertainty in its variance; regression kriging ignores both, which matters when the trend is poorly determined.

Trends far from the data

Inside the data, the trend hardly matters. Outside, it takes over: with no neighbours to correct it, universal kriging returns to the trend, and ordinary kriging to the mean.

Your turn. Predict at a point 4.3 km north-east of the cell room, well outside the survey square, by both methods.

Back-transform uk.

far <- data.frame(x = 485500, y = 5704000)
far$dist_km <- sqrt((far$x - 482300)^2 + (far$y - 5701200)^2) / 1000
ok <- krige(z ~ 1, ~x + y, soil, newdata = far, model = m, debug.level = 0)$var1.pred
uk <- krige(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, soil, newdata = far, model = mr, debug.level = 0)$var1.pred
c(ordinary_mgkg = exp(ok), universal_mgkg = exp(uk))

Ordinary kriging says 0.15 mg/kg, close to the survey’s typical outer values; universal kriging says 0.009 mg/kg, a tenth of the natural background of about 0.08. The straight-line trend in log mercury can’t level off at the background, so beyond the data it keeps falling, to impossibly clean soil. A trend fitted over 2 km says nothing reliable at 4 km; if you must extrapolate, use a trend with the right shape (one that levels off at the background), and treat the result with suspicion anyway.

Anisotropy in kriging

Lesson 3 found that the dependence reaches further toward the north-east. In gstat, geometric anisotropy is anis = c(angle, ratio) in vgm(): the range applies along the angle (clockwise from north), and range × ratio across it. The isotropic fit’s range of 693 m is a compromise between the two directions, so to compare like with like, keep their geometric mean at 693 m: major range 693/\sqrt{r}, minor 693\sqrt{r}.

The variogram fit can’t choose the ratio well from 200 samples, but cross-validation can.

Your turn. Cross-validate universal kriging with ratios 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 1 (isotropic), along 45°.

The long axis points north-east: 45°.

rmse_for <- function(r) {
  ma <- vgm(mr$psill[2], "Exp", mr$range[2] / sqrt(r), mr$psill[1], anis = c(45, r))
  cv <- krige.cv(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, soil, model = ma, debug.level = 0)
  sqrt(mean(cv$residual^2))
}
ratios <- c(0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1)
setNames(sapply(ratios, rmse_for), ratios)

An RMSE of 0.437 with a ratio of 0.3, 0.423 with 0.5, 0.426 with 0.7, and 0.440 isotropic. The best ratio, 0.5, means a range of 980 m along the wind and 490 m across it, and it cuts the RMSE from 0.440 (isotropic) to 0.423, about 4%. A modest gain. With 200 samples, the data already show the ellipse, so the map changes little; anisotropy matters most where samples are sparse, because it decides which far samples count for the gaps between them.

The Gaussian-process view

Behind the variograms and the weights is a simple model: the vector of log mercury values is multivariate normal,

\mathbf z \sim N\big(X\boldsymbol\beta,\; \Sigma(\boldsymbol\theta)\big), \qquad \Sigma_{ij} = \sigma^2 \rho(\lVert s_i - s_j \rVert; \phi) + \tau^2 \,\mathbb 1[i = j],

with a correlation function \rho (here exponential, with range \phi), a partial sill \sigma^2 and a nugget \tau^2. A random function whose values at any set of points are jointly normal is a Gaussian process, and kriging is nothing but the conditional mean of the unknown value given the observed ones (Module 3’s conditional normal distribution). Machine learning uses the same model under the name Gaussian-process regression.

With a likelihood, everything can be estimated at once, by maximum likelihood: the trend \boldsymbol\beta and the covariance parameters \boldsymbol\theta, instead of fitting a variogram to residuals from a trend fitted as if the data were independent. nlme::gls() (Module 3, Lesson 11) does it, with corExp() for the exponential correlation.

Your turn. Fit the Gaussian-process model by maximum likelihood, and read the estimated range and nugget.

Include a nugget: nugget = TRUE.

g <- gls(z ~ dist_km, data = soil, method = "ML",
         correlation = corExp(form = ~ x + y, nugget = TRUE, value = c(500, 0.05)))
c(coef(g), coef(g$modelStruct$corStruct, unconstrained = FALSE), sigma2 = g$sigma^2)

A range of 591 m (the variogram fit had 693), a nugget that’s 0.6% of the total variance of 0.77 (nlme reports the nugget as a proportion), and a different trend: a slope of -1.21 per km instead of the ordinary regression’s -1.66. Maximum likelihood lets the correlated residuals absorb part of what the ordinary regression had attributed to distance: the samples near the cell room are clustered, and a correlated cluster counts for less than as many independent samples.

Your turn. Compare the slope’s standard error from the ordinary regression with the one from the Gaussian-process model.

The standard error is the table’s second column.

fit <- lm(z ~ dist_km, data = soil)
g <- gls(z ~ dist_km, data = soil, method = "ML",
         correlation = corExp(form = ~ x + y, nugget = TRUE, value = c(500, 0.05)))
se <- c(ols = coef(summary(fit))[2, 2], gls = summary(g)$tTable[2, 2])
c(se, ratio = se[["gls"]] / se[["ols"]])

0.11 from the ordinary regression, 0.41 from the spatial model: 3.7 times larger. The ordinary regression treats 200 correlated samples as 200 independent pieces of evidence; there’s much less information in them than that, roughly as much as in 200 / 3.7^2 \approx 15 independent samples. A trend is still clearly there (t = -2.9), but a confidence interval from lm() would be far too narrow. This is the most important practical lesson of spatial statistics for anyone who fits regressions to spatial data: test the residuals for autocorrelation, and if it’s there, model it.

Your turn. Compare the two models’ AIC.

AIC(g).

fit <- lm(z ~ dist_km, data = soil)
g <- gls(z ~ dist_km, data = soil, method = "ML",
         correlation = corExp(form = ~ x + y, nugget = TRUE, value = c(500, 0.05)))
c(independent = AIC(fit), spatial = AIC(g))

483 for the independent model, 295 for the spatial one: a difference of 188, with two extra parameters. The spatial correlation isn’t a refinement; it’s most of the story. (Both are maximum-likelihood fits of the same data, so their AICs are comparable.)

NoteGo deeper: REML, big data, and Bayesian geostatistics

Maximum likelihood underestimates variance parameters slightly, because it ignores the degrees of freedom used by \boldsymbol\beta; restricted maximum likelihood (REML, method = "REML", nlme’s default) corrects that, as in mixed models. Each likelihood evaluation needs the Cholesky factor of the n \times n matrix \Sigma, which costs O(n^3): fine for 200 samples, hopeless for 100,000. Large-data methods replace \Sigma with something cheaper: low-rank basis functions (Lesson 12), sparse precision matrices (the SPDE approach of Lindgren, Rue and Lindström, 2011, implemented in INLA), or nearest-neighbour Gaussian processes (Datta and others, 2016). A Bayesian treatment puts priors on \boldsymbol\theta and averages the predictions over their posterior, which carries the uncertainty about the variogram into the kriging variance, something plug-in kriging ignores. Diggle and Ribeiro’s Model-based Geostatistics (Springer, 2007) is the standard account.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · The final map. Krige the grid with the trend and the best anisotropy, and recompute the areas where the probability of exceeding 1 mg/kg is above 0.9, 0.5 and 0.1. Compare them with Lesson 4’s 0.49, 1.21 and 2.53 km².

The third threshold is 0.1.

grid <- expand.grid(x = seq(480825, 483775, by = 50), y = seq(5699725, 5702675, by = 50))
grid$dist_km <- sqrt((grid$x - 482300)^2 + (grid$y - 5701200)^2) / 1000
best <- vgm(mr$psill[2], "Exp", mr$range[2] / sqrt(0.5), mr$psill[1], anis = c(45, 0.5))
ku <- krige(z ~ dist_km, ~x + y, soil, newdata = grid, model = best, debug.level = 0)
p1 <- 1 - pnorm((0 - ku$var1.pred) / sqrt(pmax(ku$var1.var, 1e-12)))
sapply(c(0.9, 0.5, 0.1), function(p) sum(p1 > p) * 0.0025)

0.48, 1.17 and 2.50 km², within 4% of ordinary kriging’s areas, and the residential area with a probability above 10% hardly changes (94 cells instead of 95). With samples this dense, the refinements sharpen the cross-validation but barely move the map: the decision about where to sample gardens doesn’t depend on the model choice, which is reassuring. On a sparser survey, it would. (pmax() guards against tiny negative variances from rounding, at grid cells that coincide with samples.)

Challenge 2 · The likelihood by hand. In RStudio, write the negative log-likelihood of the Gaussian-process model yourself: for given (\sigma^2, \phi, \tau^2), build \Sigma, compute the generalized least-squares \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}, and evaluate \frac{1}{2}\log\lvert\Sigma\rvert + \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf z - X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf z - X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) with chol(). Minimize it with optim() over the logs of the three parameters, and check that you get gls()’s answer.

Quick quiz

1. Universal kriging differs from ordinary kriging by:

The kriging weights become unbiased for any trend coefficients.

2. Inside the survey, universal kriging barely beat ordinary kriging (0.440 against 0.443). Why?

Trends matter where data are sparse, and they dominate where there are none.

3. Far outside the survey, universal kriging predicted 0.009 mg/kg. What went wrong?

Without neighbours, the prediction is the trend, and this trend doesn’t level off.

4. In anis = c(45, 0.5), what’s the range toward the south-east?

The ratio applies across the main direction.

5. In the Gaussian-process view, the kriging prediction is:

For jointly normal values, the best predictor is the conditional mean.

6. The slope’s standard error was 0.11 with lm() and 0.41 with gls(). Which is right, and why?

Ignoring spatial correlation makes standard errors too small and tests too confident.

Wrap-up

You’ve taken kriging beyond a constant mean. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Universal kriging adds a trend (z ~ dist_km) and uses the residuals’ variogram; regression kriging does it in two steps. Inside the data both match ordinary kriging (RMSE 0.440 against 0.443).
  • Trends extrapolate badly: 4.3 km out, the straight-line trend predicted a tenth of the natural background.
  • Anisotropy along the wind (ratio 0.5: 980 m along, 490 m across) lowered the cross-validated RMSE to 0.423, though with samples this dense the map hardly changed.
  • The Gaussian-process view: fitting trend and covariance together by maximum likelihood changed the slope from -1.66 to -1.21 per km, multiplied its standard error by 3.7, and lowered the AIC by 188.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Universal kriging
Kriging with a mean that’s a linear function of covariates, unbiased for any coefficients.
Regression kriging
Fitting the trend by regression, kriging its residuals, and adding the two.
Geometric anisotropy
A variogram whose range depends on direction, described by an angle and a ratio.
Gaussian process
A random function whose values at any set of locations are jointly normal; geostatistics’ underlying model.
Generalized least squares (GLS)
Regression that accounts for correlated errors through their covariance matrix.
Restricted maximum likelihood (REML)
A likelihood for the covariance parameters that accounts for the estimated trend.
