Dynamic spatio-temporal models

How the lake evolves, and what it looked like under the clouds

Model how the lake’s temperature field evolves from week to week. Reduce it to its three EOF amplitudes, fit a vector autoregression for them with the plant’s load and the wind as inputs, check its stability and its forecasts against persistence and climatology, and then fill the 32 cloudy weeks with a Kalman filter and smoother, with honest uncertainty. Along the way, meet the hierarchical view of spatio-temporal statistics: a data model, a process model and a parameter model.

Module 9 · Lesson 13 of 13 Week 145 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 12 found that three patterns carry the lake’s interesting variation: the wind’s tilt, the cooling-water plume and the shallow bay. Their amplitudes are three time series, with gaps where the clouds hid the lake. Two questions remain, and they’re the questions most environmental monitoring ends with:

  1. How does the field evolve? If we know this week’s lake and next week’s plant load and wind, what will next week’s lake look like?
  2. What happened when we couldn’t see? The permit counts every week, cloudy or not. What did the plume look like in the 32 cloudy weeks, and how sure can we be?

Both need a model of the dynamics: how the state at one time produces the state at the next. That’s a dynamic spatio-temporal model, the last idea of the module, and it brings together Module 6’s autoregressions, Lesson 12’s dimension reduction, and the Kalman filter.

Last lesson of Module 9! It ends with a filter that fills in what the satellite couldn’t see, and tells you how sure it is about every gap.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • think of a spatio-temporal model as a hierarchy: data model, process model, parameter model
  • turn the lake’s images into three EOF amplitudes, with gaps
  • fit a vector autoregression with inputs (the plant’s load and the wind), and check its stability
  • compare its forecasts with persistence and climatology on a test year
  • fill the cloudy weeks with a Kalman filter and smoother, and validate them

The hierarchical view

Wikle, Zammit-Mangion and Cressie organize spatio-temporal statistics as a hierarchy of three models:

  • the data model: how the observations relate to the true process. Here, the satellite measures the lake’s surface temperature with some error, and sometimes not at all (clouds).
  • the process model: how the true field evolves. Here, \boldsymbol\alpha_t = M\boldsymbol\alpha_{t-1} + G\mathbf u_t + \boldsymbol\eta_t: next week’s pattern amplitudes are a linear function of this week’s, plus the effect of the inputs \mathbf u_t (load and wind), plus an innovation \boldsymbol\eta_t.
  • the parameter model: what we assume about M, G and the variances (here, nothing: we estimate them; a Bayesian would put priors on them, as in Module 5).

Working with the EOF amplitudes \boldsymbol\alpha_t instead of the 258 pixels is what makes the process model feasible: a 3 \times 3 matrix M instead of 258 \times 258. This reduced-rank approach is how most large dynamic spatio-temporal models work.

The setup code reads the data, computes Lesson 12’s anomalies and first three EOFs (V, a 258 × 3 matrix), and defines the Kalman filter and smoother used at the end of the lesson.

Multiply the anomalies by the EOFs, V.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3)

alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V

156 weeks, 124 of them clear. The amplitudes’ standard deviations are 2.91, 2.52 and 1.57: these are UD from Lesson 12, the weight of each pattern in each week’s map, and their variances are in the same proportions as the EOFs’ shares of the variance. Projecting onto the EOFs is how a new image (next week’s, or a cloudy week’s partial one) would be reduced too.

A vector autoregression

A first-order vector autoregression, VAR(1) (Module 6, Lesson 12), predicts each amplitude from all three amplitudes of the week before: \boldsymbol\alpha_t = M\boldsymbol\alpha_{t-1} + \boldsymbol\eta_t. It can only be fitted from pairs of consecutive clear weeks. To test it honestly, fit it on 2023 and 2024, and keep 2025 for testing.

Your turn. Find the pairs of consecutive clear weeks, and how many fall in each period.

The comparison is shifted by one week: add 1 to get week t.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1   # week t, with t - 1 clear too
c(pairs = length(pair), training = sum(yr[pair] < 2025), test = sum(yr[pair] == 2025))

100 pairs: 66 for training (2023 and 2024) and 34 for testing (2025). Clouds cost more than their 20% of the weeks: every cloudy week breaks two pairs. That’s the first argument for a method, like the Kalman filter, that doesn’t need complete data.

Your turn. Fit the VAR(1) on the training pairs by least squares, one regression for all three amplitudes, without an intercept (the amplitudes have mean zero).

The predictors are last week’s amplitudes: tr - 1.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
fit <- lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ alpha[tr - 1, ] - 1)
M_var <- t(coef(fit))                                                 # rows: this week; columns: last week
round(M_var, 3)

The diagonal (0.67, 0.74 and 0.71) is each pattern’s persistence from one week to the next; the off-diagonal terms are interactions, mostly small, except that the shallow bay last week (-0.52) feeds into the tilt this week, which is probably the season showing through both. With 66 pairs and 9 coefficients, M is estimated quite roughly.

Your turn. A VAR(1) is stable (its effects die away, rather than growing) if all the eigenvalues of M have modulus less than 1. Check, and compute how many weeks it takes the slowest mode to halve.

The eigenvalues are eigen(M)$values.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
M_var <- t(coef(lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ alpha[tr - 1, ] - 1)))
lambda <- Mod(eigen(M_var)$values)
c(round(lambda, 3), half_life_weeks = log(0.5) / log(max(lambda)))

Moduli of 0.91, 0.61 and 0.60, all below 1: stable. The slowest mode halves in about 7 weeks; the other two in about a week and a half. (Complex eigenvalues, which come in pairs with the same modulus, mean oscillation: a pattern that rotates into another and back.)

Adding the inputs

The lake doesn’t evolve on its own: the plant’s load drives the plume, and the wind drives the tilt. Add them to the process model: \boldsymbol\alpha_t = M\boldsymbol\alpha_{t-1} + G\mathbf u_t + \boldsymbol\eta_t. (This is a VAR with exogenous inputs, a VARX.)

Your turn. Fit it, and look at G, the effects of this week’s load and wind.

This week’s inputs: u[tr, ].

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
fit <- lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ cbind(alpha[tr - 1, ], u[tr, ]) - 1)
B <- t(coef(fit)); M <- B[, 1:3]; G <- B[, 4:5]
round(G, 3)

A west wind of 1 m/s adds 0.69 to amplitude 1 (the tilt), and full load changes all three amplitudes, by -1.29, -0.26 and -0.49 respectively, not just the plume’s. That’s less tidy than “load drives the plume, wind drives the tilt”, because the EOFs are statistical patterns (they mix the physical modes a little, and their signs are arbitrary), and because with M in the model, part of the load’s effect is already carried by last week’s amplitudes. The forecasts are the better test.

Your turn. Forecast each 2025 test week from the previous week, with four methods, and compare their errors on the lake’s pixels (reconstructing each forecast map from the three EOFs): the VARX, the VAR without inputs, persistence (next week = this week) and climatology (anomaly zero, the average lake).

Reconstruct the maps with the EOFs: t(V).

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]; te <- pair[yr[pair] == 2025]
B <- t(coef(lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ cbind(alpha[tr - 1, ], u[tr, ]) - 1)))
M_var <- t(coef(lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ alpha[tr - 1, ] - 1)))
fc <- list(varx = cbind(alpha[te - 1, ], u[te, ]) %*% t(B),
           var = alpha[te - 1, ] %*% t(M_var),
           persistence = alpha[te - 1, ],
           climatology = matrix(0, length(te), 3))
truth <- A[match(te, obs), ]                                         # the observed anomaly maps
sapply(fc, function(f) sqrt(mean((f %*% t(V) - truth)^2)))      # RMSE over pixels, degrees C

On the 34 test weeks of 2025: 0.309 °C for the VARX, 0.325 for the VAR, 0.337 for persistence and 0.372 for climatology. Each step adds skill: dynamics beat the average lake, and knowing next week’s load and wind beats dynamics alone. Remember that even a perfect forecast of the three amplitudes would leave an error of about 0.27 °C (Lesson 12’s reconstruction error with three EOFs): the small-scale noise isn’t predictable. So the VARX captures about 60% of the predictable part that climatology misses. (In real use, the load is planned and the wind forecast, so next week’s inputs are available, with some error.)

The Kalman filter

For the cloudy weeks, the Kalman filter combines the process model with whatever data exist. At each week, it carries an estimate \mathbf a_t of the state and its covariance P_t:

  1. Predict from last week: \mathbf a_{t|t-1} = M\mathbf a_{t-1} + G\mathbf u_t, with covariance P_{t|t-1} = MP_{t-1}M^\top + Q (the process model’s uncertainty, Q, adds to what was already uncertain).
  2. Update if there’s an image: \mathbf a_t = \mathbf a_{t|t-1} + K_t(\mathbf y_t - \mathbf a_{t|t-1}), with the Kalman gain K_t = P_{t|t-1}(P_{t|t-1} + R)^{-1}, where R is the observation error’s covariance (the data model). The gain weighs the prediction against the observation by their uncertainties, exactly like Module 5’s posterior mean weighs a prior against data.

In a cloudy week, there’s no update: the estimate is the prediction, and its uncertainty grows. The setup code’s kf() does both steps; here R is tiny (the projected amplitudes are measured almost without error), so a clear week’s estimate is essentially its observation.

Your turn. Run the filter with the VARX’s matrices, and compare the estimated plume amplitude (the second) in the turnaround of April 2024, when the plant was shut down and one week was cloudy.

The plume is the second amplitude: element [2, 2] of each covariance matrix.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
fit <- lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ cbind(alpha[tr - 1, ], u[tr, ]) - 1)
B <- t(coef(fit)); M <- B[, 1:3]; G <- B[, 4:5]; Q <- cov(residuals(fit))
f <- kf(alpha, u, M, G, Q, R = diag(1e-4, 3))
turn <- which(plant$week >= "2024-04-08" & plant$week <= "2024-05-06")
rbind(observed = alpha[turn, 2], filtered = f$a[turn, 2], se = sqrt(sapply(turn, function(t) f$P[[t]][2, 2])))

In the four clear weeks, the filter’s estimate is the observation, with a tiny standard error (0.01). The plume amplitude climbs from 3.0 to 7.8; with this EOF’s sign, a positive value means a cooler outfall area, so that’s the plume fading as the plant shuts down. In the cloudy week of 29 April, the filter has only its prediction from the week before: 5.55, with a standard error of 1.62, below the clear weeks on either side (7.45 and 7.78). The filter can’t know that the following week was 7.78, and the model’s estimate of the shutdown’s effect falls short. Its standard error says how little it knows: a 95% interval of about \pm 3.2.

Your turn. Validate: hide 15 clear weeks of 2025, fill them with the filter, and compare with linear interpolation between the neighbouring clear weeks, on the pixels.

Interpolate at the hidden weeks, hid.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
fit <- lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ cbind(alpha[tr - 1, ], u[tr, ]) - 1)
B <- t(coef(fit)); M <- B[, 1:3]; G <- B[, 4:5]; Q <- cov(residuals(fit))
set.seed(9131)
hid <- sample(which(!is.na(alpha[, 1]) & yr == 2025), 15)
a2 <- alpha; a2[hid, ] <- NA
f <- kf(a2, u, M, G, Q, R = diag(1e-4, 3))
lin <- sapply(1:3, function(j) approx(seq_len(nrow(a2)), a2[, j], xout = hid, rule = 2)$y)
truth <- A[match(hid, obs), ]
c(filter = sqrt(mean((f$a[hid, ] %*% t(V) - truth)^2)), linear = sqrt(mean((lin %*% t(V) - truth)^2)),
  climatology = sqrt(mean(truth^2)))

0.308 °C for the filter, 0.312 for linear interpolation and 0.358 for the average lake. The filter only edges out interpolation, and that’s not a fair contest: the filter only uses the weeks before each gap, while interpolation uses the week after too. The smoother, next, fixes that.

The Kalman smoother (Rauch, Tung and Striebel, 1965) runs backwards after the filter, revising each week’s estimate with everything that happened afterwards. For filling past gaps, it’s the right tool; the filter is for real time.

Your turn. Smooth the filtered estimates with ks(), and compare again.

Smooth the filter’s output, f.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
fit <- lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ cbind(alpha[tr - 1, ], u[tr, ]) - 1)
B <- t(coef(fit)); M <- B[, 1:3]; G <- B[, 4:5]; Q <- cov(residuals(fit))
set.seed(9131)
hid <- sample(which(!is.na(alpha[, 1]) & yr == 2025), 15)
a2 <- alpha; a2[hid, ] <- NA
f <- kf(a2, u, M, G, Q, R = diag(1e-4, 3))
s <- ks(f, u, M, G, Q)
lin <- sapply(1:3, function(j) approx(seq_len(nrow(a2)), a2[, j], xout = hid, rule = 2)$y)
truth <- A[match(hid, obs), ]
c(smoother = sqrt(mean((s$a[hid, ] %*% t(V) - truth)^2)), linear = sqrt(mean((lin %*% t(V) - truth)^2)))

0.293 °C for the smoother against 0.312 for interpolation: with information from both sides of each gap, plus the model’s knowledge of what the load and the wind were doing, the smoother wins clearly, and it’s within 0.02 °C of the best any three-EOF reconstruction could do (about 0.27). By amplitude, it’s much better for the tilt (RMSE 0.94 against 1.97: the wind is known even when the lake isn’t seen) and for the shallow bay (0.90 against 1.01), and slightly worse for the plume (1.31 against 1.21), whose week-to-week changes are smooth enough for interpolation.

NoteGo deeper: integro-difference equations and physical dynamics

Fitting M freely works for three amplitudes, but not for a full field: a 258 \times 258 matrix has 66,564 entries. Physical dynamics give it structure. An integro-difference equation (IDE) model writes the next field as a smoothed, shifted version of the current one, Y_t(s) = \int k(s, r; \boldsymbol\theta) Y_{t-1}(r)\,dr + \eta_t(s), where the kernel k (often a Gaussian whose centre is offset by a velocity) describes diffusion and advection with a handful of parameters \boldsymbol\theta. With a spatially varying velocity, it can represent a plume drifting with the currents. The IDE package fits such models, and with basis functions (Lesson 12) the integral becomes a small matrix. The models connect directly to the advection-diffusion equations of transport phenomena. Wikle, Zammit-Mangion and Cressie’s Spatio-Temporal Statistics with R, chapter 5, develops them, with R code.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Calibration. Are the smoother’s standard errors honest? For the 15 hidden weeks, compute the mean squared z-score of each amplitude.

Divide by the standard errors, se.

alpha <- matrix(NA, nrow(plant), 3); obs <- match(rownames(Z), plant$week); alpha[obs, ] <- A %*% V
pair <- which(!is.na(alpha[-1, 1]) & !is.na(alpha[-nrow(alpha), 1])) + 1
tr <- pair[yr[pair] < 2025]
fit <- lm(alpha[tr, ] ~ cbind(alpha[tr - 1, ], u[tr, ]) - 1)
B <- t(coef(fit)); M <- B[, 1:3]; G <- B[, 4:5]; Q <- cov(residuals(fit))
set.seed(9131)
hid <- sample(which(!is.na(alpha[, 1]) & yr == 2025), 15)
a2 <- alpha; a2[hid, ] <- NA
s <- ks(kf(a2, u, M, G, Q, R = diag(1e-4, 3)), u, M, G, Q)
se <- t(sapply(hid, function(t) sqrt(diag(s$P[[t]]))))
round(colMeans(((s$a[hid, ] - alpha[hid, ]) / se)^2), 2)

0.45, 0.85 and 0.84: all below 1, so the smoother’s uncertainty is, if anything, a little pessimistic (more so for the tilt, which the known wind predicts better than the model’s Q assumes). With only 15 hidden weeks, these are rough. Unlike Lesson 11’s space-time kriging, the dynamic model’s error bands can be reported as they are. For Hana’s permit report, that means a plume estimate for every one of the 156 weeks, with an honest band, and the cloudy weeks no longer a gap in the record.

Challenge 2 · Partly cloudy. In real satellite data, clouds often cover only part of the lake. In RStudio, hide a random half of the pixels in some weeks, and change the data model: instead of observing \boldsymbol\alpha_t directly, observe the visible pixels, \mathbf y_t = V_{\text{vis}}\boldsymbol\alpha_t + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t, with V_{\text{vis}} the EOFs’ rows for those pixels. The Kalman update becomes K_t = P_{t|t-1}H^\top(HP_{t|t-1}H^\top + R)^{-1} with H = V_{\text{vis}}. How well does it reconstruct the hidden half?

Wrap-up

You’ve built a dynamic model of a spatio-temporal field. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The hierarchy: a data model (the satellite, with clouds), a process model (how the field evolves) and a parameter model.
  • Reduced-rank dynamics: three EOF amplitudes, 124 clear weeks of 156, only 100 consecutive pairs.
  • A VAR(1) is stable (eigenvalue moduli 0.91, 0.61, 0.60); with the load and the wind as inputs, it forecasts next week’s lake to 0.309 °C, against 0.337 for persistence and 0.372 for climatology.
  • The Kalman filter and smoother fill the cloudy weeks; on 15 hidden weeks, the smoother’s error was 0.293 °C, against 0.312 for interpolation, with slightly conservative standard errors.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Dynamic spatio-temporal model
A model of how a spatial field at one time produces the field at the next.
Data, process and parameter models
The three levels of a hierarchical spatio-temporal model: measurement, evolution, and parameters.
Reduced-rank dynamics
Modelling the evolution of a few basis-function or EOF amplitudes instead of the whole field.
Vector autoregression with inputs (VARX)
\boldsymbol\alpha_t = M\boldsymbol\alpha_{t-1} + G\mathbf u_t + \boldsymbol\eta_t.
Persistence and climatology forecasts
The baselines: next time equals this time, or next time equals the average.
Kalman filter
A recursive estimator of a state-space model’s state, alternating prediction and update.
Kalman gain
The weight given to a new observation relative to the prediction.
Kalman (Rauch-Tung-Striebel) smoother
A backward pass that revises the filter’s estimates using later observations.
Integro-difference equation (IDE)
A process model in which the next field is an integral (a kernel smoothing) of the current one, representing diffusion and advection.
