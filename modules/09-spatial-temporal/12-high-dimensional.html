High-dimensional spatio-temporal data

258 pixels, 124 weeks, three stories

Satellite images of the reservoir that takes the plant’s cooling water: 258 pixels every week for three years. Too many numbers to model one by one. Arrange them as a space-time matrix, remove the seasonal cycle, and find the few patterns that carry most of the variation with empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs), computed by the singular value decomposition. Read the patterns as physics (a wind-driven tilt, the cooling-water plume, a shallow bay), check them against the plant’s load and the wind, and represent a single image with a handful of basis functions.

Module 9 · Lesson 12 of 13 Week 144 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The plant draws its cooling water from the reservoir to its north-west, and returns it about 8 °C warmer through an outfall on the south shore. Its environmental permit limits how much the lake may warm. The regulator’s satellite team sends Hana a thermal image of the lake’s surface every week that the sky is clear: 258 pixels of 250 × 250 m, for 2023 to 2025.

That’s 32,000 numbers, and a model for each pixel would miss the point: the pixels move together. This lesson finds the few patterns that carry most of the variation. It’s Module 7’s principal components analysis, applied to a space-time matrix, which climate scientists call empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs).

A lake has 258 pixels but only a few things happening in it: the sun, the wind, the plant. Today you’ll let the data tell you what they are.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • arrange gridded data as a space-time matrix, and remove the seasonal cycle
  • compute EOFs and their time series with the singular value decomposition
  • decide how many EOFs matter, and interpret them as physical processes
  • check the interpretation against the plant’s load and the wind
  • reconstruct the field from a few EOFs, and represent one image with basis functions

The space-time matrix

lake_temperature.csv has one row per pixel per cloud-free week: cell, x, y (UTM metres), week, temp_c. plant_weekly.csv has, for every week, the plant’s load (as a fraction of full load) and the east-west wind (m/s, positive from the west).

The setup code reads both.

Your turn. Make the matrix of weeks × pixels, and count the cloudy weeks (weeks with no image).

Columns by cell.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
c(weeks = nrow(Z), pixels = ncol(Z), missing_values = sum(is.na(Z)), cloudy_weeks = nrow(plant) - nrow(Z))

124 clear weeks × 258 pixels, with no missing values inside the matrix: a cloudy week has no image at all, so 32 of the 156 weeks are simply absent. This lesson uses the 124 complete weeks; Lesson 13 fills in the cloudy ones.

Your turn. The seasonal cycle dwarfs everything else. Compute the lake’s mean temperature each week, its range, and how much of the variation around each pixel’s own mean is left after removing it.

Centre the columns of A.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
lake_mean <- rowMeans(Z)                                    # one value per week
A <- sweep(Z, 1, lake_mean)                                 # remove each week's lake mean
A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))                               # and each pixel's average anomaly
c(range(lake_mean), share_left = sum(A^2) / sum(sweep(Z, 2, colMeans(Z))^2))

The lake’s weekly mean runs from 4.3 °C in winter to 22.7 °C in summer, and removing it leaves only 0.34% of the variation: almost everything is the whole lake warming and cooling together. That 0.34% is where the plant, the wind and the lake’s shape show up. Without removing the common signal first, the first EOF would just be “the season”, and the interesting patterns would be squeezed into the rest.

Empirical orthogonal functions

The singular value decomposition writes any matrix as A = U D V^\top: V’s columns are orthonormal patterns over the pixels, the EOFs; U D’s columns are their amplitudes over time, the principal component (PC) time series; and the squared singular values d_k^2, divided by their sum, are the shares of the variance each pattern carries. It’s PCA (Module 7) with the weeks as observations and the pixels as variables:

a_{t,s} = \sum_k d_k \, u_{t,k} \, v_{s,k},

each week’s anomaly map is a weighted sum of the same few maps v_{\cdot,k}, with weights d_k u_{t,k} that change from week to week.

Your turn. Compute the SVD of the anomaly matrix, and the share of variance of the first six EOFs.

The variance is the singular value squared: ^2.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
A <- sweep(Z, 1, rowMeans(Z)); A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))
sv <- svd(A)
share <- sv$d^2 / sum(sv$d^2)
round(share[1:6], 3)

23.4%, 17.5% and 6.8% for the first three EOFs, then 4.2%, 2.9%, 2.7% and a long, slowly falling tail. Three patterns stand out; the rest look like noise: small-scale, short-lived variations that no single pattern describes.

Your turn. How many EOFs are needed for half the variance? And how much do the first three carry together?

Half: 0.5.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
A <- sweep(Z, 1, rowMeans(Z)); A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))
sv <- svd(A)
cum <- cumsum(sv$d^2) / sum(sv$d^2)
c(first_three = cum[3], needed_for_half = which(cum >= 0.5)[1])

The first three carry 47.6%, and 4 EOFs are needed for half. Unlike Module 7’s sensors, where three components carried 87%, a field like this has a lot of small-scale noise, spread over many dimensions. The usual ways to decide how many EOFs to keep are the same as for PCA: the scree plot’s elbow (here after 3), or North’s rule of thumb, which flags EOFs whose sampling errors overlap their neighbours’ and so can’t be interpreted separately.

What the patterns mean

An EOF is a statistical pattern, not a physical one, and its sign is arbitrary (the SVD can flip u_k and v_k together). Interpreting it means comparing its map with what we know about the lake, and its time series with what we know about the forcing.

Your turn. EOF 2 looks like a patch on the south shore. Correlate its pattern with a plume-shaped function of the distance from the outfall, and find its strongest pixel.

EOF 2 is column 2 of sv$v.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
A <- sweep(Z, 1, rowMeans(Z)); A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))
sv <- svd(A)
cc <- cells[match(colnames(Z), cells$cell), ]
outfall <- c(mean(range(cc$x)), mean(range(cc$y)) - 1500)     # middle of the south shore
plume_shape <- exp(-sqrt((cc$x - outfall[1])^2 + (cc$y - outfall[2])^2) / 1200)
c(correlation = cor(sv$v[, 2], plume_shape), strongest_x = cc$x[which.max(abs(sv$v[, 2]))],
  strongest_y = cc$y[which.max(abs(sv$v[, 2]))])

A correlation of -0.94 (the sign is arbitrary: this EOF came out negative at the outfall), and the strongest pixel is at (477125, 5707125), on the south shore next to the outfall. EOF 2 is the cooling-water plume: warm water spreading from the outfall over about a kilometre and a half.

Your turn. If EOF 2 is the plume, its time series should follow the plant’s load. Correlate them.

Match on the week column.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
A <- sweep(Z, 1, rowMeans(Z)); A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))
sv <- svd(A)
pc <- sv$u %*% diag(sv$d)                                     # PC time series
p <- plant[match(rownames(Z), plant$week), ]                 # the same weeks
c(cor_load = cor(pc[, 2], p$load), r_squared = summary(lm(pc[, 2] ~ p$load))$r.squared)

r = -0.80: the load explains 65% of the plume’s week-to-week variation (the negative sign matches the negative pattern; together, more load means a warmer plume). In April 2024, during the plant’s four-week turnaround, the plume disappears from the images. The rest of PC 2’s variation reflects the plume’s one-week memory: the water warmed last week is still there.

Your turn. EOF 1 looked like a west-east gradient. Correlate its pattern with the pixels’ x coordinate, and its time series with the east-west wind.

The wind is wind_ew_ms.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
A <- sweep(Z, 1, rowMeans(Z)); A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))
sv <- svd(A)
cc <- cells[match(colnames(Z), cells$cell), ]
p <- plant[match(rownames(Z), plant$week), ]
c(pattern_vs_x = cor(sv$v[, 1], cc$x), series_vs_wind = cor(sv$u[, 1], p$wind_ew_ms))

The pattern correlates -0.94 with the x coordinate, and its time series +0.74 with the east-west wind: when the wind blows from the west, it pushes warm surface water to the east end and cold water wells up at the west end, tilting the lake’s temperature. (Its time series also correlates -0.49 with the season, because in this period the west wind happened to be stronger in winter; EOFs can mix things that happen at the same time.) EOF 3, a patch on the north shore that’s strong in summer, is a shallow bay that warms faster than the deep lake.

Dimension reduction

Your turn. Reconstruct the anomalies from the first k EOFs, for k = 1, 2, 3, 5 and 10, and compute the root mean squared reconstruction error.

Rebuild from k EOFs.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
A <- sweep(Z, 1, rowMeans(Z)); A <- sweep(A, 2, colMeans(A))
sv <- svd(A)
rebuild <- function(k) sv$u[, 1:k, drop = FALSE] %*% diag(sv$d[1:k], k) %*% t(sv$v[, 1:k, drop = FALSE])
c(none = sqrt(mean(A^2)), sapply(c(k1 = 1, k2 = 2, k3 = 3, k5 = 5, k10 = 10), function(k) sqrt(mean((A - rebuild(k))^2))))

The anomalies have an RMS of 0.37 °C. Three EOFs bring the error down to 0.27 °C, ten to 0.22. So 124 \times 258 = 32{,}000 numbers are summarized, to within about 0.27 °C, by 3 maps and 3 time series: 3 \times 258 + 3 \times 124 = 1{,}146 numbers. That’s the point of dimension reduction for spatio-temporal data: models (Lesson 13) can work on 3 time series instead of 258.

EOFs are learned from the data. The other way to reduce dimension is to fix the patterns in advance: basis functions, such as Gaussian bumps \phi_j(s) = \exp(-\lVert s - c_j\rVert^2 / 2\sigma^2) centred on a grid of points c_j. A field is then approximated by \sum_j \alpha_j \phi_j(s), a regression on the bumps. Basis functions work for any location (not just the pixels), handle irregular data, and are how large-data spatial models (fixed rank kriging, the FRK package) stay fast.

Your turn. Represent one image, 15 July 2024, with grids of 8, 18, 32 and 72 Gaussian bumps, and compare their leave-one-out errors (for a linear regression, \text{residual}_i / (1 - h_{ii}) gives the leave-one-out residuals without refitting).

The leverages of fit.

Z <- tapply(lake$temp_c, list(lake$week, lake$cell), mean)
cc <- cells[match(colnames(Z), cells$cell), ]
z <- Z["2024-07-15", ]
loo_rmse <- function(n) {
  ctr <- expand.grid(cx = seq(min(cc$x), max(cc$x), length.out = n),
                     cy = seq(min(cc$y), max(cc$y), length.out = max(2, round(n / 2))))
  s <- 1.5 * (max(cc$x) - min(cc$x)) / n                      # bump width
  B <- sapply(seq_len(nrow(ctr)), function(j) exp(-((cc$x - ctr$cx[j])^2 + (cc$y - ctr$cy[j])^2) / (2 * s^2)))
  fit <- lm(z ~ B)
  sqrt(mean((residuals(fit) / (1 - hatvalues(fit)))^2))
}
sapply(c(b8 = 4, b18 = 6, b32 = 8, b72 = 12), loo_rmse)

Leave-one-out errors of 0.31 °C with 8 bumps, 0.25 with 18, 0.23 with 32, and back up to 0.28 with 72, which start to fit the noise. 32 bumps represent the 258 pixels well (the image’s SD is 0.78 °C): the usual bias-variance trade-off, with the number of basis functions as the complexity knob. Unlike EOFs, these bumps know nothing about lakes; they just impose smoothness.

NoteGo deeper: EOFs, Karhunen-Loève, and their limits

EOFs are the empirical version of the Karhunen-Loève expansion: any random field with a finite variance can be written as \sum_k \sqrt{\lambda_k}\,\xi_k(t)\,\phi_k(s), with uncorrelated coefficients \xi_k and orthonormal eigenfunctions \phi_k of the covariance, ordered by eigenvalue \lambda_k; it’s the most efficient expansion, in the sense of mean squared error, for any number of terms. Their limits: they need complete data (or an EM-style iteration to fill the gaps); they’re orthogonal by construction, so physical modes that aren’t orthogonal get mixed (EOF 1’s touch of season); and their sign and, when eigenvalues are close, their order are arbitrary. Rotated EOFs (varimax, as in factor analysis) and extended EOFs (patterns in space and time lag) address some of this. Hannachi, Jolliffe and Stephenson, “Empirical Orthogonal Functions and Related Techniques in Atmospheric Science: A Review”, International Journal of Climatology 27, 2007, is a thorough guide.

Climate scientists say EOF, statisticians PCA, engineers proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), signal processors the Karhunen-Loève transform; it’s the same computation.

Wrap-up

You’ve reduced a big spatio-temporal dataset to a few patterns. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The space-time matrix: 124 clear weeks × 258 pixels; the lake-wide seasonal cycle is 99.7% of the variation, and is removed first.
  • EOFs by SVD: 23.4%, 17.5% and 6.8% for the first three; their maps are V, their time series UD.
  • Interpretation: EOF 1 is the wind’s tilt (time series r = 0.74 with the east-west wind), EOF 2 the cooling-water plume (r = -0.80 with the load, map peaked at the outfall), EOF 3 the shallow north bay.
  • Dimension reduction: three EOFs reconstruct the anomalies to 0.27 °C (from 0.37), with 1,146 numbers instead of 32,000.
  • Basis functions: 32 Gaussian bumps represent one image best, by leave-one-out cross-validation.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Space-time data matrix
A matrix with one row per time and one column per location.
Empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs)
The principal component patterns of a space-time matrix: orthonormal maps ordered by the variance they carry.
Principal component time series
The amplitude of each EOF over time.
Singular value decomposition (SVD)
A = UDV^\top, with orthonormal U and V and non-negative singular values in D.
Dimension reduction
Representing many variables (pixels) by a few derived ones (EOF amplitudes or basis coefficients).
Basis functions
Fixed functions (such as Gaussian bumps on a grid) whose weighted sum represents a field.
Karhunen-Loève expansion
The theoretical, optimal orthogonal expansion of a random field, of which EOFs are the empirical version.
