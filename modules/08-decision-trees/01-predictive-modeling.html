Predictive modeling in practice

From a business question to an honest model

Start Module 8 with a business problem: a specialty chemicals supplier wants to know which customers won’t renew their contracts. Turn that into a target variable, check which inputs would be known in time, split the accounts into training, validation and test sets, fit a first model, and judge it the way the business will: how many of the leavers does it find if we call the top tenth of the list?

Module 8 · Lesson 1 of 13 Week 119 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Module 7 was about methods. Module 8 follows STAT 585, a course in applied analytics: using those methods, and a few new ones, to answer business questions, with decision trees at the center. Trees are the workhorse of business analytics because a manager can read one, and because, combined into forests and boosted ensembles, they’re among the most accurate models there are for tables of data.

The plant’s parent company sells specialty chemicals (coatings resins, plastic additives, adhesives, process chemicals) to about 4,000 industrial customers on annual supply contracts. Each year, about one customer in five doesn’t renew. Lena, the commercial director, has a team of technical sales engineers who can visit an account before its renewal, sort out its problems, and often keep it. But there are 4,000 accounts and 15 engineers. Which accounts should they visit?

customer_churn.csv has last year’s 4,000 accounts, with what was known about each one three months before its renewal date, and whether it renewed:

In this lesson you’ll:

The question and the target

A predictive model needs three things from the business before any data is touched:

  1. The decision it supports. Here: which accounts get a visit before renewal.
  2. The target: what exactly is being predicted, and when. Here: churned, “didn’t renew”, predicted three months before the renewal date, when there’s still time to visit.
  3. The unit: one row per account per renewal.

The setup code reads the data (as factors, which tree functions like), and drops the account numbers, which identify the rows but carry no information.

Your turn. Find the share of accounts that churned.

A churned account has churned == "yes".

mean(churn$churned == "yes")

20.3%: 813 of the 4,000 accounts didn’t renew. That’s the base rate, and every model will be compared with it. A list of accounts chosen at random would contain 20.3% leavers; a good model should do much better at the top of its list.

Your turn. Find the churn rate in each segment, sorted from lowest to highest.

Group by churn$segment.

round(sort(tapply(churn$churned == "yes", churn$segment, mean)), 3)

From 12.8% in Pharma and 15.0% in Food to 26.3% in Mining. Pharmaceutical customers rarely switch suppliers, because every change of raw material means requalifying the product with the regulators; mining customers buy flotation chemicals on price. A segment’s rate is a useful first fact, but within each segment there are accounts at very different risk, and that’s what the model has to find.

Inputs that exist in time

A model can only use what’s known when it’s used. Every input here was taken from the records three months before each renewal. That’s the most important check in predictive modeling, and the easiest to get wrong: a column like “number of retention calls made” or “final invoice value” would make a model look brilliant, and it couldn’t be used, because it’s recorded after the fact. Module 7’s project had one such column; this module’s project has one too.

Some inputs are known but awkward. satisfaction is missing for 31% of accounts, because those customers didn’t answer the survey, and Lesson 8 shows that the missingness itself is informative. sales_rep has 60 levels, and Lesson 8 shows what that does to trees. For now, the first models leave both out.

Training, validation and test

STAT 585’s way of working, which comes from SAS Enterprise Miner and is standard in business analytics, splits the data three ways:

  • a training set (here 50%), to fit the models;
  • a validation set (25%), to compare the models, choose their settings and pick a cut-off;
  • a test set (25%), set aside and used once, at the very end, for an honest estimate of the chosen model’s performance.

With thousands of rows, a fixed validation set is simpler than cross-validation, and with business data it has another advantage: everyone can see exactly which accounts each model was judged on.

Your turn. Assign each account at random to training (50%), validation (25%) or test (25%), and find the size and the churn rate of each part.

The test set gets the last 25%: 0.25.

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
rbind(n = table(part), churn_rate = round(tapply(churn$churned == "yes", part, mean), 4))

1,962 accounts for training, 1,030 for validation and 1,008 for testing, with churn rates of 19.8%, 21.5% and 20.2%. Assigning each account independently gives sizes and rates that wander a little around the targets. This module’s lessons all use this split, so their results can be compared.

Your turn. A stratified split assigns the leavers and the stayers separately, so that every part gets the same churn rate. Do it, by giving each class’s accounts a shuffled sequence of “train”, “train”, “valid”, “test”.

Make as many labels as there are accounts in the class: length(i).

set.seed(8012)
strat <- character(nrow(churn))
for (cl in c("no", "yes")) {
  i <- which(churn$churned == cl)
  strat[i] <- sample(rep_len(c("train", "train", "valid", "test"), length(i)))
}
rbind(n = table(strat), churn_rate = round(tapply(churn$churned == "yes", strat, mean), 4))

2,001, 1,000 and 999 accounts, each with a churn rate of 20.3%. Stratifying matters most when the target is rare (a 1% fraud rate can leave a small validation set with almost no frauds). At 20% and 4,000 rows, the simple split is fine, and the lessons keep it.

A baseline and a first model

The rest of the lesson works with the random split: train and valid come from part exactly as in your exercise.

Your turn. What’s the validation accuracy of the “model” that predicts that nobody churns?

“Nobody churns” is right for every account with churned == "no".

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
valid <- churn[part == "valid", ]
mean(valid$churned == "no")

78.5% accurate, and useless: it never finds a leaver. As in Module 7, accuracy is the wrong yardstick here. What Lena needs is a ranking of the accounts, so that the engineers’ visits go where they matter most.

Your turn. Fit a logistic regression with five inputs on the training set, and find its AUC on the training and the validation sets.

Fit on the training set, train.

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
train <- churn[part == "train", ]; valid <- churn[part == "valid", ]
m1 <- glm(churned ~ late_deliveries + volume_change_pct + price_index + tech_service + account_years,
          family = binomial, data = train)
c(train = auc(fitted(m1), train$churned), valid = auc(predict(m1, valid, type = "response"), valid$churned))

0.787 on the training set and 0.766 on validation. The validation AUC is the honest one: the model has never seen those accounts. Every input pulls in the expected direction: more late deliveries, a falling volume, a higher price and a shorter relationship raise the risk, and a technical service contract lowers it.

Your turn. Now throw everything in: all 13 inputs, with the missing satisfaction scores filled in with 7, a typical score. Compare the training and validation AUCs.

Predict the validation set, valid.

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
filled <- churn
filled$satisfaction[is.na(filled$satisfaction)] <- 7
train <- filled[part == "train", ]; valid <- filled[part == "valid", ]
m_all <- glm(churned ~ ., family = binomial, data = train)
c(n_coef = length(coef(m_all)), train = auc(fitted(m_all), train$churned),
  valid = auc(predict(m_all, valid, type = "response"), valid$churned))

81 coefficients, 59 of them for the sales reps. The training AUC rises to 0.829, and the validation AUC falls, to 0.758, below the five-input model’s 0.766. Without the sales reps, the same model gets 0.807 and 0.783: the other inputs help, and the 59 rep coefficients mostly fit noise. The gap between training and validation is how overfitting shows up in practice, and it’s why the validation set, not the training set, chooses the model.

What the business sees: lift

The engineers can visit perhaps 10% of the accounts before each renewal round. So the question isn’t “how good is the AUC?” but “if we visit the 10% of accounts with the highest scores, how many of them are real risks?” The lift of the top decile is its churn rate divided by the base rate: how many times better than picking at random.

Your turn. Score the validation accounts with the five-input model, and find the churn rate and the lift in the top 10%.

The top 10% are the scores at or above the 90th percentile: quantile(score, 0.9).

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
train <- churn[part == "train", ]; valid <- churn[part == "valid", ]
m1 <- glm(churned ~ late_deliveries + volume_change_pct + price_index + tech_service + account_years,
          family = binomial, data = train)
score <- predict(m1, valid, type = "response")
top <- score >= quantile(score, 0.9)
rate_top <- mean(valid$churned[top] == "yes")
c(n_top = sum(top), churn_rate_top = rate_top, lift = rate_top / mean(valid$churned == "yes"))

Of the 103 highest-scored validation accounts, 66% churned: a lift of 3.1. Two of every three visits would go to an account that’s about to leave, against one in five at random. That’s a sentence Lena can take to the sales meeting. Lesson 11 turns lift into gains charts and profit.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Capture. If the engineers could visit the top 20% of the list, what share of all the validation leavers would they reach?

Divide by all the validation leavers: valid$churned == "yes".

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
train <- churn[part == "train", ]; valid <- churn[part == "valid", ]
m1 <- glm(churned ~ late_deliveries + volume_change_pct + price_index + tech_service + account_years,
          family = binomial, data = train)
score <- predict(m1, valid, type = "response")
top20 <- score >= quantile(score, 0.8)
sum(valid$churned[top20] == "yes") / sum(valid$churned == "yes")

50%: visiting a fifth of the accounts reaches half of the leavers. That’s called the capture rate (or the gain) at 20%. The other half are spread thinly over the remaining 80% of the list, where visits would mostly be wasted. Lesson 11’s gains chart draws this for every depth of the list.

Challenge 2 · The one look at the test set. Suppose the five-input model is the final choice. Report its AUC on the test set.

The test set is part == "test".

set.seed(8012)
part <- sample(c("train", "valid", "test"), nrow(churn), replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.25))
train <- churn[part == "train", ]; test <- churn[part == "test", ]
m1 <- glm(churned ~ late_deliveries + volume_change_pct + price_index + tech_service + account_years,
          family = binomial, data = train)
auc(predict(m1, test, type = "response"), test$churned)

0.771, close to the validation estimate of 0.766. The validation AUC was used to choose the model, which makes it slightly optimistic in general (the winner of a comparison tends to have been a little lucky); the test AUC wasn’t used for anything, so it’s the one to report. In this module, the test set comes out only in Lessons 11 and 13, for the final comparison.

Quick quiz

1. Which comes first in a predictive modeling project?

The decision defines the target, the timing, and how the model will be judged.

2. A column records “retention discount offered”. Can a churn model three months before renewal use it?

Inputs must be known at the time the model is used. Otherwise it’s leakage.

3. What’s the validation set for?

The test set gives the final estimate, once.

4. Adding inputs raised the training AUC and lowered the validation AUC. What happened?

59 sales-rep coefficients mostly fitted noise.

5. The top decile’s churn rate is 66% and the base rate is 21.5%. What’s the lift?

0.66 / 0.215 = 3.1: three times better than picking at random.

6. When does stratifying the split matter most?

At a 20% rate and 4,000 rows, a simple random split is fine.

Wrap-up

You’ve set up a predictive modeling project the way it’s done in business analytics. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Start from the decision: which accounts to visit, three months before renewal. That fixes the target and the timing.
  • Check the inputs’ timing: only what’s known when the model is used.
  • Split three ways: training to fit, validation to compare and choose, test to report once. The lessons use a 50/25/25 random split: 1,962, 1,030 and 1,008 accounts.
  • Judge on validation: a five-input logistic regression had a validation AUC of 0.766; throwing in every input raised the training AUC and lowered the validation AUC.
  • Speak the business’s language: the top 10% of the list had a lift of 3.1, and the top 20% held half the leavers.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Predictive modeling
Building a model to predict an outcome for new cases, judged by how well it predicts rather than by what its parameters mean.
Target variable
The outcome the model predicts, defined precisely, including when it’s predicted.
Base rate
The share of cases with the outcome, without any model.
Training, validation and test sets
The three parts of the data: to fit models, to compare and tune them, and to estimate the final model’s performance once.
Stratified sampling
Sampling within each class separately, so that each part has the same class proportions.
Lift
The outcome rate among the top-scored cases divided by the base rate.
Capture rate (gain)
The share of all positive cases found in the top-scored part of the list.
