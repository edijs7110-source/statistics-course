Neural networks for prediction

Flexible, hungry, and in need of a leash

Fit a multilayer perceptron to the churn data with nnet, and watch it memorize the training accounts. Then rein it in three ways: stop training early, judged on the validation set; add weight decay; and keep the hidden layer small. Average several networks, and look inside the best one with input importance and partial dependence. Then compare it with the engineered logistic regression, and see why the network doesn’t win here.

Module 8 · Lesson 10 of 13 Week 128 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Neural networks are STAT 585’s third classic predictive model, next to trees and logistic regression. In SAS Enterprise Miner, a “Neural Network” node sits beside the “Decision Tree” and “Regression” nodes, and an analyst runs all three on the same data and compares them. Module 7 built networks from their parts. This lesson uses them the way a business analyst does: on a real prediction problem, with the validation set as the referee.

The big difference from Module 7 is the stakes: a churn network that memorizes the training accounts will send the engineers to the wrong customers. So most of the lesson is about restraint.

A neural network is the most flexible model in this module, and that’s exactly the danger. Today is about keeping it honest.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • prepare inputs for a network: dummy columns and standardizing
  • watch a network overfit, and measure how badly
  • stop training early, using the validation set
  • tune the weight decay and the number of hidden units
  • average several networks
  • explain a network with input importance and partial dependence

Preparing the inputs

Networks need numbers on comparable scales. The setup code prepares the accounts as in Lesson 9 (satisfaction filled, with a missingness flag; sales reps dropped), turns the factors into dummy columns with model.matrix(), and standardizes everything with the training means and SDs. Dtr is the training data, with the 0/1 target churn, and Dva the validation inputs; yva holds the validation target as 0/1. It also defines auc() and ll(), the log loss.

Your turn. How many input columns does the network get, and what are the means and SDs of the standardized training columns?

Apply sd to each column.

inputs <- Dtr[, names(Dtr) != "churn"]
c(columns = ncol(inputs), max_abs_mean = max(abs(colMeans(inputs))), mean_sd = mean(apply(inputs, 2, sd)))

22 columns: the 9 numeric inputs, the missingness flag, and 12 dummy columns for the segment (7), the region (4) and the service contract (1). Every column has mean 0 and SD 1. The validation inputs are scaled with the training means and SDs, so their columns aren’t exactly 0 and 1, as it should be: anything else would leak information from the validation set into the preparation.

A network that memorizes

Your turn. Fit a network with 10 hidden units, no weight decay and up to 2,000 iterations, with the cross-entropy loss (entropy = TRUE, the right loss for a 0/1 target). Compare its training and validation AUCs.

No weight decay: decay = 0.

set.seed(8101)
net <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 10, decay = 0, maxit = 2000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
c(weights = length(net$wts), train = auc(predict(net, Dtr), Dtr$churn), valid = auc(predict(net, Dva), yva))

241 weights for 1,962 accounts, a training AUC of 0.959 and a validation AUC of 0.668. The network has memorized the training accounts, noise and all, and on new accounts it’s worse than Lesson 1’s five-input logistic regression (0.766). The exact numbers can differ slightly between computers, because the optimizer’s path is sensitive to rounding.

Early stopping

A network starts from small random weights, which make it nearly linear, and becomes more complex as training goes on. Stopping the training early, at the point where the validation error is lowest, is the classic way to keep a network simple. It’s what SAS Enterprise Miner’s neural network node does by default, with the validation set as the judge. With nnet, the same random start (the same set.seed()) and a smaller maxit give the same path, cut short.

Your turn. Fit the 10-unit network with maxit of 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 and 500, from the same start, and find the validation log loss and AUC of each.

Use the function’s argument: maxit = it.

t(sapply(c(5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 500), function(it) {
  set.seed(8102)
  net <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 10, decay = 0, maxit = it, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
  p <- predict(net, Dva)
  c(iterations = it, valid_log_loss = ll(p, yva), valid_auc = auc(p, yva), train_auc = auc(predict(net, Dtr), Dtr$churn))
}))

The validation log loss is lowest after about 10 iterations (0.460), and then climbs steadily, to 0.96 by 100 iterations, while the training AUC keeps rising, from 0.79 to 0.97. The validation AUC is best even earlier, at 5 iterations (0.761), and falls to 0.69. So the right amount of training is very little: after 10 iterations, every further one fits noise. Early stopping picks the network at the bottom of the validation curve. Its weakness is that the validation set now does two jobs, stopping and judging, so a separate test set is needed for an honest estimate.

Weight decay and size

Your turn. The other leash is weight decay, a ridge penalty on the weights. Fit the 10-unit network to convergence with decays of 0, 0.01, 0.1, 1 and 5, and compare the validation AUCs.

Use decay = d.

t(sapply(c(0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 5), function(d) {
  set.seed(8103)
  net <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 10, decay = d, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
  c(decay = d, train = auc(predict(net, Dtr), Dtr$churn), valid = auc(predict(net, Dva), yva))
}))

The validation AUC climbs from 0.64 without decay to 0.71, 0.72, 0.78 and 0.79 as the decay grows to 5, while the training AUC ends at 0.81 with a decay of 5, against 0.92 to 0.96 with little or no decay. A large decay keeps the weights small, so the hidden units stay in the nearly linear part of their curves, and the network behaves almost like a logistic regression. That’s a hint about these data.

Your turn. With a decay of 1, compare networks with 1, 2, 5, 10 and 20 hidden units.

Use size = h.

sapply(c(1, 2, 5, 10, 20), function(h) {
  set.seed(8104)
  net <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = h, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE, MaxNWts = 5000)
  c(size = h, valid = auc(predict(net, Dva), yva))
})

0.788 with a single hidden unit, 0.785 with 2, 0.770 with 5, 0.768 with 10 and 0.782 with 20. One hidden unit with a sigmoid output is a logistic regression passed through an extra S-curve, and it’s as good as anything bigger. The network can’t find the thresholds and the interaction on its own from 1,962 accounts: with weight decay to keep it stable, it ends up close to a logistic regression on the raw inputs (0.787).

Your turn. Networks from different random starts disagree. Average the predictions of five networks (5 hidden units, decay 1, seeds 8111 to 8115), and compare the average’s validation AUC with each network’s.

Average across the five columns: rowMeans(preds).

preds <- sapply(8111:8115, function(seed) {
  set.seed(seed)
  net <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 5, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
  predict(net, Dva)
})
c(each = apply(preds, 2, auc, y = yva), average = auc(rowMeans(preds), yva))

The five networks score 0.770 to 0.789, and their average 0.794, better than any of them. Each network has fitted the noise in its own way, and averaging cancels part of it. It’s the same idea as bagging and random forests, next lesson but one, and the standard practice for networks in production.

Looking inside

A network’s weights mean nothing one by one, but two summaries help. Input importance combines the weights along every path from an input to the output; Olden’s method multiplies the input-to-hidden and hidden-to-output weights and sums them, keeping the signs. A partial dependence curve sets one input to a value for every account, averages the predictions, and repeats across the input’s range: it shows the model’s average response to that input.

Your turn. Fit a network with 5 hidden units and decay 1, and find its six most important inputs by Olden’s method.

bar_plot = FALSE returns the numbers instead of a plot.

set.seed(8105)
best <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 5, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
imp <- olden(best, bar_plot = FALSE)
head(imp[order(-abs(imp$importance)), , drop = FALSE], 6)

The price index (+4.3), the volume change (-3.9), the late deliveries (+3.9), the account’s age (-3.6), the service contract (-2.2) and the satisfaction (-2.2). The same inputs as every tree and regression in this module, with sensible signs: higher prices and more late deliveries raise the risk; growing volume, longer relationships, a service contract and satisfied customers lower it. Agreement between very different models is reassuring evidence that these effects are real.

Your turn. Compute the network’s partial dependence on the late deliveries, from 0 to 6: set every validation account’s late deliveries to each value in turn (before scaling), and average the predicted probabilities.

Set the column to the function’s argument, v.

set.seed(8105)
best <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 5, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
pd <- sapply(0:6, function(v) {
  X <- Xva
  X[, "late_deliveries"] <- v
  mean(predict(best, data.frame(scale(X, mu, s))))
})
round(setNames(pd, 0:6), 3)

From 12.6% with no late deliveries to 54.8% with six, rising fastest between two and four. The network has learned that late deliveries matter, and roughly where, but as a smooth S-curve. The data, as the bars in the plot below show, have a step: the churn rate is 14% with none or one late delivery, 22% with two, and jumps to 50% at three. (The bars for five and six come from only ten accounts, so don’t read much into them.) The network, held back by its weight decay, can’t make a step that sharp, which is one reason the engineered logistic regression of Lesson 9, with its 0/1-2/3+ groups, did better.

set.seed(8105)
best <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 5, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
pd <- sapply(0:6, function(v) { X <- Xva; X[, "late_deliveries"] <- v; mean(predict(best, data.frame(scale(X, mu, s)))) })
obs <- tapply(yva, pmin(valid$late_deliveries, 6), mean)
ggplot() +
  geom_col(aes(x = as.numeric(names(obs)), y = as.numeric(obs)), fill = "grey80", width = 0.6) +
  geom_line(aes(x = 0:6, y = pd), color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.9) + geom_point(aes(x = 0:6, y = pd), color = "#C2416C", size = 2) +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = 0:6) +
  labs(x = "Late deliveries (6 = 6 or more for the bars)", y = "Churn probability") + theme_minimal(base_size = 12)
NoteGo deeper: why the network didn’t find the threshold

A sigmoid hidden unit can make a step, but only if its weights are large, and a step at exactly three late deliveries needs the weight on that input to be large and the others small. Weight decay, which kept the network from overfitting, penalizes exactly such large weights. Without decay, the network can make steps, and it makes far too many, fitting noise. With 1,962 accounts and 22 inputs, there isn’t enough data for the network to tell the real step from the noisy ones, so the best it can do is a smooth compromise. That’s the general pattern: flexible models need data in proportion to their flexibility, and a few hand-crafted inputs can stand in for a lot of data. Ripley’s Pattern Recognition and Neural Networks (Cambridge, 1996), by nnet’s author, discusses weight decay and early stopping as forms of regularization.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Price. Compute the network’s partial dependence on the price index at 0.90, 0.95, 1.00, 1.03, 1.05, 1.08 and 1.12.

The column is "price_index".

set.seed(8105)
best <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Dtr, size = 5, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
prices <- c(0.90, 0.95, 1.00, 1.03, 1.05, 1.08, 1.12)
pd <- sapply(prices, function(v) {
  X <- Xva
  X[, "price_index"] <- v
  mean(predict(best, data.frame(scale(X, mu, s))))
})
round(setNames(pd, prices), 3)

From 9.5% at 10% below the market to 37.2% at 12% above it, rising smoothly the whole way. Again a smooth version of what the data hold: in truth, the risk jumps above 1.04, and mainly for accounts without a service contract, which a partial dependence curve, by averaging over all accounts, can’t show. (Lesson 13 meets two-way partial dependence, which can.) For Lena, the network’s message is still useful: every point above the market costs retention.

Challenge 2 · Against the engineered regression. Lesson 9’s engineered logistic regression had a validation AUC of 0.853. Feed the same engineered inputs to a network (5 hidden units, decay 1), and compare.

Predict the engineered validation data, Eva_d.

engineer <- function(d) {
  d$late_grp <- cut(d$late_deliveries, c(-1, 0, 2, Inf), labels = c("0", "1-2", "3+"))
  d$vol_drop <- as.integer(d$volume_change_pct < -15)
  d$high_price <- as.integer(d$price_index > 1.04)
  d$new_account <- as.integer(d$account_years <= 2)
  d
}
Etr <- model.matrix(churned ~ ., engineer(train))[, -1]; Eva <- model.matrix(churned ~ ., engineer(valid))[, -1]
m_e <- colMeans(Etr); s_e <- apply(Etr, 2, sd)
Etr_d <- data.frame(scale(Etr, m_e, s_e), churn = Dtr$churn); Eva_d <- data.frame(scale(Eva, m_e, s_e))
set.seed(8106)
net_e <- nnet(churn ~ ., data = Etr_d, size = 5, decay = 1, maxit = 1000, entropy = TRUE, trace = FALSE)
auc(predict(net_e, Eva_d), yva)

0.826, up from 0.79 with the raw inputs, but still below the engineered logistic regression (0.853) with the very same inputs. With the thresholds built in, there’s little left for the hidden layer to discover, and its extra flexibility only adds variance. On data with a few strong, simple effects, a well-specified regression is hard to beat; networks earn their keep on large datasets with complex, unknown structure, like images, text or sensor streams, which business tables rarely are.

Quick quiz

1. Why standardize the inputs with the training means and SDs, not the validation set’s?

New data must be prepared exactly as the training data were.

2. A network has a training AUC of 0.96 and a validation AUC of 0.67. What’s happening?

241 weights for 1,962 accounts, without any restraint.

3. How does early stopping restrain a network?

Training starts nearly linear, and gets more complex with each iteration.

4. What does a large weight decay make a network behave like?

Small weights keep the hidden units in the linear part of their curves.

5. What does a partial dependence curve show?

It averages over the other inputs, so it can hide interactions.

6. Why didn’t the network beat the engineered logistic regression?

Good inputs can be worth more than a flexible model.

Wrap-up

You can now use neural networks as a predictive model, and keep them honest. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Prepare with dummy columns and training-set standardization: 22 inputs.
  • Unrestrained, a 10-unit network memorized the training accounts: AUC 0.96 on training, 0.67 on validation.
  • Early stopping and weight decay restrain it; with a decay of 5, the validation AUC reached about 0.79, and one hidden unit was as good as twenty.
  • Averaging five networks beat each one: 0.794.
  • Input importance and partial dependence show what a network uses: price, volume change, late deliveries and age, with smooth responses where the truth has steps.
  • Good inputs beat flexibility: the engineered logistic regression (0.853) remained the best model so far.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Multilayer perceptron
A neural network with one or more hidden layers of units between the inputs and the output.
Early stopping
Stopping training when the validation error stops improving, to prevent overfitting.
Weight decay
A ridge penalty on a network’s weights.
Cross-entropy (log loss)
The loss -\sum[y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)] for a binary target; entropy = TRUE in nnet.
Ensemble of networks
Averaging the predictions of several networks trained from different starts.
Input importance
A summary of how strongly a network’s output depends on each input, from its weights (Garson’s, Olden’s) or from permutations.
Partial dependence
The average prediction as one input is fixed at each value for every case.
