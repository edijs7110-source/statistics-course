CART regression trees

Predicting a number with a staircase

Grow a regression tree for how long a hydrotreating catalyst lasts. Split by hand on the reduction in the sum of squares, let rpart find the poisoning and sintering thresholds and the interaction between them, and compare the tree’s staircase with a straight-line regression on new charges. Then see the staircase’s weak spots, flat predictions beyond the data, and use the tree’s findings to build a regression model that beats both.

Module 8 · Lesson 4 of 13 Week 122 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Trees aren’t only for classes. A regression tree predicts a number, and the plant has a number that matters a lot: how long a charge of hydrotreating catalyst lasts before it has to be regenerated or replaced. A changeout means a shutdown of several days and a catalyst bill of hundreds of thousands of euros, so planners want to know, for a given set of operating conditions, how many days a charge will run.

The process engineers have a feel for what shortens catalyst life. Above a certain inlet temperature, the active metal sinters. Hydrogen sulfide above a few ppm in the feed poisons it. Each regeneration costs some activity. catalyst_life.csv has 600 past charges, with their average operating conditions and how many days each lasted:

Column What it is
inlet_temp_C average reactor inlet temperature, °C
h2s_ppm average hydrogen sulfide in the feed, ppm
space_velocity_h gas hourly space velocity, per hour
pressure_bar reactor pressure, bar
feed_source the feed’s source, A to D
regenerations how many times this charge had been regenerated before
life_days how many days the charge lasted

Effects like “above 365 °C” and “above 5 ppm” are thresholds, and a regression tree is built from thresholds.

Every reaction engineer knows catalysts don’t die linearly: they’re fine until something crosses a line. Let’s see if a tree can find the lines on its own.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • split a numeric target by the reduction in the sum of squares
  • grow a regression tree with rpart(method = "anova"), and read it
  • compare it with a linear regression on held-out charges
  • see the tree find an interaction, and its staircase shape
  • see why trees can’t extrapolate
  • use the tree’s thresholds to build a better regression model

Splitting on the sum of squares

For a numeric target, a node’s impurity is its sum of squares around its mean, \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2, and its prediction is the mean. The best split is the one that reduces the sum of squares the most:

\Delta = \text{SS}(\text{parent}) - \text{SS}(L) - \text{SS}(R) .

Equivalently, it’s the split whose two groups have the most different means, weighted by their sizes, which is a one-way ANOVA with two groups. That’s why rpart calls it method = "anova".

The setup code reads the 600 charges, and puts 400 of them, chosen at random, in a training set, and the other 200 in a test set.

Your turn. Find the correlation of each numeric input with the life.

Take the column for life_days.

num <- sapply(train, is.numeric)
round(cor(train[num])[, "life_days"], 3)

The inlet temperature (-0.47), the H₂S (-0.35) and the regenerations (-0.34) have the strongest correlations, the space velocity a weaker one, and the pressure almost none. Correlations measure straight-line association, so they understate effects that are thresholds, which the next steps find.

Your turn. Compute the share of the training sum of squares removed by splitting at an H₂S of 5 ppm.

The other child is the charges with low H₂S, y[!high].

y <- train$life_days
ss <- function(v) sum((v - mean(v))^2)
high <- train$h2s_ppm >= 5
(ss(y) - ss(y[high]) - ss(y[!high])) / ss(y)

26.9%: one question about the feed’s H₂S explains more than a quarter of the variation in catalyst life. That’s the split’s R^2.

Your turn. Find the best threshold for each numeric input, and the share of the sum of squares it removes.

Divide by the parent’s sum of squares, ss(y).

y <- train$life_days
ss <- function(v) sum((v - mean(v))^2)
best <- function(x) {
  u <- sort(unique(x))
  th <- (head(u, -1) + tail(u, -1)) / 2
  d <- sapply(th, function(t) ss(y) - ss(y[x < t]) - ss(y[x >= t]))
  c(threshold = th[which.max(d)], share = max(d) / ss(y))
}
round(t(sapply(train[c("inlet_temp_C", "h2s_ppm", "space_velocity_h", "pressure_bar", "regenerations")], best)), 4)

The H₂S wins, at 5.03 ppm (26.9%), ahead of the temperature at 381.75 °C (19.9%) and the regenerations at 2.5 (10.3%). By correlation, temperature was first; by splits, H₂S is. Poisoning is a threshold effect, and a split measures exactly that: the H₂S’s correlation was diluted by all the charges below 5 ppm, where it hardly matters.

A regression tree

Your turn. Grow a regression tree with rpart’s defaults, and count its leaves.

A regression tree is method = "anova".

tree <- rpart(life_days ~ ., data = train, method = "anova")
print(tree)
sum(tree$frame$var == "<leaf>")

12 leaves. The root splits on H₂S at 5.03 ppm; both branches then split on temperature, near 375 °C; then the feed source C (a heavier feed) appears in almost every branch, and the regenerations and space velocity further down. The leaves’ predictions range from 494 days (high H₂S and high temperature together) to 1,091 days (clean feed, moderate temperature, few regenerations, low space velocity). Each leaf prints its number of charges, its sum of squares (“deviance”) and its mean.

Your turn. Compare the tree with a linear regression on all six inputs, by their RMSE on the 200 test charges.

Predict the test charges, test.

tree <- rpart(life_days ~ ., data = train, method = "anova")
line <- lm(life_days ~ ., data = train)
c(tree = rmse(predict(tree, test), test$life_days),
  linear = rmse(predict(line, test), test$life_days),
  sd = sd(test$life_days))

85 days for the tree and 125 for the linear model, against a standard deviation of 165. The tree wins clearly, because the true effects are thresholds and an interaction, which a straight-line model can only approximate. On data with smooth, additive effects, the order is usually reversed.

An interaction, found

Your turn. Grow a tree of depth 2, and look at the four leaves’ means.

maxdepth = 2.

tree2 <- rpart(life_days ~ ., data = train, method = "anova", maxdepth = 2)
print(tree2)
round(tree2$frame$yval[tree2$frame$var == "<leaf>"])

494, 753, 831 and 964 days. Going above about 375 °C costs 259 days when the feed has more than 5 ppm of H₂S (753 to 494), but only 134 days when it’s clean (964 to 831): the sintering and the poisoning compound each other. That’s an interaction, and the tree found it simply by splitting on temperature separately in each H₂S branch. In a regression, you’d have to think of adding temperature × H₂S yourself.

The staircase, and its edges

A tree’s predictions are constant within each leaf, so as one input changes, with the others fixed, the prediction moves in steps.

Your turn. Predict the life of a charge with clean feed (2 ppm), source A, no regenerations, a space velocity of 2,000 and 30 bar, at inlet temperatures of 350, 370, 380, 395, 420 and 450 °C, with the tree and with the linear model.

Predict new with the linear model too.

tree <- rpart(life_days ~ ., data = train, method = "anova")
line <- lm(life_days ~ ., data = train)
new <- data.frame(inlet_temp_C = c(350, 370, 380, 395, 420, 450), h2s_ppm = 2, space_velocity_h = 2000,
                  pressure_bar = 30, feed_source = factor("A", levels = levels(train$feed_source)), regenerations = 0)
round(cbind(temp = new$inlet_temp_C, tree = predict(tree, new), linear = predict(line, new)))

The tree says 1,000 days at 350 and 370 °C, 900 at 380, and 852 at 395, 420 and 450 °C. The data stop at 395 °C, and beyond the last split the tree’s prediction is frozen: it has no idea that a charge at 450 °C would sinter in weeks. The linear model keeps falling (655 days at 450), which is at least the right direction. Trees, like kNN in Module 7, can’t extrapolate; they always predict an average of training cases. Inside the data, the tree’s steps are crude too: real catalyst life doesn’t jump by 100 days between 374 and 375 °C.

tree <- rpart(life_days ~ ., data = train, method = "anova"); line <- lm(life_days ~ ., data = train)
g <- data.frame(inlet_temp_C = seq(330, 460, by = 0.5), h2s_ppm = 2, space_velocity_h = 2000, pressure_bar = 30,
                feed_source = factor("A", levels = levels(train$feed_source)), regenerations = 0)
pp <- rbind(data.frame(t = g$inlet_temp_C, life = predict(tree, g), m = "regression tree"),
            data.frame(t = g$inlet_temp_C, life = predict(line, g), m = "linear model"))
ggplot(pp, aes(t, life, color = m)) +
  annotate("rect", xmin = 335, xmax = 395, ymin = -Inf, ymax = Inf, fill = "grey93") +
  geom_step(data = pp[pp$m == "regression tree", ], linewidth = 0.9) + geom_line(data = pp[pp$m == "linear model", ], linewidth = 0.9) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c(`regression tree` = "#C2416C", `linear model` = "#2a78d6"), name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Inlet temperature (°C), gray: the training range", y = "Predicted life (days)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) + theme(legend.position = "top")

Your turn. Compare the tree’s R^2 on the training and test charges.

Use the test charges’ life, test$life_days.

tree <- rpart(life_days ~ ., data = train, method = "anova")
r2 <- function(p, y) 1 - sum((y - p)^2) / sum((y - mean(y))^2)
c(train = r2(predict(tree), train$life_days), test = r2(predict(tree, test), test$life_days))

0.763 on the training charges, 0.730 on the test charges: a small drop, so this 12-leaf tree generalizes well. rpart’s default cp = 0.01 stopped it before it fitted much noise; a tree grown with cp = 0 would fit the training data almost perfectly and do worse on the test set. Lesson 5 chooses the size properly.

NoteGo deeper: trees as basis functions

A regression tree with M leaves R_1, \ldots, R_M is the model

\hat f(x) = \sum_{m=1}^M c_m \, I(x \in R_m),

a linear combination of indicator functions of boxes, with c_m the mean of the training cases in box m. It’s a linear model with a basis chosen by the data, like Module 7’s splines, but with discontinuous, box-shaped basis functions. Two extensions smooth the steps: MARS (multivariate adaptive regression splines, the earth package) builds the same kind of greedy partition from hinge functions \max(0, x - t), so its predictions are continuous; model trees (the Cubist package, or partykit’s lmtree()) fit a regression in each leaf instead of a constant. The Elements of Statistical Learning, sections 9.2 and 9.4, covers both.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · From tree to regression. The tree’s findings suggest a better linear model: a hinge in temperature (no effect below a threshold, a linear decline above it), a step at 5 ppm of H₂S, their interaction, and linear terms for the rest. Fit it with the hinge at 365 °C, and find its test RMSE.

Put the hinge at 365 °C.

hinge <- lm(life_days ~ pmax(inlet_temp_C - 365, 0) * I(h2s_ppm > 5) + space_velocity_h + pressure_bar +
              feed_source + regenerations, data = train)
round(coef(hinge), 3)
rmse(predict(hinge, test), test$life_days)

A test RMSE of 59 days: better than the tree (85) and much better than the plain linear model (125). Its coefficients read like an engineering rule: each °C above 365 costs 7.3 days, or 10.8 with a poisoned feed; more than 5 ppm of H₂S costs 217 days; each regeneration 35 days; feed C 113 days. This is a common, and very effective, way to use trees: as an exploration tool that shows where the thresholds and interactions are, so that a simpler, smoother model can be built around them.

Challenge 2 · Where’s the kink? The tree put its temperature split near 375 °C. Search for the hinge point k from 350 to 385 °C that gives the hinge model the smallest training residual sum of squares.

deviance(fit) is the residual sum of squares.

ks <- 350:385
rss <- sapply(ks, function(k) {
  fit <- lm(life_days ~ pmax(inlet_temp_C - k, 0) * I(h2s_ppm > 5) + space_velocity_h + pressure_bar +
              feed_source + regenerations, data = train)
  deviance(fit)
})
ks[which.min(rss)]

363 °C, close to the sintering threshold that the engineers expected. The tree’s split, near 375 °C, was about 10 °C too high: a tree puts its threshold where a step best separates the means, and a gradual decline that starts at 363 °C looks most step-like further up. Tree thresholds are a good first guess for where something happens, not a precise estimate of it.

Quick quiz

1. What does a regression tree predict in a leaf?

That’s why its predictions form a staircase.

2. How does a regression tree choose a split?

Equivalently, the split whose two group means differ most, weighted by size.

3. Why did the tree beat the linear model on catalyst life?

On smooth, additive relationships, linear models usually win.

4. What does a tree predict for an input value far beyond the training data?

Its prediction is frozen beyond the last split.

5. How did the tree show the interaction between temperature and H₂S?

High temperature cost 259 days with a poisoned feed and 134 with a clean one.

6. What’s a good use of a regression tree, besides predicting?

The hinge model built from the tree’s findings beat the tree.

Wrap-up

You can now grow and use regression trees. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Regression trees split on the reduction in the sum of squares, and predict each leaf’s mean.
  • The best first split was H₂S at 5.03 ppm, removing 26.9% of the sum of squares, even though temperature had the stronger correlation.
  • The 12-leaf tree had a test RMSE of 85 days, against 125 for a linear model, and generalized well (R^2 0.763 on training, 0.730 on test).
  • It found the interaction: high temperature cost 259 days with a poisoned feed, 134 with a clean one.
  • Its staircase can’t extrapolate: frozen at 852 days beyond 389 °C.
  • Tree-inspired regression, with a hinge at 365 °C and a step at 5 ppm, beat both, at 59 days, and the best hinge was at 363 °C.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Regression tree
A tree that predicts a numeric target by the mean of the training cases in each leaf.
Sum of squares (deviance)
\sum(y_i - \bar y)^2 within a node: a regression tree’s impurity.
Piecewise-constant prediction
A prediction that is constant within regions and jumps between them: a staircase.
Interaction
When one input’s effect depends on another’s value.
Extrapolation
Predicting outside the range of the training data, which trees can’t do.
Hinge function
\max(0, x - t): zero below a threshold t, linear above it.
