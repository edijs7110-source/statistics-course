CART classification trees

Probabilities, priors and the price of a mistake

Grow a full CART classification tree for customer churn with rpart, and read it as rules. Score the validation accounts, find the confusion matrix and the AUC, and see what the tree considers important. Then change what it’s aiming for: move the cut-off, give the classes equal prior probabilities, and tell it that missing a leaver costs four times as much as a wasted visit, and watch the tree itself change shape.

Module 8 · Lesson 3 of 13 Week 121 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 2 grew a tree two levels deep, by hand and with rpart(). This lesson lets rpart grow a full tree with its default settings, and looks at it the way a business analyst would: as a set of rules, as a source of probabilities, and as a classifier that can be told what its mistakes cost.

That last part matters for Lena’s problem. A visit to an account that wasn’t going to leave wastes an engineer’s day: call it a cost of 1. Missing an account that leaves loses a customer, and the margin with it: much more, say 4. A tree grown to minimize the plain error rate treats both mistakes alike. CART has two ways to make it care: prior probabilities and a loss matrix.

A tree that’s 81% accurate sounds great, until you notice it misses two thirds of the customers who leave. Today we teach the tree what really matters.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • grow a CART classification tree with rpart, and read it as rules
  • get class predictions and probabilities, a confusion matrix and the AUC
  • read the tree’s variable importance
  • move the classification cut-off
  • change the tree with prior probabilities and with a loss matrix
  • compare the policies by their cost

A full tree

The setup code makes Lesson 1’s partition, and defines the auc() function from Lesson 1. The trees in this lesson leave out the 60 sales reps (Lesson 8 deals with them), but include the satisfaction score, with its missing values: rpart handles them with surrogate splits, also in Lesson 8.

Your turn. Grow a tree with rpart’s defaults, and count its leaves.

A classification tree is method = "class". (rpart would guess it from the factor response, but it’s good practice to say so.)

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
print(tree)
sum(tree$frame$var == "<leaf>")

10 leaves. Below the first split (late deliveries), the low-risk branch splits on a volume drop of 14.5%, then on the account’s age and the price index; the high-risk branch splits on the segment, then on age and complaints. These are the effects built into the data, found in the order of their strength, and the interactions too: the price index matters only for established accounts whose volume is falling. The satisfaction score doesn’t appear: its information is mostly carried by the complaints and late deliveries, which cause it.

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
rpart.plot(tree, type = 4, extra = 106, box.palette = list("#F4C0D1", "#C2416C"), branch.lty = 1, roundint = FALSE, digits = 3, tweak = 1.05)

Classes and probabilities

predict(tree, newdata, type = "class") gives each account’s predicted class, the majority class of its leaf; type = "prob" gives the leaf’s class proportions.

Your turn. Predict the classes of the validation accounts, and make the confusion matrix and the accuracy.

type = "class".

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
cl <- predict(tree, valid, type = "class")
print(table(predicted = cl, actual = valid$churned))
mean(cl == valid$churned)

81.2% accurate, against 78.5% for predicting that nobody leaves. But of the 221 validation leavers, the tree flags only 79, and misses 142. The majority-class rule predicts “yes” only in leaves where more than half the accounts churned, and most leavers are in leaves where they’re a minority.

Your turn. Find the validation AUC of the tree’s probabilities, and how many different probabilities it gives.

The churn probability is the "yes" column.

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
p <- predict(tree, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"]
c(auc = auc(p, valid$churned), distinct = length(unique(p)))

An AUC of 0.690, with only 10 distinct probabilities, one per leaf. Lesson 1’s five-input logistic regression had 0.766. The tree’s ranking is coarse: 1,480 training accounts share the lowest leaf, so the tree can’t rank them against each other at all. That’s the price of a tree’s readability. Pruning (Lesson 5) finds the right size, and Lessons 12 and 13 average hundreds of trees to get a fine ranking back.

What the tree thinks is important

rpart’s variable importance adds up, for each input, the impurity decreases of the splits it made, plus a share of those it would have made as a surrogate. It’s a quick summary of what the tree relies on.

Your turn. Look at the tree’s variable importance.

It’s stored in tree$variable.importance.

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
round(tree$variable.importance, 1)

The late deliveries (89.4) far ahead, then the volume change (30.0), the account’s age (22.2), the price index (15.5) and the segment (11.6). For Lena, that’s a priority list for the operations side as much as for sales: fixing deliveries would do more for retention than any visit. The technical service contract, which matters a lot in the data (it changes the effect of price), doesn’t appear at all, because the tree splits on price only once, in a small branch, and has no room left to split on service under it. Importance only describes what this tree used.

Moving the cut-off

The simplest way to catch more leavers is to classify with a lower cut-off on the probability, as in Module 7. If missing a leaver costs 4 and a wasted visit 1, the break-even probability is 1/(1 + 4) = 0.2: visit an account when its churn probability is above 0.2.

Your turn. Classify the validation accounts as leavers when the tree’s probability is at least 0.2, and make the confusion matrix.

Use 0.2.

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
p <- predict(tree, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"]
cl <- ifelse(p >= 0.2, "yes", "no")
table(predicted = cl, actual = valid$churned)

83 leavers caught instead of 79, and 55 wasted visits instead of 52: hardly any change. With only 10 leaf probabilities, moving the cut-off from 0.5 to 0.2 only re-labels the leaves whose churn rate lies in between, and only one does: a small leaf with a rate of 0.22 and 9 training accounts. Moving the cut-off can only use the ranking the tree already has. Priors and losses change the tree itself.

Prior probabilities

By default, rpart takes the class proportions in the training data as the prior probabilities: 80% stayers, 20% leavers. Setting the priors to 50/50 makes the tree behave as if leavers were as common as stayers. Every leaver then counts four times as much in the impurity calculations, so the tree looks harder for splits that isolate them, and calls a leaf “yes” much more easily.

Your turn. Grow the tree with equal priors, and make its validation confusion matrix. Also find its leaves and its AUC.

The priors are for the classes in level order, “no” then “yes”: c(0.5, 0.5).

tree_p <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class", parms = list(prior = c(0.5, 0.5)))
print(table(predicted = predict(tree_p, valid, type = "class"), actual = valid$churned))
c(leaves = sum(tree_p$frame$var == "<leaf>"), auc = auc(predict(tree_p, valid)[, "yes"], valid$churned))

Now 158 leavers caught (63 missed), at the cost of 186 wasted visits. The tree is different: 5 leaves instead of 10, a simpler tree aimed at separating leavers, with a validation AUC of 0.759, better than the default tree’s 0.690. Its “probabilities” are now adjusted to the 50/50 priors, so they’re no longer the real churn rates: a leaf with 30% leavers shows up as about 63%. Use them to rank, not as probabilities.

A loss matrix

The more direct way is to tell rpart what each mistake costs. The loss matrix has the true classes in its rows and the predicted classes in its columns, with zeros on the diagonal:

predicted no predicted yes
actually no 0 1 (a wasted visit)
actually yes 4 (a lost customer) 0

rpart then labels each leaf with the class that has the lower expected loss, and takes the losses into account when it chooses splits (by altering the priors, as the Go deeper box explains).

Your turn. Grow the tree with this loss matrix, and make its validation confusion matrix.

Pass the matrix: loss = L. Check that L[2, 1], true “yes” predicted “no”, is 4.

L <- matrix(c(0, 4,
              1, 0), nrow = 2)
L
tree_l <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class", parms = list(loss = L))
print(table(predicted = predict(tree_l, valid, type = "class"), actual = valid$churned))
sum(tree_l$frame$var == "<leaf>")

The same 5-leaf tree as with equal priors, and the same confusion matrix: 158 leavers caught, 186 wasted visits. That’s no coincidence: with two classes, a loss of 4 for missing a leaver is equivalent to raising the leavers’ prior from 0.2 to exactly 0.5 (the Go deeper box below shows why). The difference is in what you write down: the loss matrix says what the business believes, in its own units, so it’s the clearer way to set up the problem.

NoteGo deeper: priors and losses are two views of one thing

For two classes, a loss matrix with costs L_{12} (true class 1 predicted as 2) and L_{21} is equivalent, for labeling the leaves, to altered priors

\tilde\pi_j = \frac{\pi_j L_{j\cdot}}{\sum_k \pi_k L_{k\cdot}},

where L_{j\cdot} is the cost of misclassifying class j. rpart grows the tree with these altered priors, so a loss of 4 for missing a leaver acts like raising the leavers’ prior from 0.20 to 0.2 \times 4/(0.8 \times 1 + 0.2 \times 4) = 0.5: exactly the equal priors of the previous step. That’s why the two trees here are similar. With more than two classes, a loss matrix can’t always be expressed as priors, and rpart’s “generalized Gini” handles it. Therneau and Atkinson’s rpart vignette, section 4, gives the details.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Which policy costs least? With a wasted visit costing 1 and a lost customer 4, find the total validation cost of four policies: the default tree’s classes, the default tree with a cut-off of 0.2, the equal-priors tree, and the loss-matrix tree.

A missed leaver costs 4.

cost <- function(cl) sum(cl == "yes" & valid$churned == "no") * 1 + sum(cl == "no" & valid$churned == "yes") * 4
tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
tree_p <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class", parms = list(prior = c(0.5, 0.5)))
tree_l <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class", parms = list(loss = matrix(c(0, 4, 1, 0), 2)))
c(default = cost(predict(tree, valid, type = "class")),
  cutoff_0.2 = cost(ifelse(predict(tree, valid)[, "yes"] >= 0.2, "yes", "no")),
  priors = cost(predict(tree_p, valid, type = "class")),
  loss = cost(predict(tree_l, valid, type = "class")))

620 for the default tree, 607 with the 0.2 cut-off, and 438 for both the equal-priors and the loss-matrix trees. Building the costs into the tree saved 29% against the default; moving the cut-off saved 2%, because a cut-off can only re-label the leaves of a tree that was grown for a different goal. When the costs of mistakes are unequal, tell the tree while it grows. Lesson 11 does the same with euros instead of a 1-to-4 ratio.

Challenge 2 · Rules for the sales team. rpart.plot::rpart.rules() writes each leaf of a tree as an if-then rule. Print the rules of the default tree, and find the rule with the highest churn rate.

Count the rules: nrow(rules).

tree <- rpart(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, method = "class")
rules <- rpart.rules(tree, roundint = FALSE)
print(rules)
nrow(rules)

Ten rules, one per leaf, sorted by churn rate. The riskiest, at 75%: three or more late deliveries, not in Food or Pharma, and at least one complaint. The next, at 73%: three or more late deliveries in Food or Pharma, but a new account. Rules like these go straight into a sales playbook, which is why trees are so popular in business, whatever their AUC.

Quick quiz

1. What does predict(tree, type = "class") return for an account?

With default priors and losses, that’s the class with more than half of the leaf’s training cases.

2. Why did the default tree have a lower AUC than the logistic regression?

A coarse ranking loses AUC. Ensembles of trees fix that.

3. What does setting equal priors do?

The resulting probabilities are adjusted to the priors, so they’re for ranking.

4. In rpart’s loss matrix, what is L[2, 1]?

Rows are the true classes, columns the predicted ones.

5. Why did moving the cut-off from 0.5 to 0.2 change so little?

A cut-off can only re-label whole leaves.

6. What does rpart’s variable importance measure?

It describes what this particular tree relied on.

Wrap-up

You can now grow a classification tree, read it, and make it care about costs. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • A default tree for churn had 10 leaves, and found the planted thresholds and interactions in order of strength.
  • Its predictions are leaf proportions: a validation AUC of 0.690 from only 10 distinct values, and 81.2% accuracy that missed 142 of 221 leavers.
  • Variable importance put the late deliveries far ahead of the volume change, the account’s age and the price.
  • Moving the cut-off re-labels whole leaves, so it changed little here.
  • Priors and a loss matrix change the tree itself: equal priors gave a 5-leaf tree that caught 158 leavers, with an AUC of 0.759.
  • Costs compare the policies directly: building the 4-to-1 cost into the tree cost far less than the default tree’s 620.

NoteKey words from this lesson
CART
Classification and regression trees: binary recursive partitioning with impurity-based splits and cost-complexity pruning.
Class probability (tree)
The share of each class among the training cases in a leaf.
Prior probabilities
The class probabilities a tree assumes; by default, the training proportions.
Loss matrix
A matrix of misclassification costs, with true classes in the rows and predicted classes in the columns.
Variable importance (rpart)
The sum of the impurity decreases from an input’s primary and surrogate splits.
Decision rule
A leaf written as the conditions on its path from the root: “if … and … then …”.
