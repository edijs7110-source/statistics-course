Assessing and comparing models

Lift, gains and euros

Compare the module’s best models the way a business does. Draw gains and lift charts on the validation accounts, compare the models when the engineers can make only 150 visits, and turn a churn probability into the expected value of a visit with a profit matrix. Find the profit-maximizing number of visits and cut-off, check them against theory, and then, once, score the test set to report what the chosen model would earn.

Module 8 · Lesson 11 of 13 Week 129 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Ten lessons have produced a dozen churn models. The AUC has been the yardstick so far, and it’s a good one for comparing rankings. But Lena doesn’t care about the AUC. She cares about what happens when her engineers visit the accounts at the top of the list: how many leavers they reach, and what it’s worth. This lesson measures the models in her terms, with the tools STAT 585 uses: gains and lift charts, and a profit matrix.

The three contenders are the module’s best of each kind: Lesson 9’s engineered logistic regression, Lesson 5’s pruned CART tree (20 leaves), and Lesson 7’s conditional inference tree.

This is the lesson where models turn into money. By the end, you’ll be able to tell Lena exactly how many accounts to visit, and what it’s worth.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • compare models by AUC, cumulative gains and lift, on the validation set
  • compare them under a capacity limit: 150 visits
  • build a profit matrix, and derive the break-even churn probability
  • choose the number of visits and the cut-off that maximize profit
  • score the test set once, and report the chosen model’s value

Three models on the validation set

The setup code refits the three models on the training accounts, and puts their validation churn probabilities in the data frame scores, with a column per model. y is TRUE for the validation accounts that churned.

Your turn. Find each model’s validation AUC.

Pass the target, y.

round(sapply(scores, auc, y = y), 3)

0.853 for the engineered logistic regression, 0.767 for the pruned CART tree and 0.792 for ctree, as in earlier lessons. The AUC averages the ranking over the whole list, including its bottom, where nobody will be visited. The next charts look at the part of the list that matters.

Gains and lift

Sort the accounts from the highest score to the lowest. The cumulative gains at depth d is the share of all the leavers found in the top d of the list. The lift of a decile is its churn rate divided by the base rate.

Your turn. Find each model’s cumulative gains at depths of 10%, 20%, 30% and 50%.

Divide by the total number of leavers, sum(y).

gains <- function(p, y, depths = c(0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5)) {
  o <- order(p, decreasing = TRUE)
  found <- cumsum(y[o]) / sum(y)
  setNames(sapply(depths, function(d) found[ceiling(d * length(y))]), paste0(depths * 100, "%"))
}
round(sapply(scores, gains, y = y), 3)

In the top 10% of the list, the logistic regression finds 35.7% of the leavers, CART 28.1% and ctree 29.4%; in the top 20%, 59.7%, 51.1% and 52.0%. Visiting a fifth of the accounts by the logistic regression’s list reaches six leavers in ten; by CART’s, five in ten. A model with no skill would find 10% in the top 10%, 20% in the top 20%, and so on: the diagonal of the gains chart below.

gcurve <- do.call(rbind, lapply(names(scores), function(m) { o <- order(scores[[m]], decreasing = TRUE)
  data.frame(depth = seq_along(o) / length(o), found = cumsum(y[o]) / sum(y), model = m) }))
ggplot(gcurve, aes(depth, found, color = model)) + geom_abline(linetype = "dashed", color = "grey60") + geom_line(linewidth = 0.9) +

Your turn. Find the lift in each decile for each model.

Divide by the base rate, mean(y).

lift <- function(p, y) {
  o <- order(p, decreasing = TRUE)
  decile <- ceiling(seq_along(o) / length(o) * 10)
  tapply(y[o], decile, mean) / mean(y)
}
round(sapply(scores, lift, y = y), 2)

The logistic regression’s top decile has a lift of 3.57: its accounts are 3.6 times as likely to leave as average. Its lift then falls steadily, to 0.09 in the bottom decile, the shape of a good ranking. The trees’ lifts are lower at the top (2.81 and 2.94) and bumpy lower down: CART’s bottom decile has a lift of 0.86, because a tree gives whole leaves the same score, and ties at the bottom are broken arbitrarily.

Comparing in euros

Finance has estimated the economics of a visit. A visit costs about €500 of an engineer’s time. A customer who would have left and is kept is worth €15,000 a year in margin. And a visit to an account that was going to leave keeps it about 40% of the time. That’s a profit matrix:

the account would leave the account would stay
visit 0.4 \times 15{,}000 - 500 = 5{,}500 -500
don’t visit 0 0

(The “don’t visit” row is 0 because profits are measured against doing nothing.) Lena has 15 engineers who can each make 10 visits before the renewal round: 150 visits for the validation set’s 1,030 accounts.

Your turn. For each model, find the profit of visiting the 150 accounts it ranks highest.

Each of the n_visits visits costs 500.

value <- function(p, y, n_visits) {
  top <- order(p, decreasing = TRUE)[seq_len(n_visits)]
  sum(y[top]) * (0.4 * 15000) - 500 * n_visits
}
sapply(scores, value, y = y, n_visits = 150)

€543,000 with the logistic regression’s list, €441,000 with CART’s and €459,000 with ctree’s. The difference between the best model and the best tree is €84,000 on 1,030 accounts, in one renewal round, from a better ranking of the same accounts. That’s a number Lena can weigh against the extra effort of maintaining a regression with engineered inputs, which an AUC difference of 0.06 isn’t.

How many visits?

Without the capacity limit, the question becomes: which accounts are worth a visit at all? The expected value of visiting an account with churn probability p is

p \times 5{,}500 + (1 - p) \times (-500) = 6{,}000\,p - 500,

which is positive when p > 500/6{,}000 = 0.083.

Your turn. Compute the break-even probability, and the expected value of a visit at churn probabilities of 0.05, 0.2 and 0.5.

If the account would stay, the visit loses loss_stay.

gain_leave <- 0.4 * 15000 - 500
loss_stay <- -500
ev <- function(p) p * gain_leave + (1 - p) * loss_stay
c(break_even = -loss_stay / (gain_leave - loss_stay), ev_0.05 = ev(0.05), ev_0.2 = ev(0.2), ev_0.5 = ev(0.5))

A break-even of 0.083: an account with a 1-in-12 chance of leaving is worth a visit. At 5%, a visit loses €200 on average; at 20%, it earns €700; at 50%, €2,500. Because a kept customer is worth so much more than a visit costs, the break-even is low, and many accounts qualify. The calculation is only as good as the probabilities, which is why Lesson 9 checked the model’s calibration.

Your turn. For each model, compute the profit of visiting the top n accounts, for n from 0 to 1,030 in steps of 10, and find the best n and its profit.

Use the inner function’s argument: n.

value <- function(p, y, n_visits) {
  top <- order(p, decreasing = TRUE)[seq_len(n_visits)]
  sum(y[top]) * 6000 - 500 * n_visits
}
ns <- seq(0, 1030, by = 10)
prof <- sapply(scores, function(p) sapply(ns, function(n) value(p, y, n)))
rbind(best_n = ns[apply(prof, 2, which.max)], best_profit = apply(prof, 2, max), visit_all = prof[nrow(prof), ])

The logistic regression’s best is to visit 680 accounts, for €926,000; ctree’s, 650 accounts for €857,000. CART’s profit keeps rising to the end: its best is to visit everyone, for €811,000, because its bottom leaves still hold leavers. With this generous profit matrix, even a mediocre model says “visit most accounts”, and the good model’s advantage is that it knows which third to skip: €115,000 more than visiting everyone.

ns <- seq(0, 1030, by = 10)
pc <- do.call(rbind, lapply(names(scores), function(m) { o <- order(scores[[m]], decreasing = TRUE)
  data.frame(n = ns, profit = sapply(ns, function(k) sum(y[o[seq_len(k)]]) * 6000 - 500 * k), model = m) }))
ggplot(pc, aes(n, profit / 1000, color = model)) + geom_vline(xintercept = 150, linetype = "dashed", color = "grey60") + geom_line(linewidth = 0.9) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c(logistic = "#C2416C", cart = "#1baf7a", ctree = "#2a78d6"), name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Accounts visited (highest scores first)", y = "Profit (thousand €)") + theme_minimal(base_size = 12) + theme(legend.position = "top")

Your turn. In practice, the rule is a cut-off on the probability rather than a number of visits. Find the cut-off from 0.02 to 0.60 that maximizes the logistic regression’s validation profit, and compare it with the theoretical break-even, 0.083.

The theoretical cut-off is 500 / 6000.

profit_at <- function(p, y, t) { visit <- p >= t; sum(y[visit]) * 6000 - 500 * sum(visit) }
ts <- seq(0.02, 0.60, by = 0.01)
pr <- sapply(ts, function(t) profit_at(scores$logistic, y, t))
c(best_cutoff = ts[which.max(pr)], best_profit = max(pr),
  theory_profit = profit_at(scores$logistic, y, 500 / 6000), theory_visits = sum(scores$logistic >= 500 / 6000))

The best validation cut-off is 0.06, for €922,500; the theoretical 0.083 gives €913,500, with 501 visits. Within 1%. The empirical optimum is a little lower than theory because the model’s probabilities are a little low on this validation set (Lesson 9). Use the theoretical cut-off, which depends only on the costs, unless the model’s calibration is known to be off; the empirical one is tuned to one set of accounts’ luck.

The test set, once

The model (the engineered logistic regression) and the rule (visit when \hat p \ge 0.083) are chosen. Now, and only now, the test set gives the honest estimate.

Your turn. Score the 1,008 test accounts, and find the test AUC, the number of visits, the leavers reached, and the profit, against visiting everyone.

Visiting everyone costs 500 for each of the length(y_test) accounts.

p_test <- predict(logit, prep(raw_test), type = "response")
y_test <- raw_test$churned == "yes"
visit <- p_test >= 500 / 6000
c(test_auc = auc(p_test, y_test), visits = sum(visit), leavers_reached = sum(y_test[visit]), leavers = sum(y_test),
  profit = sum(y_test[visit]) * 6000 - 500 * sum(visit), visit_everyone = sum(y_test) * 6000 - 500 * length(y_test))

A test AUC of 0.865, a little above the validation estimate (0.853): the model generalizes. The rule visits 482 of 1,008 accounts, reaches 185 of the 204 leavers, and earns €869,000, against €720,000 for visiting everyone and nothing for visiting no one. That’s the number for Lena’s report: about €149,000 per 1,000 accounts better than the blanket policy, and almost €870,000 better than doing nothing, per renewal round.

NoteGo deeper: what a profit matrix assumes

The calculation assumes that a visit’s effect is the same for every leaver (a 40% save rate), that visits don’t change the behavior of customers who would have stayed, and that the model’s probabilities are calibrated. The first two are strong: a visit may save a customer annoyed by late deliveries far more easily than one leaving because of a competitor’s price. Estimating who responds to a visit, rather than who will leave, is uplift modeling, and it needs data from a randomized trial: visited and unvisited accounts with similar scores. Without it, a churn model can send engineers to customers who will leave whatever anyone does. Gutierrez and Gérardy’s “Causal Inference and Uplift Modeling: A Review of the Literature” (Proceedings of Machine Learning Research 67, 2017) is an accessible start.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · A lower save rate. Suppose a visit saves only 20% of the leavers. Find the new break-even probability, and the logistic regression’s validation profit and number of visits at it.

Break even when p \times 3{,}000 = 500: divide by gain.

gain <- 0.2 * 15000
t20 <- 500 / gain
visit <- scores$logistic >= t20
c(cutoff = t20, visits = sum(visit), profit = sum(y[visit]) * gain - 500 * sum(visit))

The break-even doubles, to 1/6 = 0.167, the rule visits 358 accounts instead of 501, and the validation profit falls to €331,000, about a third of the 40% case. When visits work less well, fewer accounts are worth one, and the model’s ranking matters more, because the margin for error at the top of the list is smaller. The save rate is the most uncertain number in the profit matrix, so a report should show how the decision changes with it.

Challenge 2 · Did the ranking hold? Find the test AUCs of the pruned CART tree and ctree too. (Strictly, this is a second look at the test set; it’s fine after the choice is made, as a report, not as a reason to change the choice.)

Compare with y_test.

0.865 for the logistic regression, 0.783 for CART and 0.794 for ctree: the same order as on the validation set, and the same gaps. The choice holds up. Quick quiz

1. What does the cumulative gains at 20% measure?

The logistic regression found 60% of the leavers in its top 20%.

2. A decile’s lift is 3.6. What does that mean?

Lift is a rate relative to the base rate.

3. A visit costs €500 and saves 40% of leavers worth €15,000. Above what churn probability is a visit worth it?

6{,}000p - 500 > 0 when p > 1/12.

4. Why compare models at a fixed number of visits (150)?

The best model earned €84,000 more than the best tree at 150 visits.

5. Why use the theoretical cut-off rather than the one that maximized validation profit?

Wrap-up

You can now compare models the way a business decides. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Gains and lift show the top of the list: the logistic regression found 60% of the leavers in its top 20%, with a top-decile lift of 3.57; the trees found about 51%.
  • Under a capacity limit of 150 visits, the logistic regression’s list was worth €543,000, against €441,000 and €459,000 for the trees.
  • A profit matrix gives the value of a visit, 6{,}000p - 500, and a break-even probability of 0.083.
  • The profit curve peaked at 680 visits (€926,000); the profit-maximizing cut-off (0.06) and the theoretical one (0.083) were within 1%.
  • On the test set, once: AUC 0.865, 482 visits reaching 185 of 204 leavers, and €869,000, against €720,000 for visiting everyone.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Cumulative gains
The share of all positive cases found in the top d of a list sorted by score.
Lift
The positive rate in a part of the list divided by the overall rate.
Gains chart
Cumulative gains plotted against the depth of the list, with the diagonal for random selection.
Profit matrix
The profit or cost of each combination of decision and outcome.
Break-even probability
The predicted probability above which an action has a positive expected profit.
Profit curve
The total profit of acting on the top n cases, plotted against n.
Uplift modeling
Predicting the effect of an action on each case, rather than the outcome itself.
