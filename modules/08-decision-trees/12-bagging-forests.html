Bagging and random forests

Many unstable trees make one stable forest

A deep tree is a high-variance model: grow two on slightly different samples and they disagree. Average fifty of them grown on bootstrap samples and the AUC jumps, which is bagging, built here by hand. Estimate the forest’s error from the out-of-bag accounts, then let randomForest decorrelate the trees by sampling inputs at each split. Tune mtry, read two kinds of variable importance (and catch one of them flattering a useless input), and grow a regression forest for catalyst life.

Module 8 · Lesson 12 of 13 Week 130 of 160 About 5–7 hours

A single tree has a deep flaw that pruning only partly fixes: it’s unstable. Change a few training accounts, and the first split can move, and with it everything below. That’s high variance, in the bias-variance sense of Module 7, and it’s why single trees, however well pruned, rank accounts poorly. Leo Breiman’s fix, in two steps (1996 and 2001), is one of the great ideas of machine learning: grow many trees on different samples, and average them.

One tree is a single opinion, and a moody one. A forest is a committee, and committees of independent, moody members are surprisingly wise.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • see how much two deep trees on bootstrap samples disagree
  • build bagging by hand, from 50 bootstrap trees
  • estimate a bagged model’s error from its out-of-bag cases
  • grow random forests with randomForest(), and see why sampling inputs helps
  • tune mtry and the number of trees by the out-of-bag error
  • compare two measures of variable importance, and catch one being fooled
  • grow a regression forest

Unstable trees

The setup code prepares the accounts as in Lesson 9: satisfaction filled with a missingness flag, and no sales reps (randomForest can’t handle a factor with more than 53 levels). It makes Lesson 1’s partition, and defines auc(), which here takes the “no”/“yes” factor.

Your turn. Draw two bootstrap samples of the training accounts (sampling with replacement), grow a deep, unpruned tree on each, and compare their first splits and the correlation of their validation predictions.

A bootstrap sample is drawn with replacement.

set.seed(8121)
b1 <- train[sample(nrow(train), replace = TRUE), ]
b2 <- train[sample(nrow(train), replace = TRUE), ]
t1 <- rpart(churned ~ ., data = b1, method = "class", control = deep)
t2 <- rpart(churned ~ ., data = b2, method = "class", control = deep)
p1 <- predict(t1, valid)[, "yes"]; p2 <- predict(t2, valid)[, "yes"]
list(first_splits = c(as.character(t1$frame$var[2]), as.character(t2$frame$var[2])),
     correlation = cor(p1, p2), aucs = c(auc(p1, valid$churned), auc(p2, valid$churned)))

The two trees split first on different inputs below the root’s late-delivery split (the volume change in one, the price in the other), and their predictions for the validation accounts are correlated at only 0.45. Same population, same method, two quite different models, with validation AUCs of 0.777 and 0.756. Each deep tree has captured the real structure plus a different helping of noise. Averaging them should keep the structure and cancel some of the noise.

Bagging by hand

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating): grow B deep trees, each on its own bootstrap sample of the training data, and average their predicted probabilities. Each tree is unbiased-ish and very variable; their average keeps the low bias and has a much lower variance.

Your turn. Bag 50 deep trees, and compare the validation AUC of the average with that of a single tree.

Average across the 50 columns: rowMeans(P).

set.seed(8122)
B <- 50
P <- matrix(NA, nrow(valid), B)
for (b in 1:B) {
  i <- sample(nrow(train), replace = TRUE)
  tree_b <- rpart(churned ~ ., data = train[i, ], method = "class", control = deep)
  P[, b] <- predict(tree_b, valid)[, "yes"]
}
c(single_tree = auc(P[, 1], valid$churned), mean_single = mean(apply(P, 2, auc, y = valid$churned)),
  bagged = auc(rowMeans(P), valid$churned))

The first tree alone has an AUC of 0.721, and the 50 trees average 0.745 each. Their average has 0.826, better than any single tree in this module, without any pruning or tuning. The averaged probabilities also take hundreds of distinct values, so the ranking is fine-grained, which was the other weakness of single trees.

Out-of-bag error

A bootstrap sample of n accounts leaves out each account with probability (1 - 1/n)^n \approx e^{-1} = 0.368. So every tree has about 37% of the accounts out of bag (OOB): unseen. Predicting each account with only the trees that didn’t see it gives an honest error estimate for free, with no validation set.

Your turn. For one bootstrap sample of the 1,962 training accounts, find the share that’s out of bag, and compare with e^{-1}.

An account is out of bag if it isn’t in the sample i.

set.seed(8122)
i <- sample(nrow(train), replace = TRUE)
c(share_out_of_bag = mean(!(seq_len(nrow(train)) %in% i)), e_minus_1 = exp(-1))

36.95% of the accounts are out of bag, against e^{-1} = 36.79\%. The other 63% are in the sample, many of them more than once.

Your turn. Bag the 50 trees again, but this time also record each tree’s predictions for its out-of-bag training accounts. Average each account’s out-of-bag predictions, and find the OOB AUC.

Predict the out-of-bag accounts, train[out, ].

set.seed(8122)
B <- 50
oob <- matrix(NA, nrow(train), B)
for (b in 1:B) {
  i <- sample(nrow(train), replace = TRUE)
  tree_b <- rpart(churned ~ ., data = train[i, ], method = "class", control = deep)
  out <- setdiff(seq_len(nrow(train)), i)
  oob[out, b] <- predict(tree_b, train[out, ])[, "yes"]
}
p_oob <- rowMeans(oob, na.rm = TRUE)
auc(p_oob, train$churned)

An OOB AUC of 0.841, on the training accounts, each judged only by trees that never saw it. It’s close to the validation AUC (0.826), and costs nothing extra. (Each account is averaged over about 18 of the 50 trees, so the OOB estimate is slightly pessimistic, as for a smaller forest.) With bagging and forests, the OOB error can replace cross-validation for tuning.

Random forests

Bagged trees are correlated with each other: the late deliveries are such a strong first split that almost every bootstrap tree uses them, and the trees end up similar. Averaging correlated trees cancels less of the noise. A random forest decorrelates them with one more source of randomness: at each split, only a random subset of mtry inputs is considered. With p inputs, the default for classification is \sqrt p. Weaker inputs then get their chance at the top of some trees, and the trees disagree in more useful ways.

Your turn. Fit a random forest of 300 trees with the default mtry, and a bagged forest (mtry = all 13 inputs), and compare their validation AUCs and OOB error rates.

300 trees for both: ntree = 300.

p <- ncol(train) - 1
set.seed(8123)
bag <- randomForest(churned ~ ., data = train, mtry = p, ntree = 300)
set.seed(8123)
rf <- randomForest(churned ~ ., data = train, ntree = 300)
rbind(mtry = c(bag$mtry, rf$mtry),
      oob_error = c(bag$err.rate[300, "OOB"], rf$err.rate[300, "OOB"]),
      valid_auc = c(auc(predict(bag, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"], valid$churned),
                    auc(predict(rf, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"], valid$churned)))

The bagged forest has an OOB error of 15.9% and a validation AUC of 0.823; the random forest, with mtry = 3, 15.0% and 0.832. Sampling inputs helped, a little, here; it helps more when a few inputs dominate and many others carry weaker signal. randomForest’s trees are grown fully deep (down to leaves of one case for classification), which is fine: the averaging does the regularizing.

Your turn. How many trees are enough? Look at the random forest’s OOB error after 1, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 and 300 trees.

The overall OOB error is the "OOB" column.

set.seed(8123)
rf <- randomForest(churned ~ ., data = train, ntree = 300)
round(rf$err.rate[c(1, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 300), "OOB"], 4)

20.4% after one tree, 20.1% after 10, 16.8% after 25, then 15.8%, 15.1%, 14.9% and 15.0%: the error falls quickly and levels off after about 100 trees. Unlike a tree’s depth or a network’s training time, more trees never cause overfitting; past the plateau, they only cost computing time. A few hundred is usually plenty.

Your turn. Tune mtry: fit forests with mtry of 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 and 13, and compare their OOB AUC (from the OOB votes, rf$votes) and validation AUC.

Use the function’s argument: mtry = m.

t(sapply(c(1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13), function(m) {
  set.seed(8124)
  f <- randomForest(churned ~ ., data = train, mtry = m, ntree = 300)
  c(mtry = m, oob_auc = auc(f$votes[, "yes"], train$churned),
    valid_auc = auc(predict(f, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"], valid$churned))
}))

The OOB AUC peaks at mtry 2 or 3 (0.853), and the validation AUC agrees (0.834 and 0.833), with mtry = 1 (each split on a random input) and mtry = 13 (bagging) the worst. The default, \lfloor\sqrt{13}\rfloor = 3, is a good choice here, as it often is. Tuning by OOB, without touching the validation set, picked the same answer.

Variable importance

A forest has two importance measures. The Gini importance (MeanDecreaseGini) adds up each input’s impurity decreases over all the trees, as rpart does for one tree. The permutation importance (MeanDecreaseAccuracy) shuffles each input in each tree’s out-of-bag cases and measures how much the accuracy drops, as Module 7 did by hand.

Your turn. Fit a forest with importance = TRUE, and compare the two measures.

importance = TRUE computes the permutation importance.

set.seed(8125)
rf_i <- randomForest(churned ~ ., data = train, ntree = 300, importance = TRUE)
im <- importance(rf_i)[, c("MeanDecreaseAccuracy", "MeanDecreaseGini")]
round(im[order(-im[, "MeanDecreaseAccuracy"]), ], 1)

By permutation, the late deliveries (45.4), the volume change (23.0), the price (18.8) and the account’s age (16.1) lead, and the volume, the region and the payment terms are near zero: exactly the inputs that matter in the data, and the ones that don’t. The Gini importance agrees at the top, but it gives the useless volume (59.9) and region (36.6) high scores, above the complaints and the service contract. Gini importance rewards inputs with many possible split points (the volume is continuous) or many levels, for the same reason as Lesson 7’s selection bias. Trust the permutation importance.

NoteGo deeper: why averaging works, and where it stops

If B identically distributed predictions each have variance \sigma^2 and pairwise correlation \rho, their average has variance

\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\sigma^2 .

As B grows, the second term vanishes, but the first stays: averaging can’t remove the variance the trees share. Bagging reduces \sigma^2’s share by averaging; random forests also reduce \rho, by sampling inputs, at the cost of a little extra bias in each tree. That’s why mtry has an optimum, and why more trees stop helping. It also explains what forests can’t do: they reduce variance, not bias. A forest of trees that all miss a smooth trend (trees can’t extrapolate, Lesson 4) misses it too. The Elements of Statistical Learning, chapter 15, has the analysis, and Breiman’s “Random Forests” (Machine Learning 45, 2001) is the original.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · A regression forest. Grow a random forest of 500 trees for catalyst life (Lesson 4’s 400 training charges), with the default mtry and with mtry = 4, and compare their test RMSEs with the tree’s (85 days) and the hinge regression’s (59).

mtry = 4.

cat_life <- read.csv("data/catalyst_life.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)
cat_life$charge <- NULL
set.seed(8022); tr <- sample(600, 400)
rmse <- function(f) sqrt(mean((cat_life$life_days[-tr] - predict(f, cat_life[-tr, ]))^2))
set.seed(8126)
rf_default <- randomForest(life_days ~ ., data = cat_life[tr, ], ntree = 500)
set.seed(8126)
rf_4 <- randomForest(life_days ~ ., data = cat_life[tr, ], ntree = 500, mtry = 4)
c(default_mtry = rf_default$mtry, rmse_default = rmse(rf_default), rmse_mtry4 = rmse(rf_4),
  oob_rmse_default = sqrt(rf_default$mse[500]))

For regression, the default mtry is p/3, here 2, with a test RMSE of 73.6 days (OOB RMSE 65.4); with mtry = 4, 69.2. Both beat the single tree (85) by a lot, but neither beats the hinge regression built from the tree’s findings (59). The forest averages staircases, so its predictions are smoother than one tree’s, but it still can’t produce a clean hinge or extrapolate it. Where the structure is simple and known, a good parametric model wins; forests win when it’s complicated and unknown.

Challenge 2 · A pure-noise input. Add a column of random numbers to the training and validation accounts, fit a forest with importance, and find the noise column’s rank among the 14 inputs by each importance measure.

The Gini importance is "MeanDecreaseGini".

set.seed(8127)
train_n <- train; train_n$noise <- rnorm(nrow(train))
rf_n <- randomForest(churned ~ ., data = train_n, ntree = 300, importance = TRUE)
im <- importance(rf_n)
c(rank_permutation = rank(-im[, "MeanDecreaseAccuracy"])[["noise"]],
  rank_gini = rank(-im[, "MeanDecreaseGini"])[["noise"]])

By permutation importance, the noise column ranks 13th of 14, at the bottom with the payment terms and the missingness flag, where it belongs. By Gini importance, it ranks 7th, above the complaints, the satisfaction and the service contract, all of which really matter. A continuous column of random numbers offers a split point between every pair of values, and deep trees use them to fit noise in small nodes, which the Gini importance adds up as if it were useful. Adding a noise column is a good sanity check for any importance measure: anything ranked below it can’t be trusted.

Quick quiz

1. Why do single deep trees predict poorly?

Two trees on bootstrap samples had predictions correlated at only 0.45.

2. What is bagging?

Random forests add the input sampling.

3. About what share of the training cases is out of bag for each tree?

(1 - 1/n)^n \approx e^{-1} = 0.368.

4. What does mtry control in a random forest?

Smaller mtry decorrelates the trees, at some cost in each tree’s accuracy.

5. Can adding more trees to a random forest cause overfitting?

The number of trees isn’t a flexibility setting.

6. Why prefer permutation importance to Gini importance?

The useless volume had a Gini importance above the complaints’.

Wrap-up

You can now build and tune tree ensembles that average away variance. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Deep trees are unstable: two bootstrap trees’ predictions correlated at 0.45.
  • Bagging 50 deep trees lifted the validation AUC from about 0.745 per tree to 0.826.
  • Out-of-bag cases (37% per tree) give an honest error estimate for free: an OOB AUC of 0.841.
  • Random forests sample mtry inputs per split, decorrelating the trees: 0.832 with the default mtry = 3, against 0.823 for bagging. The OOB error levels off after about 100 trees.
  • Permutation importance found exactly the inputs that matter; Gini importance flattered the useless volume and region.
  • Regression forests cut catalyst life’s RMSE from 85 days (one tree) to 69 to 74, but didn’t beat the tree-inspired hinge regression.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Bagging (bootstrap aggregating)
Averaging the predictions of models fitted to bootstrap samples of the training data.
Bootstrap sample
A sample of n cases drawn with replacement from n.
Out-of-bag (OOB) cases
The training cases not in a given tree’s bootstrap sample; about 37% of them.
OOB error
The error of predicting each training case with only the trees that didn’t see it.
Random forest
Bagged trees in which each split considers only a random subset of mtry inputs.
Decorrelation
Making the ensemble’s members less similar, so that averaging cancels more of their errors.
Gini importance
The total impurity decrease from an input’s splits across a forest.
Permutation importance
The drop in out-of-bag accuracy when an input’s values are shuffled.
