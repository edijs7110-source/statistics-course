Conditional inference trees and C5.0

Two other ways to grow a tree

First, a flaw: on pure noise, CART picks an input with 20 categories 95% of the time, because it has so many ways to split. Conditional inference trees fix that by choosing inputs with permutation tests, and stopping when nothing is significant. Grow one on the churn data, read its tests, and see it refuse the 60 sales reps. Then meet C5.0, the machine-learning tradition’s tree, with its rule sets and its built-in boosting, and compare all of them on the validation accounts.

Module 8 · Lesson 7 of 13 Week 125 of 160 About 5–7 hours

CART and CHAID aren’t the only tree algorithms. This lesson covers the two others you’re most likely to meet: conditional inference trees (ctree), from statistics, which fix a bias in CART, and C5.0, from machine learning, the last of a line of algorithms (ID3, C4.5, C5.0) by the computer scientist Ross Quinlan.

Three families of trees, three ways of thinking: CART asks “which split purifies most?”, CHAID and ctree ask “which input is most significant?”, and C5.0 asks “which split gains the most information?” Let’s see where that leads.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • show by simulation that CART prefers inputs with many possible splits
  • grow a conditional inference tree with partykit::ctree(), and read its permutation tests
  • see ctree’s significance-based stopping, and change its threshold
  • compute C5.0’s gain ratio by hand, and read and check a C5.0 rule set
  • see boosted C5.0 beat every single tree on the validation set

CART’s selection bias

CART tries every split of every input and picks the best. An input with many ways to split has many chances to look good by luck. A numeric input with 300 distinct values has 299 candidate thresholds; a 20-level factor has 2^{19} - 1 = 524{,}287 possible groupings.

The setup code makes Lesson 1’s partition, and defines auc().

Your turn. Simulate 200 datasets in which the target is pure noise and there are three inputs, all noise too: a numeric one, a binary one and a 20-level factor. Grow a one-split CART tree on each, and count how often each input is chosen.

One split: maxdepth = 1.

set.seed(8071)
pick <- replicate(200, {
  d <- data.frame(y = factor(sample(c("a", "b"), 300, replace = TRUE)),
                  x_num = rnorm(300),
                  x_bin = factor(sample(c("u", "v"), 300, replace = TRUE)),
                  x_cat = factor(sample(LETTERS[1:20], 300, replace = TRUE)))
  fit <- rpart(y ~ ., data = d, control = rpart.control(cp = 0, maxdepth = 1, minbucket = 5))
  if (nrow(fit$frame) > 1) as.character(fit$frame$var[1]) else "none"
})
table(pick)

The 20-level factor is chosen 191 times out of 200, the numeric input 5 times, and the binary input never (4 trees didn’t split at all). All three are equally useless; a fair method would pick each about a third of the time. In real data, that means a high-cardinality input, like a customer’s postcode or the sales rep, gets into the tree, and high up, whether it matters or not. That’s variable selection bias.

Your turn. Count the candidate binary splits of each input in one such dataset.

A factor with k levels has 2^{k-1} - 1 binary splits.

set.seed(8071)
x_num <- rnorm(300)
c(numeric = length(unique(x_num)) - 1, binary = 2^(2 - 1) - 1, factor_20 = 2^(20 - 1) - 1)

299, 1 and 524,287. (CART’s ordering trick checks only 19 of the factor’s splits, but they’re the 19 best of all 524,287, so the factor gets the benefit of the whole search.) The binary input has one chance; the factor, half a million. The impurity decrease of the best split grows with the number of chances, so the comparison between inputs isn’t fair.

cart_rep <- rpart(churned ~ ., data = train, method = "class")

The sales rep is now the second most important input (64.6), ahead of the volume change and the price, and the validation AUC is 0.743. Three of the 60 reps really do lose customers, so there’s some signal, but CART’s importance is wildly inflated by the search: a grouping of 60 reps into “good” and “bad” can always be found that fits the training data well, and most of it is noise. Conditional inference trees

Torsten Hothorn, Kurt Hornik and Achim Zeileis’s conditional inference trees (2006) separate the two questions CART answers at once:

  1. Which input? At each node, test the association between each input and the target with a permutation test, which gives a p-value that’s comparable across inputs of every type and number of levels. Adjust the p-values for the number of inputs (Bonferroni, by default). Pick the input with the smallest adjusted p-value.
  2. Where to split it? Only then, for the chosen input, find the best binary split.

If no input’s adjusted p-value is below the threshold (by default, $1 - $ mincriterion = 0.05), the node isn’t split. Like CHAID, it stops by significance and doesn’t prune. On the same 200 noise datasets, a ctree forced to split at the root picks the numeric input 71 times, the binary one 69 times and the 20-level factor 60 times: about equally often, as a fair method should. With its default threshold, it splits only 8 of the 200 datasets at all (4%, close to the 5% a 0.05 test should give), which is the right answer for noise.

Your turn. Grow a conditional inference tree on the churn data, with all the inputs, sales reps included. Count its leaves, and find its validation AUC.

The churn probability is the "yes" column.

set.seed(8072)
ct <- ctree(churned ~ ., data = train)
print(ct)
c(leaves = length(nodeids(ct, terminal = TRUE)),
  auc = auc(predict(ct, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"], valid$churned))

14 leaves, a validation AUC of 0.792, the best single tree so far, and no sales reps anywhere in it. The tree also found something CART missed: under the late-delivery split, it splits on the price index, then, for accounts priced above the market, on the technical service contract, which is the interaction built into the data. It uses the satisfaction score several times, and it splits once on the payment terms, deep in the tree, which is probably noise: a significance test at 5% will pass a useless split now and then. The set.seed() matters here for two reasons. For some inputs, ctree computes its p-values with a randomized algorithm; and by default ctree has no surrogate splits, so an account whose satisfaction score is missing is sent down a random branch when it’s predicted. (ctree_control(maxsurrogate = 3) turns surrogates on, but ctree can’t search surrogate splits on a factor with 31 or more levels, like the sales reps.) That’s why the exercises predict right after fitting, with nothing random in between.

Your turn. Look at the permutation tests at the root: each input’s test statistic and p-value.

The test results are in the node’s criterion element.

set.seed(8072)
ct <- ctree(churned ~ ., data = train)
crit <- info_node(node_party(ct))$criterion
signif(sort(crit["p.value", ]), 3)

The late deliveries are by far the most significant (p = 8 \times 10^{-43}), then the satisfaction, the complaints, the price index and the volume change. The sales rep’s p-value is 1: tested fairly, as one input with 59 degrees of freedom, its association with churn is no stronger than chance would give. (These p-values are already Bonferroni-adjusted for the 13 inputs.) The real effect of three bad reps is too diluted among 60 to show; that’s the other side of a fair test.

Your turn. ctree’s mincriterion is 1 - \alpha. Grow the tree with mincriterion = 0.99 and 0.90, and compare their leaves and validation AUCs.

Use the function’s argument: mincriterion = m.

sapply(c(0.99, 0.95, 0.90), function(m) {
  set.seed(8072)
  fit <- ctree(churned ~ ., data = train, control = ctree_control(mincriterion = m))
  c(mincriterion = m, leaves = length(nodeids(fit, terminal = TRUE)),
    auc = auc(predict(fit, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"], valid$churned))
})

11, 14 and 15 leaves, with validation AUCs of 0.783, 0.792 and 0.797. The threshold acts like CART’s complexity parameter, but in units anyone can understand: “split only when the evidence is significant at 1%”. The top of the tree is the same at every threshold, because the strong effects pass any of them; the threshold only decides how many weak splits are added at the bottom.

C5.0

Quinlan’s C5.0 grows trees by information gain ratio, an entropy-based criterion divided by the split’s own entropy, which penalizes splits into many small groups. It splits categorical inputs into one branch per level (or groups of levels), prunes by an error-based estimate rather than cross-validation, and has two extras: it can convert its tree into a compact rule set, and it has boosting built in.

The entropy of a node, in bits, is H = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k, where p_k is the share of class k: 0 for a pure node, 1 for a 50/50 node. A split’s information gain is the parent’s entropy minus the children’s average entropy, weighted by their sizes. Its split information is the entropy of the split itself, -\sum_j w_j \log_2 w_j, where w_j is the share of accounts sent to branch j: it grows with the number of branches. The gain ratio is the gain divided by the split information.

Your turn. Compute the gain, the split information and the gain ratio of three splits of the training accounts: late deliveries (3 or more against fewer), the segment (8 branches) and the sales rep (60 branches).

The split information is the entropy of the split itself: of x, not of y.

H <- function(y) {                                         # entropy, in bits
  p <- prop.table(table(y)); p <- p[p > 0]
  -sum(p * log2(p))
}
gain_ratio <- function(x, y) {
  w <- prop.table(table(x))                                # the branch shares
  gain <- H(y) - sum(w * tapply(y, x, H))
  split_info <- H(x)
  c(gain = gain, split_info = split_info, ratio = gain / split_info)
}
round(rbind(late_3plus = gain_ratio(train$late_deliveries >= 3, train$churned),
            segment = gain_ratio(train$segment, train$churned),
            sales_rep = gain_ratio(train$sales_rep, train$churned)), 4)

The late-delivery split gains 0.083 bits, with a split information of 0.56, so a gain ratio of 0.150: far ahead of the others. Look at the other two. On gain alone, the 60 sales reps (0.0223) look three times as good as the 8 segments (0.0074), because 60 small groups are purer by chance: the same bias as CART’s. Their split information is 5.9 bits against 2.9, and dividing by it shrinks the reps’ advantage to 0.0038 against 0.0025. The gain ratio reduces the bias, but doesn’t remove it; ctree’s tests are the cleaner fix.

A tree with 6 leaves, and a validation AUC of 0.688, much like CART’s default tree (0.690). C5.0’s pruning is aggressive on these data; summary(c5) prints the tree, and C5.0Control(CF = 0.5) prunes less.

Six rules. Each shows how many training accounts it covers and how many it gets wrong (48/12: 48 covered, 12 wrong), its lift (how many times the overall churn rate its accounts have), and its confidence in brackets. The strongest “yes” rule, Rule 4: a price more than 1.048 times the market and a volume drop of 15% or more. Unlike a tree’s leaves, rules can overlap; an account can match several, and they vote. Rule sets are often shorter and easier to act on than the tree they came from: these six could go on one slide for the sales meeting.

Your turn. Check Rule 4 on the training accounts: how many it covers, what share of them churned, and its lift, that share divided by the overall churn rate.

A volume change of -15 or less.

covered <- train$price_index > 1.048 & train$volume_change_pct <= -15
rate <- mean(train$churned[covered] == "yes")
c(covered = sum(covered), churn_rate = rate, lift = rate / mean(train$churned == "yes"))

48 accounts, of which 36 churned (75%), against 19.8% overall: a lift of 3.79. C5.0 reports 3.7 and a confidence of 0.740 because it uses a cautious estimate of the rate, (36 + 1) / (48 + 2) = 0.74 (the Laplace correction), which pulls small rules toward 50%. A rule is just a filter on the data, so anyone with a spreadsheet can check it, which is part of its appeal.

C5.0’s trials argument grows a sequence of trees, each focusing on the accounts the previous ones got wrong, and lets them vote. That’s boosting, the subject of Lesson 13. Here it is with 10 trials:

c5b <- C5.0(churned ~ . - sales_rep, data = train, trials = 10)
c(trials_used = c5b$trials[["Actual"]], auc = auc(predict(c5b, valid, type = "prob")[, "yes"], valid$churned))
#> trials_used         auc 
#>   7.0000000   0.8185515

A validation AUC of 0.819, far above any single tree. C5.0 stopped after 7 of the 10 trials, because the later trees were no longer improving the training fit. A committee of small trees ranks accounts much more finely than one tree can, which is the idea behind every ensemble in Lessons 12 and 13. The price: no single tree or rule set to read any more.

NoteGo deeper: how ctree’s tests work

For an input X and target Y, ctree computes a linear statistic T = \sum_i g(X_i) h(Y_i)^\top, where g and h code the variables (indicator columns for factors, the values themselves for numbers). Under the null hypothesis that X and Y are independent, every permutation of Y against X is equally likely, and Strasser and Weber (1999) gave the exact mean and covariance of T over all permutations. A standardized quadratic form of T is then approximately chi-squared, with degrees of freedom equal to the dimension of T: 1 for a numeric input with a binary target, 59 for the sales reps. Because every input’s p-value refers to its own degrees of freedom, a 60-level factor gets no free advantage. The framework covers numeric, categorical, censored and multivariate targets with the same machinery. Hothorn, Hornik and Zeileis, “Unbiased Recursive Partitioning: A Conditional Inference Framework”, Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics 15(3), 2006, is the reference.

0.690 for CART, 0.743 for CART with the reps and 0.792 for ctree; add C5.0’s 0.688 and boosted C5.0’s 0.819 from above (Lesson 5’s pruned CART tree had 0.767). Among single trees, ctree wins here, with an honest test for every split; among everything, the ensemble wins. Single trees are all within a few points of each other once they’re the right size, and which wins depends on the data. The real jump comes from combining trees.

Wrap-up

You’ve met the other two families of trees. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Selection bias: on noise, CART picked a 20-level factor 191 times out of 200; on churn, it promoted the 60 sales reps to the second most important input.
  • Conditional inference trees choose inputs by permutation tests with adjusted p-values, then choose the split, and stop when nothing is significant. On churn: 14 leaves, no sales reps, the price-by-service interaction found, and a validation AUC of 0.792.
  • mincriterion sets the significance threshold: 11 to 15 leaves between 0.99 and 0.90.
  • C5.0 grows trees by gain ratio, which penalizes many-branch splits (but doesn’t remove their advantage), writes rule sets (6 rules here) that anyone can check, and boosts: 0.688 as a single tree, 0.819 boosted. It runs in RStudio, not in the browser.
  • The lesson: right-sized single trees are all similar; combining trees is what makes the big difference.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Variable selection bias
A tree’s tendency to choose inputs with many possible splits, whether or not they matter.
Conditional inference tree (ctree)
A tree that chooses inputs by permutation tests of independence, then chooses the split, and stops when nothing is significant.
Permutation test
A test that compares a statistic with its distribution over all permutations of the data under the null hypothesis.
mincriterion
In ctree, one minus the significance threshold for splitting.
C5.0
Quinlan’s tree algorithm, using the information gain ratio, with rule sets and boosting.
Information gain ratio
The decrease in entropy from a split, divided by the entropy of the split itself.
Rule set
A list of if-then rules, possibly overlapping, extracted from a tree.
