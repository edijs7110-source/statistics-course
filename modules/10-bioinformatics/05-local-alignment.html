Pairwise alignment II: local alignment and scoring

Finding the part that matches, and knowing when it means something

Most related proteins share a domain, not their whole length. Find the best local alignment with the Smith-Waterman algorithm, by hand and with pwalign. Then look inside the scores: what a BLOSUM62 entry means, how to estimate one from aligned families, and the statistical question every alignment raises: could a score this high happen by chance? Answer it by shuffling, with an extreme-value (Gumbel) distribution, and with E-values and bit scores.

Module 10 · Lesson 5 of 13 Week 151 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Global alignment assumes two sequences correspond from end to end. Often they don’t: two proteins may share one domain and nothing else, or a gene may be compared with a whole genome. Local alignment finds the best-matching parts. And it raises a question that global alignment hides: every pair of sequences has a best local alignment, even two random ones. When is a score high enough to mean the proteins are related?

Today’s statistics is the reason BLAST works: the distribution of the best of many random scores is not normal, but it’s known, and it’s beautiful.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • compute a Smith-Waterman local alignment by hand, and with pwalign
  • read and estimate log-odds substitution scores like BLOSUM62’s
  • test a score’s significance by shuffling one sequence
  • fit an extreme-value (Gumbel) distribution to the shuffled scores
  • turn scores into p-values, E-values and bit scores

Smith-Waterman

Temple Smith and Michael Waterman (1981) changed one thing in Needleman-Wunsch: a cell can’t go below zero,

F(i, j) = \max\{0,\ F(i-1, j-1) + s(x_i, y_j),\ F(i-1, j) + g,\ F(i, j-1) + g\},

so a bad region “resets” the alignment instead of dragging it down. The first row and column are all zeros, the best score is the largest cell anywhere in the matrix, and the traceback starts there and stops at the first zero. For this to find local regions, the expected score of aligning two random letters must be negative; otherwise long random alignments would always win.

The setup code reads the family and pwalign’s BLOSUM matrices.

Your turn. Compute the Smith-Waterman matrix for the classic textbook pair HEAGAWGHEE and PAWHEAE (Durbin and others, 1998), with BLOSUM50 and a gap score of -8, and find the best local score.

The floor is 0.

x <- strsplit("HEAGAWGHEE", "")[[1]]; y <- strsplit("PAWHEAE", "")[[1]]
g <- -8
F <- matrix(0, length(x) + 1, length(y) + 1)              # zeros in the first row and column
for (i in 2:(length(x) + 1)) for (j in 2:(length(y) + 1))
  F[i, j] <- max(0, F[i - 1, j - 1] + BLOSUM50[x[i - 1], y[j - 1]], F[i - 1, j] + g, F[i, j - 1] + g)
c(best = max(F), row = which(F == max(F), arr.ind = TRUE)[1, 1] - 1, col = which(F == max(F), arr.ind = TRUE)[1, 2] - 1)

A best score of 28, ending at the 9th letter of HEAGAWGHEE (the second-to-last E) and the 5th of PAWHEAE (its E): the local alignment AWGHE over AW-HE. The rest of each sequence is simply left out.

Where the scores come from

A substitution score is a log-odds ratio:

s(a, b) = \frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{q_{ab}}{p_a\,p_b},

where q_{ab} is the frequency with which a and b are aligned in true alignments of related proteins, and p_a p_b the frequency expected by chance. Positive scores mean “aligned more often than chance”. BLOSUM62 (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1992) estimated q_{ab} from conserved blocks of proteins clustered at 62% identity, and reports scores in half-bits (\lambda = \ln 2 / 2), rounded to integers. PAM matrices (Dayhoff, 1978) were built instead from close alignments and extrapolated to more distant ones by matrix powers: PAM250 for distant proteins, PAM30 for close ones. With BLOSUM, a higher number is for closer proteins.

Your turn. Estimate log-odds scores yourself from the aligned KRED family: count how often each pair of residues occurs in the same column (over all pairs of sequences), compare with chance, and convert to half-bits. Compare a few with BLOSUM62.

Round to 2 decimals.

al <- as.matrix(readAAStringSet("data/kred_family_aligned.fasta"))   # 25 x columns
aa <- c("A", "R", "N", "D", "C", "Q", "E", "G", "H", "I", "L", "K", "M", "F", "P", "S", "T", "W", "Y", "V")
Q <- matrix(0, 20, 20, dimnames = list(aa, aa))
for (k in seq_len(ncol(al))) {
  col <- al[, k]; col <- col[col %in% aa]
  tab <- table(factor(col, levels = aa)); Q <- Q + outer(tab, tab) - diag(as.numeric(tab))   # pairs in this column
}
q <- Q / sum(Q); pa <- rowSums(q)
s_est <- 2 * log2(q / outer(pa, pa))                                  # half-bits
round(c(LL = s_est["L", "L"], LI = s_est["L", "I"], LD = s_est["L", "D"], cor_with_blosum = cor(as.vector(s_est[is.finite(s_est)]), as.vector(BLOSUM62[aa, aa][is.finite(s_est)]))), 2)

From the family’s own alignment: L with L 5.2, L with I 0.1, L with D -3.1, and overall the estimated scores correlate 0.75 with BLOSUM62. They’re noisier (some pairs are rare in 25 sequences), and they’re specific to this family’s degree of divergence. That’s what BLOSUM did on a large scale, from thousands of conserved blocks.

Is the score significant?

The most distant family member, SDR_24, aligns locally with PL7_KRED with some score. Is that more than two unrelated proteins of the same lengths and compositions would get? The simplest answer is a permutation test: shuffle one sequence many times (keeping its composition, destroying its order), align each shuffle, and see where the real score falls.

Your turn. Align PL7_KRED with SDR_24, then with 200 shuffles of SDR_24.

200 shuffles.

target <- fam[[25]]
sw <- function(s) score(pairwiseAlignment(fam[[1]], s, type = "local", substitutionMatrix = "BLOSUM62", gapOpening = 10, gapExtension = 1))
observed <- sw(target)
set.seed(10052)
shuffled <- replicate(200, sw(AAString(paste(sample(strsplit(as.character(target), "")[[1]]), collapse = ""))))
c(observed = observed, max_shuffled = max(shuffled), mean = mean(shuffled), sd = sd(shuffled),
  p_empirical = (sum(shuffled >= observed) + 1) / (200 + 1))

The real score is 344; the 200 shuffles score at most 51, with a mean of 32 and an SD of 5.2. None comes close: the empirical p-value is 1/201 = 0.005, the smallest that 200 shuffles can give. SDR_24 shares only about 35% of its residues with PL7_KRED, but that’s far beyond chance.

A permutation p-value can’t go below 1/(n+1), so to say how significant a score of several hundred is, we need the distribution’s shape. The best local alignment score is a maximum over many possible alignments, and maxima don’t follow the normal distribution: they follow an extreme-value distribution. For local alignment scores, Karlin and Altschul (1990) proved it’s the Gumbel distribution:

P(S \ge x) \approx 1 - \exp\left(-e^{-\lambda(x - \mu)}\right),

with a long right tail. Its mean is \mu + 0.5772/\lambda and its standard deviation \pi/(\lambda\sqrt6), so the parameters can be estimated from a sample’s mean and standard deviation (the method of moments).

Your turn. Fit the Gumbel distribution to the shuffled scores, and compute the p-value of the observed score.

The standard deviation is \pi/(\lambda\sqrt 6).

target <- fam[[25]]
sw <- function(s) score(pairwiseAlignment(fam[[1]], s, type = "local", substitutionMatrix = "BLOSUM62", gapOpening = 10, gapExtension = 1))
observed <- sw(target)
set.seed(10052)
shuffled <- replicate(200, sw(AAString(paste(sample(strsplit(as.character(target), "")[[1]]), collapse = ""))))
lambda <- pi / (sd(shuffled) * sqrt(6))
mu <- mean(shuffled) - 0.5772 / lambda
p_gumbel <- function(x) 1 - exp(-exp(-lambda * (x - mu)))
c(lambda = lambda, mu = mu, p_at_60 = p_gumbel(60), log10_p_observed = -lambda * (observed - mu) / log(10))   # the tail, on the log scale

\lambda = 0.248 and \mu = 29.9. A score of 60 already has a p-value of 0.0006, and the observed 344 has \log_{10} p = -34. For large scores, P \approx e^{-\lambda(x - \mu)}, which the code computes on the log scale, because the exact formula rounds to 0 in floating point. The histogram below shows the fit.

E-values and bit scores

In a database search, a query is compared with many sequences, and some random hit will score well. The E-value is the expected number of hits with a score at least S by chance, in a search of this size. Karlin and Altschul’s formula for a query of length m against a database of total length n is

E = K\,m\,n\,e^{-\lambda S},

with constants \lambda and K that depend on the scoring system. For one comparison, E \approx P when both are small; for a database of N comparable sequences, E \approx N \times P. An E-value below about 0.001 is strong evidence of homology; near 1, a hit is what chance alone would give. The bit score S' = (\lambda S - \ln K)/\ln 2 normalizes a raw score so that E = m\,n\,2^{-S'}, comparable between scoring systems.

Your turn. Using the Gumbel fit, find the score that would have an E-value of 0.001 in a search of 600 sequences like these, and of 250 million (the size of UniProt).

Divide by lambda.

target <- fam[[25]]
sw <- function(s) score(pairwiseAlignment(fam[[1]], s, type = "local", substitutionMatrix = "BLOSUM62", gapOpening = 10, gapExtension = 1))
set.seed(10052)
shuffled <- replicate(200, sw(AAString(paste(sample(strsplit(as.character(target), "")[[1]]), collapse = ""))))
lambda <- pi / (sd(shuffled) * sqrt(6)); mu <- mean(shuffled) - 0.5772 / lambda
score_for_E <- function(E, N) mu - log(-log(1 - E / N)) / lambda         # solve N * P(S >= x) = E for x
c(db600 = score_for_E(0.001, 600), uniprot = score_for_E(0.001, 2.5e8))

For an E-value of 0.001, a score of 84 is needed among 600 sequences, and 136 among UniProt’s 250 million. The bigger the database, the higher the bar: the same alignment can be convincing in a small search and borderline in a large one.

Your turn. Estimate K from the fit (\mu = \ln(Kmn)/\lambda, with m and n the two sequences’ lengths), and compute the observed alignment’s bit score.

Bits: divide by \ln 2.

target <- fam[[25]]
sw <- function(s) score(pairwiseAlignment(fam[[1]], s, type = "local", substitutionMatrix = "BLOSUM62", gapOpening = 10, gapExtension = 1))
observed <- sw(target)
set.seed(10052)
shuffled <- replicate(200, sw(AAString(paste(sample(strsplit(as.character(target), "")[[1]]), collapse = ""))))
lambda <- pi / (sd(shuffled) * sqrt(6)); mu <- mean(shuffled) - 0.5772 / lambda
m <- length(fam[[1]]); n <- length(target)
K <- exp(lambda * mu) / (m * n)
c(K = K, bits = (lambda * observed - log(K)) / log(2))

K = 0.026 and a bit score of 128. With bits, E = mn\,2^{-S'}: here about 252 \times 250 \times 2^{-128}, around 10^{-34}, the same conclusion in the currency BLAST reports.

NoteGo deeper: why the Gumbel, and why it’s only approximate with gaps

For ungapped local alignments, Karlin and Altschul (1990) proved that the maximal segment score has, asymptotically, a Gumbel distribution, with \lambda the unique positive solution of \sum_{a,b} p_a p_b e^{\lambda s(a,b)} = 1 (which exists exactly when the expected score is negative and some score is positive), and K given by a more complicated formula. The intuition: an alignment’s running score is a random walk with negative drift; a high-scoring segment is a rare excursion upward, and the maximum of many nearly independent excursions, each with an exponential tail, is Gumbel. With gaps there’s no proof, but simulations show the Gumbel form still holds, and BLAST uses \lambda and K estimated by simulation for each scoring system. Also, \lambda connects to the matrix: for BLOSUM62 in half-bits, the ungapped \lambda is close to \ln 2/2 = 0.347. Samuel Karlin and Stephen Altschul, “Methods for assessing the statistical significance of molecular sequence features by using general scoring schemes”, PNAS 87, 1990, pages 2264 to 2268.

Quick quiz

1. What makes Smith-Waterman local?

A bad region resets the score instead of penalizing the whole alignment.

2. A positive BLOSUM62 score for a pair of amino acids means:

It’s a log-odds ratio of observed to expected frequency.

3. Why must the expected score of two random residues be negative for local alignment?

With negative drift, only real similarity produces high-scoring segments.

4. Local alignment scores of unrelated sequences follow:

The score is a maximum over many possible alignments.

5. An E-value of 0.5 means:

E-values depend on the database size.

6. For aligning two distant proteins, which matrix is usually better?

Lower BLOSUM numbers come from more divergent blocks.

Wrap-up

You’ve found local similarities and judged them. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Smith-Waterman adds a zero floor to the recurrence; the textbook pair scores 28 (AWGHE over AW-HE), and a hidden domain is found where global alignment fails.
  • Substitution scores are log-odds ratios in half-bits; the family’s own estimates correlate 0.75 with BLOSUM62; the expected random score is negative.
  • Significance: shuffling gives a null distribution, which is Gumbel; PL7_KRED against its most distant relative is far beyond any shuffle.
  • E-values (E = Kmn\,e^{-\lambda S}) and bit scores put scores on a common, database-aware scale.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Local alignment
An alignment of the best-matching parts of two sequences.
Smith-Waterman algorithm
Dynamic programming for optimal local alignment, with a floor of zero.
Log-odds score
The logarithm of the ratio of the observed pairing frequency to the frequency expected by chance.
BLOSUM and PAM matrices
Families of amino-acid substitution matrices for different evolutionary distances.
Permutation (shuffle) test
Comparing a score with scores of shuffled sequences that keep the composition but destroy the order.
Gumbel (extreme-value) distribution
The distribution of maxima, which local alignment scores of random sequences follow.
E-value
The expected number of chance hits with at least a given score in a search.
Bit score
A normalized score, independent of the scoring system’s scale.
