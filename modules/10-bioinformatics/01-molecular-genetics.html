DNA as data

DNA is a chain of four kinds of nucleotides, named by their bases: A (adenine), C (cytosine), G (guanine) and T (thymine). Two chains wind together into the double helix, with A always paired to T and C to G, so each strand determines the other. The two strands run in opposite directions, conventionally labelled 5′ to 3′; sequences are written 5′ to 3′.

A gene is a stretch of DNA that’s copied (transcribed) into RNA; for most genes, the RNA is then translated into a protein, three bases (a codon) per amino acid. That’s the central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein. RNA uses U (uracil) instead of T.

The setup code reads a 3,000-base fragment of the PL-7 genome from a FASTA file (a text format: a header line starting with >, then the sequence) using nothing but base R, and builds the genetic code: a named vector from the 64 codons to the 20 amino acids (one-letter codes) and * for the three stop codons.

Your turn. How long is the fragment, and how many of each base does it have?

Tabulate bases.

bases <- strsplit(fragment, "")[[1]]              # one letter per element
c(length = length(bases), table(bases))

3,000 bases: 543 A, 934 C, 964 G and 559 T. The counts of A and T are close, and so are those of C and G. Within a single strand they don’t have to be (only the two strands together are exactly balanced), but in long sequences they usually are, a regularity known as Chargaff’s second rule.

Your turn. The GC content, the share of G and C, varies a lot between organisms (from about 25% to 75% in bacteria) and between regions of one genome. Compute it.

G or C.

bases <- strsplit(fragment, "")[[1]]
mean(bases %in% c("G", "C"))

63.3%: PL-7’s genome is GC-rich, like other Rhodococcus species. A G-C pair is held by three hydrogen bonds and an A-T pair by two, so GC-rich DNA is more stable, and the GC content shapes which codons an organism uses, as you’ll see below.

The other strand

The sequence in the file is one strand. The other strand is its reverse complement: swap A↔︎T and C↔︎G (the complement), then reverse the order (because the strands run in opposite directions, and we always write 5′ to 3′). Genes can lie on either strand.

Your turn. Write a function for the reverse complement with chartr() (character translation) and rev(), and apply it to the first 12 bases.

A becomes T, C becomes G, G becomes C, T becomes A: "TGCA".

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
c(forward = substr(fragment, 1, 12), reverse_complement = revcomp(substr(fragment, 1, 12)))

GCTGTGGGCCAA becomes TTGGCCCACAGC: read backwards, with every base swapped. Applying revcomp() twice gives the original back, a quick check worth making whenever you write sequence code.

From gene to protein

The genome annotation says the kred gene is at complement(1101..1859): positions 1,101 to 1,859, on the reverse strand. To read it, take those bases and reverse-complement them. The result is the gene’s coding strand, which has the same sequence as its messenger RNA, with T for U.

Your turn. Extract the gene, and check how it starts and ends.

The gene ends at 1,859.

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
kred <- revcomp(substr(fragment, 1101, 1859))
mrna <- chartr("T", "U", kred)                      # transcription: T becomes U
c(length = nchar(kred), first = substr(mrna, 1, 15), last = substr(mrna, nchar(mrna) - 2, nchar(mrna)))

759 bases, starting with AUG and ending with UGA. 759 is a multiple of 3: 253 codons.

Your turn. Split the gene into codons with substring(), and count them.

A codon is 3 bases.

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
kred <- revcomp(substr(fragment, 1101, 1859))
starts <- seq(1, nchar(kred), by = 3)
cod <- substring(kred, starts, starts + 2)
c(n_codons = length(cod), first = cod[1], last = cod[length(cod)], n_stops = sum(genetic_code[cod] == "*"))

253 codons: ATG, the start codon (which also codes for methionine), then 251 more, and TGA, a stop codon, at the end, and no other stop in between. A stretch from a start codon to the next stop in the same frame, with no stop in between, is an open reading frame (ORF); long ORFs are how gene-finding programs spot genes in bacterial genomes.

Your turn. Translate the gene with the genetic code, and look at the protein.

Look up each codon: genetic_code[cod].

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
kred <- revcomp(substr(fragment, 1101, 1859))
cod <- substring(kred, seq(1, nchar(kred), 3), seq(3, nchar(kred), 3))
protein <- paste(genetic_code[cod], collapse = "")
c(length = nchar(protein), start = substr(protein, 1, 25), motif_at = regexpr("TG...G.G", protein))

253 characters: 252 amino acids, then * for the stop. The protein starts MCRKHPMKESG, and the regular expression TG...G.G (a dot matches any amino acid) finds the cofactor-binding motif TGAASGIG at position 12. That’s the signature of the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR) family, where the enzyme binds its NADPH cofactor; Lesson 7 shows that it’s conserved in every member of the family.

Codon usage

The genetic code is degenerate: 61 codons for 20 amino acids, so most amino acids have two to six codons, usually differing at the third position. Organisms don’t use synonymous codons equally, and a GC-rich genome like PL-7’s favours G and C at the third position, where the choice doesn’t change the protein.

Your turn. Compute the GC content at each of the three codon positions.

Group by pos.

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
kred <- revcomp(substr(fragment, 1101, 1859))
x <- strsplit(kred, "")[[1]]
pos <- rep(1:3, length.out = length(x))
round(tapply(x %in% c("G", "C"), pos, mean), 3)

47.4% at the first position, 42.7% at the second and 82.6% at the third. The first two positions mostly decide the amino acid, so their composition follows the protein; the third is mostly free, and there the genome’s GC preference shows almost unchecked.

Mutations

A point mutation changes one base. If the new codon codes for the same amino acid, the mutation is synonymous (silent); if not, it’s non-synonymous (missense), or nonsense if it creates a stop codon. The share of possible changes that are synonymous matters for measuring selection: a gene under strong selection to keep its protein accumulates synonymous changes faster than non-synonymous ones.

Your turn. For every codon of the gene (without the stop), try all 9 possible single-base changes, and classify them.

Compare with the original codon, cd.

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
kred <- revcomp(substr(fragment, 1101, 1859))
cod <- substring(kred, seq(1, 756, 3), seq(3, 756, 3))                 # 252 codons, no stop
kind <- unlist(lapply(cod, function(cd) {
  unlist(lapply(1:3, function(p) sapply(setdiff(c("A", "C", "G", "T"), substr(cd, p, p)), function(nb) {
    new <- cd; substr(new, p, p) <- nb
    if (genetic_code[new] == "*") "nonsense" else if (genetic_code[new] == genetic_code[cd]) "synonymous" else "missense"
  })))
}))
round(prop.table(table(kind)), 3)

21.6% synonymous, 74.3% missense and 4.1% nonsense, and almost all the synonymous changes are at third positions. When genes are compared between species, the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous differences, each per possible site (called dN/dS, or ω), is the classic measure of selection: well below 1 for a gene whose protein is under constraint.

Your turn. A frameshift, an insertion or deletion of a number of bases that isn’t a multiple of 3, shifts every codon after it. Delete the 301st base of the gene, translate, and find the first stop codon.

Continue from base 302.

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
kred <- revcomp(substr(fragment, 1101, 1859))
mut <- paste0(substr(kred, 1, 300), substr(kred, 302, nchar(kred)))   # skip base 301
cod <- substring(mut, seq(1, nchar(mut) - 2, 3), seq(3, nchar(mut), 3))
p <- genetic_code[cod]
c(first_stop = which(p == "*")[1], same_until = which(p != genetic_code[substring(kred, seq(1, 756, 3), seq(3, 756, 3))][seq_along(p)])[1])

The protein is unchanged up to codon 100; every amino acid from codon 101 on is different, and a stop codon appears at codon 159. The result is a truncated protein of 158 residues, the last 58 of them scrambled, almost certainly inactive. That’s why a frameshift in an enzyme gene is a red flag (the project has one).

NoteGo deeper: what the central dogma leaves out

Real genes are messier. In bacteria, genes are mostly continuous ORFs, often grouped in operons transcribed together; in eukaryotes, genes are split into exons separated by introns, which are spliced out of the RNA, and one gene can be spliced several ways into different proteins. Many genes make RNA that’s never translated (ribosomal RNA, which Lessons 8 and 9 use for phylogenetics, transfer RNA, and many regulatory RNAs). Translation starts at a start codon downstream of a ribosome-binding site (in bacteria, the Shine-Dalgarno sequence, AGGAGG, about 8 bases before the start). Some organisms use slightly different genetic codes (mitochondria, for instance). And proteins are modified after translation. Bruce Alberts and others’ Molecular Biology of the Cell (7th edition, Norton, 2022), chapters 4 to 7, is the standard account.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Six reading frames. A DNA sequence can be read in three frames on each strand. Count the stop codons in each of the six frames of the gene region (positions 1,101 to 1,859).

The reverse frames read revcomp(region).

revcomp <- function(s) paste(rev(strsplit(chartr("ACGT", "TGCA", s), "")[[1]]), collapse = "")
region <- substr(fragment, 1101, 1859)
stops_in <- function(s, offset) {
  st <- seq(1 + offset, nchar(s) - 2, by = 3)
  sum(genetic_code[substring(s, st, st + 2)] == "*")
}
c(forward = sapply(0:2, function(o) stops_in(region, o)),
  reverse = sapply(0:2, function(o) stops_in(revcomp(region), o)))

The gene’s own frame (reverse strand, first frame) has a single stop, at the very end; the other five frames have 4 to 12 stops each in the same 759 bases. In random sequence, a stop appears on average every 21 codons (3 of the 64), a little less often in GC-rich DNA, where the AT-rich stop codons are rarer. So an open frame of 252 codons is very unlikely by chance, and that’s the statistical basis of bacterial gene finding.

Quick quiz

1. The complement of the DNA sequence 5′-ATGC-3′, written 5′ to 3′, is:

Complement (TACG), then reverse, because the strands run in opposite directions.

2. A codon is:

64 codons, 20 amino acids and stop.

3. The gene is at complement(1101..1859). To read it 5′ to 3′:

“complement” means the gene is on the other strand.

4. Why is the GC content highest at the third codon position in PL-7’s genes?

The degeneracy of the code is mostly at the third position.

5. A mutation that changes a codon into a stop codon is:

It truncates the protein.

6. Deleting one base inside a gene:

A frameshift; deleting three bases would remove one amino acid instead.

Wrap-up

You’ve turned a genome fragment into a protein. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • DNA as a string: 3,000 bases of A, C, G and T, with 63% GC; the other strand is the reverse complement.
  • From gene to protein: the kred gene, on the reverse strand at 1,101 to 1,859, is 759 bases: 252 codons from ATG plus a stop, translated through the genetic code into a 252-residue protein with the cofactor motif TGxxxGxG at position 12.
  • Codon usage: GC-rich genomes favour G and C at the third codon position.
  • Mutations: about 22% of possible point changes are synonymous; a frameshift scrambles everything after it.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Nucleotide, base
A unit of DNA or RNA; the bases are A, C, G, T (U in RNA).
Strand, reverse complement
DNA is double-stranded; the other strand, read 5′ to 3′, is the reverse complement.
Gene
A stretch of DNA transcribed into RNA, most often coding for a protein.
Central dogma
DNA is transcribed into RNA, which is translated into protein.
Codon, genetic code
Three bases specifying one amino acid or a stop; the code maps the 64 codons to amino acids.
Open reading frame (ORF)
A run of codons from a start codon to the next in-frame stop codon.
GC content
The share of G and C bases in a sequence.
Synonymous, missense, nonsense mutation
A base change that keeps the amino acid, changes it, or creates a stop codon.
Frameshift
An insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame.
