Phylogenetic trees I

Distances, UPGMA and neighbour joining

Who’s related to whom in the plant’s wastewater bioreactor? From aligned 16S rRNA genes of 14 bacteria, compute evolutionary distances (raw, Jukes-Cantor and Kimura), build trees by UPGMA and neighbour joining with ape, root them with an outgroup, and compare them with the true tree. See why UPGMA fails when one lineage evolves faster, and why distance corrections matter.

Module 10 · Lesson 8 of 13 Week 154 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The plant’s wastewater treatment unit runs on bacteria: they eat the organic waste, turn ammonia into nitrate and then into nitrogen gas, and store phosphate. Its operators care which bacteria are there, because a change in the community warns of trouble before the effluent does. Hana’s colleagues sequenced the 16S ribosomal RNA gene of 14 bacteria isolated from the bioreactor, PL-7 among them. The 16S gene is in every bacterium, changes slowly, and is the standard ruler of bacterial relationships.

A phylogenetic tree is a hypothesis about how sequences (and the organisms that carry them) descend from common ancestors. This lesson builds trees from distances; Lesson 9 builds them from the characters directly.

Trees are where bioinformatics becomes history. The data are today’s sequences; the tree is the story that explains them.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • learn the vocabulary of trees: tips, nodes, branches, clades, roots
  • compute raw distances between sequences, and correct them with the Jukes-Cantor and Kimura models
  • build trees with UPGMA and neighbour joining, and root them with an outgroup
  • measure how far a tree is from the truth with the Robinson-Foulds distance
  • see what a violated molecular clock does to UPGMA

Trees and their vocabulary

A tree’s tips (leaves) are the sequences; its internal nodes are inferred ancestors; branch lengths measure change, usually in substitutions per site. A clade is an ancestor and all its descendants. A rooted tree has a node marking the oldest ancestor, so it shows the direction of time; an unrooted tree shows only relationships. Most methods produce unrooted trees, rooted afterwards with an outgroup, a group known to have branched off first.

The ape package stores trees as phylo objects and reads and writes the Newick format, in which a tree is a nested set of parentheses: ((A:0.1,B:0.2):0.05,C:0.3);.

bioreactor_16s.fasta has 1,200 aligned positions of the 14 bacteria’s 16S genes. Because the course’s sequences are simulated, the true tree is known too (bioreactor_true_tree.nwk): a luxury real studies never have, and a way to see which methods work. The setup code reads the alignment.

print(s16)

Distances

The simplest distance is the p-distance: the share of sites at which two sequences differ. But it underestimates evolution: a site can change twice (A to G and back to A, or A to G to C), and only the net change is visible. Over long times, two random DNA sequences can’t be more than 75% different, however long ago they split.

Your turn. Compute the p-distances, and find the closest and the most distant pairs.

The p-distance is model = "raw".

p <- dist.dna(s16, model = "raw")
m <- as.matrix(p); diag(m) <- NA
c(min = min(m, na.rm = TRUE), max = max(m, na.rm = TRUE), closest = paste(rownames(which(m == min(m, na.rm = TRUE), arr.ind = TRUE)), collapse = " + "))

From 5.9% (Gordonia and Rhodococcus PL-7, close relatives) to 26.8% different. At 27% raw difference, many sites have changed more than once.

The Jukes-Cantor model (1969) assumes every base changes to each other base at the same rate. Under it, the expected number of substitutions per site behind an observed p-distance p is

d_{\text{JC}} = -\tfrac{3}{4}\ln\left(1 - \tfrac{4}{3}p\right),

always larger than p, more so for large p, and infinite at p = 0.75.

Your turn. Correct the largest p-distance by hand, and compare with dist.dna(model = "JC69").

Correct pmax.

p <- as.matrix(dist.dna(s16, model = "raw"))
jc <- as.matrix(dist.dna(s16, model = "JC69"))
pmax <- max(p)
c(p = pmax, jc_by_hand = -3 / 4 * log(1 - 4 / 3 * pmax), jc_ape = max(jc))

The largest p-distance, 0.268, corrects to 0.332 substitutions per site: about a quarter more change than the raw count shows. Your formula and ape agree exactly.

Real DNA isn’t that simple: transitions (A↔︎G, C↔︎T, between bases of the same shape) happen more often than transversions (the other eight changes). The Kimura two-parameter model (K80) allows the two rates to differ.

Your turn. Count the transitions and transversions between every pair of sequences, and compute their overall ratio.

model = "TV".

ts <- dist.dna(s16, model = "TS")                    # number of transitions, per pair
tv <- dist.dna(s16, model = "TV")                    # number of transversions
c(transitions = sum(ts), transversions = sum(tv), ratio = sum(ts) / sum(tv))

12,530 transitions and 8,875 transversions over all pairs: a ratio of 1.41, although there are twice as many kinds of transversion as of transition. Per possible change, transitions are almost three times as frequent; the Kimura model’s ratio \kappa estimates the rate ratio itself, corrected for multiple hits (the simulation used 4).

Building trees

UPGMA (unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean) is average-linkage clustering (Module 7): repeatedly join the two closest clusters, and define the distance between clusters as the average of their members’ distances. It produces a rooted tree in which every tip is the same distance from the root, an ultrametric tree. That’s correct only if all lineages evolve at the same rate: the molecular clock.

Neighbour joining (Saitou and Nei, 1987) doesn’t assume a clock. At each step, it joins the pair i, j that minimizes Q(i, j) = (n - 2) d_{ij} - \sum_k d_{ik} - \sum_k d_{jk}, which corrects each distance for how far the two sequences are from everything else, so a fast-evolving sequence isn’t mistaken for a distant one. It produces an unrooted tree, and it’s exact if the distances are exactly additive along some tree.

The Robinson-Foulds distance (dist.topo()) counts the splits (bipartitions of the tips) that are in one tree but not the other: 0 for identical topologies.

Your turn. Build a UPGMA tree from the Kimura distances, check that it’s ultrametric, and compare it with the true tree.

UPGMA is average linkage.

truth <- read.tree("data/bioreactor_true_tree.nwk")
d <- dist.dna(s16, model = "K80")
up <- as.phylo(hclust(d, method = "average"))
c(ultrametric = is.ultrametric(up), rf_to_truth = dist.topo(unroot(up), unroot(truth)))

Ultrametric, as UPGMA trees always are, and 4 splits away from the truth. The plot below shows what went wrong: the two Accumulibacter sequences, which evolve three times faster than the rest, are torn apart. Accumulibacter_B branches off alone near the root, as if it were the most distant organism of all, and Accumulibacter_A sits on its own deep branch. To UPGMA, a long branch looks like an old one.

Your turn. Build neighbour-joining trees from the raw, Jukes-Cantor and Kimura distances, and compare each with the true tree.

The function is nj.

truth <- read.tree("data/bioreactor_true_tree.nwk")
sapply(c("raw", "JC69", "K80"), function(model) {
  tr <- nj(dist.dna(s16, model = model))
  dist.topo(unroot(tr), unroot(truth))
})

With Jukes-Cantor distances, neighbour joining recovers the true tree exactly (distance 0); with raw distances it’s 2 splits off, and with the Kimura distances 2 as well, placing the Accumulibacter pair one node too deep. The internal branch that decides it is short (0.03 substitutions per site), and with 1,200 sites the data can’t pin it down reliably; Lesson 9’s bootstrap measures that uncertainty. Note that the Kimura model, which matches how the data were simulated, didn’t do better here: with this little data, sampling noise can matter more than the model.

Your turn. Root the neighbour-joining tree with the Bacteroidetes outgroup, and check that the outgroup forms a clade.

Use outgroup.

tr <- nj(dist.dna(s16, model = "JC69"))
rooted <- root(tr, outgroup = outgroup, resolve.root = TRUE)
c(rooted = is.rooted(rooted), outgroup_is_a_clade = is.monophyletic(rooted, outgroup))

Rooted, and the three Bacteroidetes form a clade. An outgroup works only if it really did branch off first; here that’s known from many other studies (the Bacteroidetes are far from both the Proteobacteria and the Actinobacteria). is.monophyletic() checks whether a set of tips forms a clade.

Your turn. Is there a molecular clock? In the rooted neighbour-joining tree, compute each tip’s distance from the root, and find the extremes.

Largest first: decreasing = TRUE.

rooted <- root(nj(dist.dna(s16, model = "JC69")), outgroup = outgroup, resolve.root = TRUE)
depth <- node.depth.edgelength(rooted)[1:Ntip(rooted)]       # distance from the root to each tip
names(depth) <- rooted$tip.label
round(sort(depth, decreasing = TRUE), 3)[1:4]

Accumulibacter_B (0.239) and Accumulibacter_A (0.222) are the farthest from the root, followed by the two Nitrospira (0.188 and 0.177). With a molecular clock, all tips would be the same distance from the root; here the Accumulibacter lineage has evolved much faster. (Polyphosphate-accumulating bacteria like Accumulibacter really are under unusual selection in wastewater plants, though the speed-up here is the simulation’s.)

Your turn. How well does each tree represent the data? Compare the distances along each tree (cophenetic()) with the Kimura distances they were built from.

The neighbour-joining tree is nt.

d <- dist.dna(s16, model = "K80")
up <- as.phylo(hclust(d, "average")); nt <- nj(d)
tips <- labels(d)
dd <- as.vector(as.dist(as.matrix(d)[tips, tips]))
c(upgma = cor(dd, as.vector(as.dist(cophenetic(up)[tips, tips]))),
  nj = cor(dd, as.vector(as.dist(cophenetic(nt)[tips, tips]))))

A correlation of 0.930 between the UPGMA tree’s distances and the data, and 0.991 for neighbour joining: NJ’s unequal branch lengths can represent the data, UPGMA’s clock can’t.

NoteGo deeper: why distances need models, and how many trees there are

Distance corrections are models of evolution in disguise. The Jukes-Cantor formula comes from a Markov chain on the four bases (Module 4’s Markov chains) with equal rates: the probability that a site differs after time t is \frac34(1 - e^{-4\alpha t}), and inverting it gives d_{\text{JC}}. Kimura’s adds a transition/transversion ratio; the general time-reversible (GTR) model allows six rates and unequal base frequencies; and gamma-distributed rates across sites (some sites nearly invariant, others fast) matter as much as the substitution model. Under-correcting distances can create long-branch attraction, where fast lineages are wrongly grouped together. As for search: the number of unrooted binary trees of n tips is (2n-5)!! = 3 \times 5 \times 7 \times \dots \times (2n-5), already 2 million for 10 tips and 8 \times 10^{21} for 20, which is why fast algorithms like neighbour joining matter. Joseph Felsenstein’s Inferring Phylogenies (Sinauer, 2004), chapters 3, 11 and 13, is the standard reference.

The most recent common ancestor of PL-7 and "Mycobacterium".

rooted <- root(nj(dist.dna(s16, model = "JC69")), outgroup = outgroup, resolve.root = TRUE)

Gordonia, Mycobacterium and Rhodococcus PL-7: the three Actinobacteria (Mycobacteriales) form a clade, with PL-7 closest to Gordonia, as the 16S distances suggested. Genus names on sequences are hypotheses too; a 16S tree is how a new isolate’s genus is checked.

Wrap-up

You’ve built your first phylogenies. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Trees: tips, nodes, branches, clades; rooted or unrooted; Newick format.
  • Distances: p-distances underestimate evolution; the Jukes-Cantor and Kimura corrections account for multiple hits; transitions outnumber transversions 1.4 to 1.
  • UPGMA assumes a clock and misplaced the fast Accumulibacter lineage (Robinson-Foulds distance 4 from the truth).
  • Neighbour joining on Jukes-Cantor distances recovered the true topology exactly; rooted with the Bacteroidetes, PL-7 groups with Gordonia and Mycobacterium.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Phylogenetic tree
A branching diagram of descent from common ancestors.
Tip, node, branch, clade
A sequence, an inferred ancestor, the line connecting them, and an ancestor with all its descendants.
Rooted and unrooted trees
Trees with and without a marked oldest ancestor.
Outgroup
Taxa known to have branched off first, used to root a tree.
p-distance
The proportion of differing sites between two sequences.
Jukes-Cantor and Kimura (K80) distances
Distances corrected for multiple substitutions, under equal rates or with a transition/transversion ratio.
UPGMA
Average-linkage clustering, which assumes a molecular clock.
Neighbour joining
A distance method that doesn’t assume a clock.
Robinson-Foulds distance
The number of splits present in one tree but not the other.
Molecular clock
The hypothesis that all lineages evolve at the same rate.
