Database searching

Finding relatives among thousands of proteins

Search a 600-protein database for relatives of PL-7’s ketoreductase, the way BLAST does it. See BLAST’s two ideas: short words and their neighbourhoods as seeds, and a fast filter that leaves only a few candidates for full alignment. Then score every database protein with Smith-Waterman, estimate the score distribution of unrelated proteins, turn scores into E-values, read the hit list, and meet the limit of sequence search: a real relative that’s sunk below the noise.

Module 10 · Lesson 6 of 13 Week 152 of 160 About 5–7 hours

When Amaya’s group sequenced PL-7, the first thing they did with every gene was a database search: compare its protein with every protein known, and list the ones that look related. Relatives with known functions tell you what a new protein probably does. The search tool of choice since 1990 is BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool), run millions of times a day against databases of hundreds of millions of proteins.

Here, the database is a small one: 600 proteins, among which are PL-7’s enzyme’s relatives. It’s small enough to align the query with every entry by Smith-Waterman, which is what BLAST approximates, so you can see both the shortcut and the exact answer.

A database search is Lesson 5’s statistics done 600 times, plus a clever trick to avoid most of the work. The trick is why BLAST is fast; the statistics are why it’s trusted.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • explore a protein database
  • see BLAST’s word seeds and their neighbourhoods
  • filter the database with shared words before aligning
  • score every entry with Smith-Waterman, and estimate the null distribution
  • compute E-values, read a hit list, and see where sequence search runs out

The database

protein_db.fasta has 600 proteins, each with a header like a real database’s: an identifier, a description and the organism. The setup code reads it and the query (PL7_KRED), and aligns the query with every entry, scores only, in one vectorized call (it takes well under a second).

Your turn. Count the entries and residues, and look at the lengths.

The lengths are width(db).

print(head(names(db), 3))
c(entries = length(db), residues = sum(width(db)), shortest = min(width(db)), longest = max(width(db)))

600 entries, 171,888 residues, from 120 to 450 residues long. Real databases are a million times larger (UniProt holds about 250 million proteins), which is why search speed matters.

BLAST’s shortcut: words

Aligning a query with every protein in a database of hundreds of millions takes too long. BLAST (Altschul and others, 1990) exploits the fact that a real alignment almost always contains a short stretch of high similarity. It breaks the query into words of 3 amino acids, and for each word builds its neighbourhood: all 8,000 three-letter words that score at least a threshold T (11, with BLOSUM62) against it. Database sequences containing a neighbourhood word are seeds, extended into alignments; everything else is skipped.

Your turn. For the query word TGA (from the TGxxxGxG motif), find all three-letter words with a BLOSUM62 score of at least 11.

The threshold is 11.

data(BLOSUM62, package = "pwalign")
aa <- c("A", "R", "N", "D", "C", "Q", "E", "G", "H", "I", "L", "K", "M", "F", "P", "S", "T", "W", "Y", "V")
w <- c("T", "G", "A")
all3 <- expand.grid(a = aa, b = aa, c = aa, stringsAsFactors = FALSE)       # 8,000 words
s3 <- BLOSUM62[w[1], all3$a] + BLOSUM62[w[2], all3$b] + BLOSUM62[w[3], all3$c]
nb <- paste0(all3$a, all3$b, all3$c)[s3 >= 11]
c(score_of_itself = sum(diag(BLOSUM62[w, w])), neighbourhood = length(nb), examples = paste(head(nb, 5), collapse = " "))

TGA scores 15 against itself (5 + 6 + 4), and its neighbourhood at T = 11 has 7 words, including SGA, TGC, TGG and TGS: one conservative change is allowed, mostly at the first or third position, because the glycine’s score of 6 is hard to make up elsewhere. A typical query word has a few dozen neighbours; the threshold trades sensitivity (more seeds) against speed (more extensions).

Your turn. A simpler filter: count, for every database entry, how many of the query’s distinct 3-letter words it contains exactly, and compare the family members (identifiers starting with PL7, SDR or RMT) with the rest.

Count the entry’s words found among the query’s words, qw.

words_of <- function(s) { s <- as.character(s); unique(substring(s, 1:(nchar(s) - 2), 3:nchar(s))) }
qw <- words_of(query)
shared <- sapply(seq_along(db), function(i) sum(words_of(db[[i]]) %in% qw))
family <- grepl("^(PL7|SDR|RMT)", names(db))
rbind(family = summary(shared[family]), others = summary(shared[!family]))

Family members share a median of 43 of the query’s words (from 17 up to all 243 of the query itself); unrelated entries share a median of 12 and at most 32. Exact 3-letter words are a crude test, but they already separate most relatives from the rest.

Your turn. Use the shared-word count as a filter: keep only entries sharing at least 25 of the query’s words. How many entries pass, and how many of the 29 family members?

At least 25 shared words.

words_of <- function(s) { s <- as.character(s); unique(substring(s, 1:(nchar(s) - 2), 3:nchar(s))) }
qw <- words_of(query)
shared <- sapply(seq_along(db), function(i) sum(words_of(db[[i]]) %in% qw))
family <- grepl("^(PL7|SDR|RMT)", names(db))
keep <- shared >= 25
c(passed = sum(keep), family_kept = sum(keep & family), family_total = sum(family), others_kept = sum(keep & !family))

35 entries pass: 25 of the 29 family members and 10 unrelated ones. Aligning 35 entries instead of 600 is 17 times less work, but 4 relatives were lost. That’s the trade-off every heuristic makes, and BLAST’s neighbourhood words and two-hit seeds are a far better filter than shared exact words.

The exact search, and its statistics

Your turn. The setup aligned the query with all 600 entries. Look at the score distribution: the top ten, and the bulk.

Add the 99th percentile, 0.99.

top <- order(sc, decreasing = TRUE)[1:10]
print(data.frame(score = sc[top], entry = substr(names(db)[top], 1, 60)))
quantile(sc, c(0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99))

The query finds itself (1,333), then 9 family members with scores of 634 to 892. The median of all 600 scores is 31, the 95th percentile 49, but the 99th percentile jumps to 750: the top 5% includes the family, which is completely separated from the bulk.

Most entries are unrelated to the query, so their scores are the null distribution, and BLAST-like programs estimate it from the search itself. Your turn. Fit a Gumbel distribution by the method of moments to the scores below 100 (the clearly unrelated bulk), and compute every entry’s E-value, E = N \times P(S \ge s), with N = 600.

Below 100.

bulk <- sc[sc < 100]
lambda <- pi / (sd(bulk) * sqrt(6)); mu <- mean(bulk) - 0.5772 / lambda
log10_p <- ifelse(sc < 150, log10(1 - exp(-exp(-lambda * (sc - mu)))), -lambda * (sc - mu) / log(10))
log10_E <- log10(length(db)) + log10_p
c(n_bulk = length(bulk), lambda = lambda, mu = mu, log10_E_top = max(-log10_E) * -1)

572 scores below 100 make up the bulk. The fitted Gumbel has \lambda = 0.256 and \mu = 29.4, close to Lesson 5’s fit from shuffles (0.248 and 29.9). The best hit (the query itself) has E = 10^{-142}. The code uses the exact formula for moderate scores and the tail approximation e^{-\lambda(x-\mu)} for large ones, where the exact one rounds to 0.

Your turn. List the significant hits, at E < 0.001, and check what they are.

E below 0.001.

bulk <- sc[sc < 100]
lambda <- pi / (sd(bulk) * sqrt(6)); mu <- mean(bulk) - 0.5772 / lambda
log10_p <- ifelse(sc < 150, log10(1 - exp(-exp(-lambda * (sc - mu)))), -lambda * (sc - mu) / log(10))
log10_E <- log10(length(db)) + log10_p
hits <- which(log10_E < log10(0.001))
table(substr(names(db)[hits], 1, 3))

28 significant hits: the query itself, 24 SDR family members and 3 of the remote relatives, RMT_01 to RMT_03. No unrelated protein reaches E < 0.001; the score needed is about 80, and the best unrelated entry scored 54.

Your turn. For the hits, compute the percentage identity of their local alignments, and find the least similar significant hit.

pid(al).

hit_ids <- which(sc > 100)
al <- pairwiseAlignment(db[hit_ids], query, type = "local", substitutionMatrix = "BLOSUM62", gapOpening = 10, gapExtension = 1)
res <- data.frame(entry = substr(names(db)[hit_ids], 1, 7), score = score(al), identity = round(pid(al), 1))
res <- res[order(res$identity), ]
head(res, 4)

The least similar significant hit, RMT_03, shares only 27.4% of the residues in its local alignment (score 127), followed by RMT_02 at 31.6% and SDR_05 at 36.5%. That’s the twilight zone, around 20 to 30% identity, where unrelated proteins can also align with similar identities over short stretches, and only the score’s significance (which accounts for the alignment’s length) can tell them apart.

Your turn. The database contains a fourth remote relative, RMT_04, a true member of the family that diverged a very long time ago. Find its score and E-value.

Search for "RMT_04".

bulk <- sc[sc < 100]
lambda <- pi / (sd(bulk) * sqrt(6)); mu <- mean(bulk) - 0.5772 / lambda
i <- grep("^RMT_04", names(db))
p <- 1 - exp(-exp(-lambda * (sc[i] - mu)))
c(score = sc[i], rank = rank(-sc)[i], E = length(db) * p)

A score of 33, ranked 218th of 600, with an E-value of 198: indistinguishable from unrelated proteins. RMT_04 has the same fold and function as PL7_KRED (it’s simulated from the same ancestor), but after so many substitutions, its sequence carries no detectable trace of the relationship. Structural comparison (Lesson 11) or profile methods (the box below) can sometimes still find such relatives.

NoteGo deeper: what BLAST adds, and PSI-BLAST and profile HMMs

Real BLAST adds several refinements: the two-hit method (extend only when two seeds lie on the same diagonal within 40 residues, which cuts extensions tenfold), X-drop extension (stop extending when the score falls too far below its maximum), gapped extension only for the best ungapped hits, low-complexity filtering (to stop repeats like QQQQQQ from matching everything), and compositional adjustment of the scores. Its E-values use \lambda and K precomputed for each scoring system, with corrections for the edges of the sequences. To reach further into the twilight zone, PSI-BLAST iterates: it builds a position-specific scoring matrix from the first round’s hits and searches again with it; HMMER searches with profile hidden Markov models (Lessons 7 and 10) built from whole family alignments, and finds relatives that pairwise search misses. Stephen Altschul and others, “Gapped BLAST and PSI-BLAST: a new generation of protein database search programs”, Nucleic Acids Research 25, 1997, pages 3389 to 3402.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Annotation transfer. Most database descriptions are themselves predicted by similarity, so errors spread. Tabulate the descriptions of all entries with E < 0.001, then of those between rank 30 and 40 (the best non-significant hits). Would you trust a description transferred from those?

Ranks 30 to 40: ord[30:40].

desc <- sub("^\\S+ ", "", sub(" \\[.*$", "", names(db)))
ord <- order(sc, decreasing = TRUE)
list(significant = table(desc[ord[1:28]]), next_ten = table(desc[ord[30:40]]))

All 28 significant hits are described as short-chain dehydrogenases or reductases. The next eleven, just below significance, have eleven different descriptions (methyltransferases, regulators, transporters, …): nothing to transfer. In a real database, a hit’s description is only as good as its own evidence, and the chain of transfers behind it.

Quick quiz

1. Why doesn’t BLAST align the query with every database sequence?

Smith-Waterman on every entry is exact, but too slow for hundreds of millions of sequences.

2. A word’s neighbourhood is:

Seeds can be similar words, not only identical ones.

3. Where does a database search’s null distribution come from?

Most of a database is unrelated to any given query.

4. The same alignment gives E = 0.0001 in a small database and E = 10 in a large one. Why?

More comparisons, more chance high scores.

5. RMT_04 is a true relative but scored like random proteins. What does that show?

Profile methods and structure comparison reach further.

6. Why can transferring a function from a database hit be risky?

Prefer hits with experimental evidence, such as Swiss-Prot entries.

Wrap-up

You’ve searched a database like BLAST. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The database: 600 proteins, 171,888 residues.
  • BLAST’s shortcut: 3-letter words and their neighbourhoods as seeds; even a crude shared-word filter keeps the family and drops most of the rest.
  • The exact search: Smith-Waterman against every entry; the unrelated bulk gives the Gumbel null; E-values = N \times P.
  • The hits: 28 significant hits, all family members, down to about 30% identity.
  • The limit: RMT_04, a true but very distant relative, scores like noise: sequence search has a horizon.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Database search
Comparing a query sequence with every entry of a database to find similar sequences.
BLAST
The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool: a heuristic local alignment search with word seeds and E-values.
Word, neighbourhood, seed
A short substring of the query; the words scoring at least T against it; a database position matching a neighbourhood word.
Hit list
The database entries ranked by score or E-value.
E-value
The expected number of chance hits at least as good, in a search of this size.
Twilight zone
Sequence identities of about 20 to 30%, where alignment can no longer reliably distinguish relatives from chance.
Annotation transfer
Assigning a function to a new protein from its database hits.
