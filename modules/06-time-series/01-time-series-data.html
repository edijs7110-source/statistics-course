Time series data

When the order of the data matters

Meet the plant’s monthly steam demand, twelve years of it. Store it as a time series object, plot it over time, by season and against its own past, and pick apart its trend, its seasonality and its autocorrelation. Then see why the independence assumption of earlier modules fails for data like these.

Module 6 · Lesson 1 of 13 Week 90 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Every dataset in this course so far has had a hidden assumption: the observations are independent. Batch 17’s yield says nothing about batch 18’s, and shuffling the rows changes nothing. For a lot of plant data, that’s false. Today’s steam demand is a lot like yesterday’s, this January is a lot like last January, and if you shuffle the months, you destroy exactly the information you need. Data recorded in time order, where the order matters, is a time series, and this module is about the methods built for it.

The first series is the plant’s steam demand: how many thousand tonnes (kt) of steam the boilers made each month, from January 2014 to December 2025. Steam drives turbines and reboilers, but it also heats buildings and keeps pipes from freezing, so demand rises in winter. The utilities engineer also has each month’s heating degree days (HDD): the sum, over the month’s days, of how far the average outdoor temperature fell below 15.5 °C. A cold month has many, a summer month almost none.

Welcome to Module 6! Time is the one variable every plant measures. Let’s learn to read a series the way an old operator reads a trend chart: at a glance.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • store data as a time series object, with its start and frequency
  • select stretches of time, and aggregate months into years
  • plot a series over time, by season and against its past
  • describe trend, seasonality and cycles
  • measure autocorrelation, and see why it breaks the independence assumption

Time series objects

R’s built-in ts class stores a series of equally spaced observations with its timing: the time of the first observation, and the frequency, the number of observations per unit of time. For monthly data with years as the unit, the frequency is 12: quarterly data has 4, weekly about 52.18, and hourly data with days as the unit 24.

Your turn. Make a monthly ts object of the steam demand, starting in January 2014, and look at its time attributes with tsp(): the start, the end and the frequency.

Monthly data with years as the unit has a frequency of 12.

steam_ts <- ts(steam$steam_kt, start = c(2014, 1), frequency = 12)
tsp(steam_ts)

The series starts at 2014.000 and ends at 2025.917, December 2025, which is 2025 + 11/12, with 12 observations per year. start = c(2014, 1) means “year 2014, period 1”, January. Once R knows the timing, plots get a proper time axis, and functions like window(), cycle() and aggregate() work by date.

Your turn. The setup code has the series as y. Use window() to pick out 2020, and find its average monthly demand.

The year ends in period 12, December.

y2020 <- window(y, start = c(2020, 1), end = c(2020, 12))
mean(y2020)

39.34 kt a month in 2020. window() keeps the result a ts object, with the right start date, so you can keep using time series functions on it.

Your turn. Add up each year’s months with aggregate(), which sums a monthly series into a yearly one.

Total demand is the sum: FUN = sum.

annual <- aggregate(y, FUN = sum)
annual

From 447 kt in 2014 to 525 kt in 2025. The yearly totals rise in most years, but not all: 2015 and 2017 were slightly lower than the year before. Mild winters use less steam, so the yearly totals mix the growth of the plant with the weather.

Trend, seasonality and cycles

Here’s the series over time, a time plot:

autoplot(y, color = "#C2416C") +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Steam demand (kt per month)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Three kinds of pattern show up in time series, and this one has two of them:

  • A trend is a long-term rise or fall. Steam demand drifts upward as the plant grows.
  • Seasonality is a pattern that repeats with a fixed period, tied to the calendar: here, every 12 months, high in winter and low in summer.
  • A cycle is a rise and fall without a fixed period, like a business cycle that lasts anywhere from two to ten years. Cycles are much harder to forecast than seasons. Lesson 13 meets a series with long, irregular swings.

A seasonal plot draws each year as its own line against the months, which makes the seasonal pattern, and any year that breaks it, easy to see:

ggseasonplot(y, year.labels = FALSE) +
  scale_color_viridis_d(option = "magma", end = 0.85, name = "Year") +
  labs(title = NULL, x = NULL, y = "Steam demand (kt per month)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Your turn. cycle() gives each observation’s position in the cycle, here the month, 1 to 12. Use it to find the average demand for each calendar month.

The grouping is the month, cycle(y).

round(tapply(y, cycle(y), mean), 2)

January averages 45.5 kt and August 34.4: a seasonal swing of 11 kt, about a quarter of the average demand. The pattern is smooth, falling from January to August and rising again, as the heating degree days do.

Your turn. Fit a straight-line trend with lm(), using time(y), the time in years, as the predictor. How much does demand grow per year?

The predictor is the time, time(y).

trend <- lm(y ~ time(y))
coef(trend)[2]                  # kt per month, gained each year

0.60 kt per month per year: monthly demand grows by about 1.5% a year. Careful with the standard error that summary(trend) would print, though. It assumes independent residuals, and the residuals of this series are anything but, as the next section shows.

Autocorrelation

The defining feature of a time series is that each observation is related to its own past. A lag plot shows it: each month’s demand against the demand k months earlier.

lagged <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c(1, 6, 12), function(k) data.frame(now = y[-(1:k)], before = y[1:(length(y) - k)], lag = paste("lag", k))))
lagged$lag <- factor(lagged$lag, levels = paste("lag", c(1, 6, 12)))
ggplot(lagged, aes(x = before, y = now)) +
  geom_point(size = 1, color = "#C2416C", alpha = 0.7) +
  facet_wrap(~ lag) +
  labs(x = "Demand k months earlier (kt)", y = "Demand (kt)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The autocorrelation at lag k, r_k, is the correlation in each of those panels, computed in a standard way: with the overall mean and variance of the series, rather than each panel’s own. Together, the r_k are the autocorrelation function (ACF), which Lesson 3 uses to tell models apart.

Your turn. Compute the ACF with acf(..., plot = FALSE), and pick out the autocorrelations at lags 1, 6 and 12. (The first element is lag 0, which is always 1.)

Lag k is element k + 1, so lag 12 is element 13.

r <- acf(y, lag.max = 24, plot = FALSE)$acf
c(lag1 = r[2], lag6 = r[7], lag12 = r[13])

0.83 at lag 1, −0.49 at lag 6, and 0.81 at lag 12. Consecutive months are strongly alike, months half a year apart are opposites, and the same month a year apart is alike again. For independent data, every autocorrelation beyond lag 0 would be near 0. Here, knowing one month tells you a lot about the next. That’s useful for forecasting, and fatal for any method that assumes independence: standard errors, t-tests and confidence intervals that ignore it are too small, sometimes by a factor of several.

Your turn. How closely does steam demand follow the weather? Find the correlation between the monthly demand and the heating degree days.

The heating degree days are in the column hdd.

cor(steam$steam_kt, steam$hdd)

0.86. Most of the seasonal swing is the weather, so a model that uses the heating degree days can explain the seasonality instead of just copying it. That’s Lesson 10, regression with time series errors. A warning from the same lesson: two series that both trend or both have seasons can be highly correlated without being related at all.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Year over year. Comparing each month with the same month a year earlier removes the seasonality: diff(y, lag = 12). That’s a seasonal difference, and Lesson 9 builds models on it. Find the average year-over-year change, and its SD.

A year is 12 months.

yoy <- diff(y, lag = 12)
c(mean = mean(yoy), sd = sd(yoy))

An average rise of 0.59 kt over the same month last year, close to the trend’s 0.60 per year, but with an SD of 1.9: from one year to the next, the weather moves a month’s demand by much more than the plant’s growth does. The 132 year-over-year changes have no seasonality left, which makes the trend much easier to see.

Challenge 2 · When is the peak? In which month did each year’s demand peak? Group the series by year, floor(time(y)), and find the position of each year’s maximum.

which.max(v) gives the position of the largest value.

tapply(y, floor(time(y)), function(v) which.max(v))

The peak came in January (1) in three years, February (2) in four, and December (12) in five. The seasonal pattern is fixed, but the coldest month isn’t: it depends on the weather. A model with a fixed seasonal shape would put the peak in January every year, so it’d be off in most years by a month or so. A model with the heating degree days wouldn’t.

Quick quiz

1. What does the frequency of a ts object mean?

With the start time, it gives every observation its date.

2. What distinguishes a cycle from seasonality?

That’s why seasons are easy to forecast and cycles aren’t.

3. A monthly series has an autocorrelation of 0.81 at lag 12. What does that suggest?

Seasonal series have large autocorrelations at multiples of the seasonal period.

4. Why is autocorrelation a problem for an ordinary regression’s standard errors?

Twelve correlated months carry less information than twelve independent ones. Lesson 10 fixes this.

5. What does diff(y, lag = 12) compute for a monthly series?

A seasonal difference. It removes a stable seasonal pattern.

6. What does a seasonal plot show that a time plot doesn’t?

In a time plot, the seasonal shape is spread across the years. A seasonal plot stacks them.

Wrap-up

You can now read a time series. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • ts objects store a series with its start and frequency, so that window(), cycle(), time() and aggregate() work by date.
  • Trend: steam demand grows by 0.60 kt per month each year, from 447 kt a year in 2014 to 525 kt in 2025.
  • Seasonality: January averages 45.5 kt and August 34.4, following the heating degree days (correlation 0.86).
  • Plots: time plots show trend and seasonality, seasonal plots the seasonal shape, and lag plots the relation of a series to its past.
  • Autocorrelation: 0.83 at lag 1 and 0.81 at lag 12. Observations in time aren’t independent, which is useful for forecasting and dangerous for ordinary standard errors.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Time series
Observations recorded in time order, where the order matters.
Frequency
The number of observations per unit of time in a ts object: 12 for monthly data in years.
Trend
A long-term rise or fall in a series.
Seasonality
A pattern that repeats with a fixed, known period, such as every 12 months.
Cycle
Rises and falls without a fixed period.
Lag
The number of time steps between two observations being compared.
Autocorrelation function (ACF)
The correlations of a series with its own past at each lag.
Seasonal difference
The change from each observation to the one a full season earlier, such as diff(y, lag = 12).
