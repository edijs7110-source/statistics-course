Seasonal ARIMA

Models that remember last year

Extend ARIMA to seasonal data. Decide on seasonal differencing for the steam demand, read the seasonal moving-average signature in the ACF, fit the classic airline model and its relatives, and use the residual checks to choose between them, finding along the way what a pure seasonal model can’t explain. Then forecast 2026 month by month, and simulate the year’s total demand with an interval.

Module 6 · Lesson 9 of 13 Week 98 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The steam demand’s strongest memory isn’t last month, it’s last year: Lesson 1 found an autocorrelation of 0.81 at lag 12. A seasonal ARIMA model has terms for that. It’s written ARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)_m: the usual non-seasonal part, plus seasonal AR, differencing and MA terms of orders P, D and Q at multiples of the period m, here 12. In backshift notation, where B y_t = y_{t-1} and B^{12} y_t = y_{t-12}, the seasonal and non-seasonal parts multiply:

\underbrace{(1 - \phi B)}_{\text{AR}}\underbrace{(1 - B)^d}_{\text{difference}}\underbrace{(1 - B^{12})^D}_{\text{seasonal difference}}\, y_t = \underbrace{(1 + \theta B)}_{\text{MA}}\underbrace{(1 + \Theta B^{12})}_{\text{seasonal MA}}\,\varepsilon_t .

That multiplication means the terms interact: an MA(1) and a seasonal MA(1) together put structure at lags 1, 12 and 13.

Seasonal ARIMA is ARIMA with a calendar. Same Box-Jenkins recipe, just with a second set of ingredients every twelve months.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • decide how many seasonal differences a series needs
  • recognize a seasonal MA term in the ACF
  • fit the airline model and its relatives
  • use residual checks to choose between models with different differencing
  • forecast a seasonal series, and simulate a yearly total

Seasonal differencing

The seasonal difference, y_t - y_{t-12}, removes a seasonal pattern that repeats from year to year. nsdiffs() tests whether it’s needed, using a measure of seasonal strength.

Your turn. Ask how many seasonal differences the steam demand needs, and then how many ordinary differences the seasonally differenced series needs.

A seasonal difference for monthly data uses lag = 12.

c(seasonal = nsdiffs(y), ordinary_after = ndiffs(diff(y, lag = 12)))

One seasonal difference, D = 1, and then no ordinary difference, d = 0, according to the KPSS test. That decision is closer than it looks, as you’ll see. A rule worth knowing: d + D should rarely exceed 2, and with a seasonal difference, a drift term means a steady trend in the year-over-year changes.

Identifying seasonal terms

Seasonal terms show up in the ACF and PACF at the seasonal lags, 12, 24, 36. The same rules as Lesson 5 apply there: a seasonal MA(1) gives an ACF spike at lag 12 and nothing at 24, with the PACF decaying across 12, 24, 36. A seasonal AR(1) does the opposite.

Your turn. Look at the seasonally differenced series’ ACF at lags 1, 2, 12 and 24, and its PACF at 12 and 24.

The ACF at lag 24 is element 25.

sd12 <- diff(y, lag = 12)
r <- acf(sd12, lag.max = 36, plot = FALSE)$acf
pr <- pacf(sd12, lag.max = 36, plot = FALSE)$acf
c(acf1 = r[2], acf2 = r[3], acf12 = r[13], acf24 = r[25], pacf12 = pr[12], pacf24 = pr[24])

At the seasonal lags, the ACF has one large spike, −0.50 at lag 12, and nothing at 24 (−0.01), while the PACF decays, −0.51 then −0.25. That’s a seasonal MA(1), with a negative coefficient. At the short lags, the ACF is 0.22 then 0.11: a small AR(1). So the first candidate is ARIMA(1, 0, 0)(0, 1, 1)_{12}. The negative spike at lag 12 is typical after seasonal differencing: it often means the seasonal pattern is nearly constant, and the difference partly overdid it, as in Lesson 5’s overdifferencing.

Your turn. Check the signature with the theoretical ACF of a pure seasonal MA(1), y_t = \varepsilon_t + \Theta\varepsilon_{t-12}, with \Theta = -0.8. In ARMAacf(), that’s an MA(12) whose first eleven coefficients are 0.

Eleven zeros, then the coefficient at lag 12.

r <- ARMAacf(ma = c(rep(0, 11), -0.8), lag.max = 13)
r[c("1", "11", "12", "13")]

0 everywhere except lag 12, where it’s -0.8/(1 + 0.64) = -0.49: Lesson 5’s MA(1) formula, stretched to the seasonal period. Close to the sample’s −0.50.

The airline model

The most famous seasonal model is ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}, called the airline model because Box and Jenkins first fitted it to airline passenger numbers, Lesson 2’s AirPassengers. It takes both an ordinary and a seasonal difference, and has one MA term for each. It’s a natural first try for monthly data with a trend and a stable season.

Your turn. Fit the airline model to the steam demand.

One seasonal difference: D = 1.

airline <- Arima(y, order = c(0, 1, 1), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1))
c(coef(airline), aicc = airline$aicc)

\hat\theta = -0.86 and \hat\Theta = -0.91, with an AICc of 476.6. Both MA coefficients are close to −1. As Lesson 5 showed, an MA coefficient near −1 after differencing means the difference nearly cancels out: here, the trend and the seasonal pattern are both nearly deterministic, changing very slowly. That’s consistent with Lesson 2’s ETS fit, whose trend and seasonal smoothing parameters were almost 0.

Choosing between candidates

Your turn. Fit the candidate you identified, ARIMA(1,0,0)(0,1,1)_{12} with drift (the drift handles the trend, since there’s no ordinary difference), and check its residuals with the Ljung-Box test over 24 lags, two seasons, with fitdf = 2 for its two ARMA coefficients.

Two ARMA coefficients, the AR and the seasonal MA: fitdf = 2.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1), include.drift = TRUE)
c(coef(fit), aicc = fit$aicc, ljung_box_p = Box.test(residuals(fit), lag = 24, type = "Ljung-Box", fitdf = 2)$p.value)

\hat\phi = 0.34, \hat\Theta = -0.88, and a drift of 0.052 kt a month in the year-over-year changes, about Lesson 1’s 0.6 kt a year. But the residuals fail: p = 0.017. The model misses something. Its AICc, 478.4, can’t be compared with the airline model’s, because the two have different differencing (d = 0 against d = 1). So the residual checks have to decide.

Your turn. Within the d = D = 1 family, which AICc can compare, try ARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}, the airline model plus an AR term. Compare its AICc with the airline’s 476.6, and check its residuals.

One AR term: p = 1.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 1, 1), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1))
c(coef(fit), aicc = fit$aicc, ljung_box_p = Box.test(residuals(fit), lag = 24, type = "Ljung-Box", fitdf = 3)$p.value)

\hat\phi = 0.21 (SE 0.09), and an AICc of 474.1, 2.5 better than the airline model. Its residuals pass, p = 0.10, though not by much. Of the dozen seasonal models one might try here, this is among the best on both counts. Seasonal model selection is rarely clean: several models are nearly as good, and the forecasts they give are usually close too.

Forecasting a seasonal series

Your turn. Forecast 2026 with ARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}, and find the forecasts and 95% intervals for January and July.

July 2026 is the seventh month ahead.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 1, 1), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1))
fc <- forecast(fit, h = 12)
rbind(january = c(fc$mean[1], fc$lower[1, 2], fc$upper[1, 2]),
      july = c(fc$mean[7], fc$lower[7, 2], fc$upper[7, 2]))

January 2026: 50.2 kt (47.6 to 52.9). July 2026: 39.3 kt (36.4 to 42.1). Very close to Lesson 2’s ETS forecast for January, 50.0. The intervals are about ±2.7 kt in January and ±2.9 in July: the winter peak is more predictable than you might think, because the model knows it’s coming. What it can’t know is next winter’s weather.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 1, 1), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1))
fc <- forecast(fit, h = 12)
autoplot(window(y, start = 2021), color = "#C2416C") +
  autolayer(fc, color = "#3A1F35", fill = "#F4C0D1", alpha = 0.7, showgap = FALSE) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Steam demand (kt per month)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Your turn. The utilities budget needs 2026’s total. Adding up the monthly forecasts gives its point forecast, but the monthly errors are correlated, so their intervals can’t simply be added. Simulate 2,000 future years from the model instead, and take the 5th and 95th percentiles of their totals.

future = TRUE simulates forward from the end of the data.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 1, 1), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1))
fc <- forecast(fit, h = 12)
set.seed(6091)
totals <- replicate(2000, sum(simulate(fit, nsim = 12, future = TRUE)))
c(point = sum(fc$mean), quantile(totals, c(0.05, 0.95)), total_2025 = sum(window(y, start = 2025)))

A total of 532 kt for 2026, with a 90% interval from 518 to 547, against 525 kt in 2025. It’s the predictive simulation recipe from Module 5 again: draw future paths, compute the quantity you care about on each, and summarize. It works for any function of the forecasts, such as the number of months above a capacity limit.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Seasonal naive is an ARIMA model. ARIMA(0,0,0)(0,1,0)_{12} says the seasonal differences are white noise: this year’s month equals last year’s, plus noise. Check that its forecasts are the seasonal naive method’s.

One seasonal difference, D = 1.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(0, 0, 0), seasonal = c(0, 1, 0))
rbind(arima = forecast(fit, h = 2)$mean, snaive = snaive(y, h = 2)$mean)

49.05 and 48.97 both ways: January and February 2025, copied. Every benchmark of Lesson 8 is an ARIMA model: naive is ARIMA(0,1,0), drift is ARIMA(0,1,0) with drift, and seasonal naive is this one. That’s another way to see why they’re benchmarks: they’re the simplest members of the family.

Challenge 2 · What’s left over? The identified model with drift failed its residual check. Find which of its residual autocorrelations, at lags 1 to 24, are outside \pm 1.96/\sqrt{144}.

The series has 144 months.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1), include.drift = TRUE)
r <- acf(residuals(fit), lag.max = 24, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1]
big <- which(abs(r) > 1.96 / sqrt(144))
rbind(lag = big, autocorrelation = r[big])

Lags 5, 9 and 18, at 0.21, 0.19 and 0.26. No simple ARMA term explains a pattern at lags 5, 9 and 18. It’s the weather: a cold spell that lasts a few months, or a mild winter, leaves its mark on the residuals, because the model only knows the calendar, not the temperature. Every pure seasonal model here has some of this left over. Lesson 10 adds the heating degree days to the model, and the leftover structure goes away.

Quick quiz

1. What does ARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12} mean?

The capital letters are the seasonal orders, and the subscript is the period.

2. After a seasonal difference, the ACF has a single spike at lag 12 and the PACF decays across 12, 24, 36. Which seasonal term does that suggest?

The same rule as for non-seasonal MA terms, at the seasonal lags.

3. Why is it called the airline model?

That’s AirPassengers, 1949 to 1960.

4. Two seasonal models have AICc values of 474.1 and 478.4, but one has d = 1 and the other d = 0. Which is better?

Use residual checks, or test-set accuracy and cross-validation (Lesson 8).

5. Why can’t you add up twelve monthly 95% intervals to get an interval for the year’s total?

Simulating future paths handles the correlation automatically.

6. The residuals of a seasonal model have autocorrelations at lags 5, 9 and 18. What’s the most likely explanation?

That’s the motivation for regression with ARIMA errors.

Wrap-up

You can now model and forecast seasonal series. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Seasonal differencing: the steam demand needs one seasonal difference, D = 1.
  • Identification: after it, a single ACF spike at lag 12 (−0.50) and a decaying PACF point to a seasonal MA(1).
  • Fitting: the airline model, ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}, has both MA coefficients near −1. Adding an AR term, ARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}, improves AICc to 474.1, and its residuals pass (p = 0.10). The d = 0 alternative fails (p = 0.017).
  • Forecasts: 50.2 kt for January 2026 (47.6 to 52.9) and 39.3 for July, and a 2026 total of 532 kt (90% interval 518 to 547), by simulation.
  • What’s left: residual autocorrelations at lags 5, 9 and 18 that only the weather can explain.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Seasonal ARIMA, ARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)_m
An ARIMA model with seasonal AR, differencing and MA terms at multiples of the period m.
Seasonal difference
y_t - y_{t-m}. D is the number of seasonal differences.
Backshift operator
B y_t = y_{t-1}. Seasonal models multiply polynomials in B and B^m.
Seasonal MA term
A moving-average term at lag m, shown by a single ACF spike at the seasonal lag.
Airline model
ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}, the classic model for monthly data with trend and seasonality.
Simulated forecast paths
Future series drawn from a fitted model, used for the distribution of totals or other functions of the forecasts.
