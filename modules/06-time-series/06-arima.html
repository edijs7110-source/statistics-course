ARIMA modeling

The Box-Jenkins approach, from start to forecast

Model the weekly sulfur content of the crude feed with the Box-Jenkins approach: decide how many differences it needs, identify an ARIMA(1,1,0) from the differenced series’ ACF and PACF, fit it by maximum likelihood, compute AIC by hand and search a grid of models by AICc, and compare the result with auto.arima(). Then test for drift, forecast twelve weeks ahead, and connect ARIMA(0,1,1) to Lesson 2’s exponential smoothing.

Module 6 · Lesson 6 of 13 Week 95 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The plant’s crude unit processes a blend of crudes that changes as cargoes arrive, so the sulfur content of the feed drifts from week to week. Sulfur matters: it poisons catalysts downstream and decides how hard the hydrotreaters have to work. The lab has measured it weekly since January 2019, 364 weeks in all, and the planners want a forecast for the next quarter.

The series wanders, from a high of 1.9 wt% in 2019 to lows near 0.4 in 2022 and 2025, so it isn’t stationary. An ARIMA(p, d, q) model handles that: difference the series d times, then fit an ARMA(p, q) to the result. The “I” stands for integrated: the model for the original series is the ARMA model, summed up d times. George Box and Gwilym Jenkins turned that into a method in 1970, and it’s still the standard approach:

  1. Identify: make the series stationary by differencing, then choose p and q from the ACF and PACF.
  2. Estimate: fit the candidates by maximum likelihood.
  3. Check: look at the residuals, which should be white noise (Lesson 7).
  4. Forecast: and only then.

Identify, estimate, check, forecast. The Box-Jenkins cycle is a recipe, and like any good recipe, it’s best followed in order!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • decide the order of differencing, d
  • identify p and q from the differenced series
  • fit an ARIMA model by maximum likelihood, and compute its AIC by hand
  • compare models by AICc, by hand and with auto.arima()
  • test whether a drift term is needed
  • forecast, and see the link between ARIMA and exponential smoothing

Step 1: identification

ggplot(feed, aes(x = as.Date(week), y = sulfur_wt_pct)) +
  geom_line(color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.5) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Sulfur in the feed (wt%)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Your turn. How many differences does the series need? Ask ndiffs(), and run the ADF test on the series and on its differences.

Test the differences, diff(x).

c(ndiffs = forecast::ndiffs(x),
  adf_level = tseries::adf.test(x)$p.value,
  adf_changes = tseries::adf.test(diff(x))$p.value)

One difference: the ADF test can’t reject a unit root for the level (p = 0.31), and rejects it for the weekly changes (p = 0.01). So d = 1. Physically, that makes sense: the crude slate drifts with the market and the cargoes, with nothing pulling it back to a fixed sulfur level.

Your turn. Now look at the differenced series’ ACF and PACF at lags 1 to 3.

Lags 1 to 3 of the PACF are elements 1 to 3.

dx <- diff(x)
rbind(acf = acf(dx, lag.max = 3, plot = FALSE)$acf[2:4],
      pacf = pacf(dx, lag.max = 3, plot = FALSE)$acf[1:3])

The ACF decays geometrically, 0.57, 0.29, 0.15, roughly halving each lag, and the PACF has one spike, 0.57, then nothing (−0.05 and 0.00). That’s the AR(1) pattern from Lesson 5’s table, so the candidate is ARIMA(1,1,0): the weekly changes follow an AR(1). A change in the crude slate tends to continue for a few weeks, as a supply shift works its way through the tanks.

Step 2: estimation

The forecast package’s Arima() is a front end to R’s arima() with a few conveniences, such as a drift term and forecasting from the fit.

Your turn. Fit ARIMA(1,1,0), and look at the AR coefficient, its standard error, the shock variance and the log-likelihood.

One difference: d = 1.

fit <- forecast::Arima(x, order = c(1, 1, 0))
c(coef(fit), se = sqrt(vcov(fit)[1, 1]), sigma2 = fit$sigma2, loglik = fit$loglik)

\hat\phi = 0.575 (SE 0.043), close to the 0.6 the series was simulated with, and a shock variance of 0.00069, an SD of 0.026 wt% a week. The model is \Delta y_t = 0.575\,\Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t. With d = 1, there’s no mean or constant, because a constant in the differences would mean a steady drift. That’s a separate choice, tested below.

Your turn. Models are compared by an information criterion, which rewards fit and penalizes parameters. AIC is -2\log L + 2k, where k counts the estimated parameters, including the shock variance. Compute it by hand, and compare it with fit$aic.

The penalty is 2k.

fit <- forecast::Arima(x, order = c(1, 1, 0))
k <- 2                               # phi and sigma^2
c(by_hand = -2 * fit$loglik + 2 * k, reported = fit$aic)

−1607.4 both ways. Only differences in AIC between models matter. Lower is better, and a difference of 2 or more is worth noticing. AICc corrects AIC for small samples, and auto.arima() uses it. But AIC values can only be compared between models with the same d: differencing changes the data the likelihood is computed on.

Your turn. Fit every ARIMA(p, 1, q) with p and q from 0 to 2, and rank them by AICc.

The second argument comes from the grid’s q column.

grid <- expand.grid(p = 0:2, q = 0:2)
grid$aicc <- mapply(function(p, q) forecast::Arima(x, order = c(p, 1, q))$aicc, grid$p, grid$q)
head(grid[order(grid$aicc), ], 4)

ARIMA(1,1,0) comes first, at −1607.4. Next are ARIMA(1,1,1) and ARIMA(2,1,0), about 1.2 worse: each adds a parameter for almost no gain in fit. The identification and the information criterion agree, which is the reassuring case. When they don’t, prefer the simpler model, and let the diagnostics of Lesson 7 decide.

Letting auto.arima() choose

auto.arima() automates the same steps: it chooses d with repeated KPSS tests, then searches over p and q by AICc. By default, the search is stepwise: it starts from a few simple models and moves to neighbors while AICc improves. That’s fast, but it can miss the best model. stepwise = FALSE searches all models up to a maximum order.

Your turn. Run both searches.

The full search has stepwise = FALSE.

stepwise <- forecast::auto.arima(x)
full <- forecast::auto.arima(x, stepwise = FALSE, approximation = FALSE)
rbind(stepwise = forecast::arimaorder(stepwise), full = forecast::arimaorder(full))

Both choose (1, 1, 0). That’s three methods agreeing: the ACF and PACF, the hand-made grid and the automatic search. (approximation = FALSE makes it compute exact likelihoods, slower for long series. Rob Hyndman, who wrote the package, recommends checking an automatic choice by hand, as you just did.)

Your turn. Is there a steady drift, an average change per week? Fit the model with include.drift = TRUE, and look at the drift’s estimate and standard error.

Set include.drift = TRUE.

fit <- forecast::Arima(x, order = c(1, 1, 0), include.drift = TRUE)
rbind(estimate = coef(fit), se = sqrt(diag(vcov(fit))))

A drift of −0.002 wt% a week, with a standard error of 0.003: indistinguishable from 0. Over seven years, the series fell from 1.62 to 0.77, which looks like a downward trend, but a random walk wanders that far easily. Keep the model without drift. A drift term in a model with d = 1 means a straight-line trend in the forecasts, and assuming one without evidence would make long-range forecasts confidently wrong.

Forecasting

An ARIMA(1,1,0) forecast continues the latest change for a while, decaying by \phi each week, and then stays flat. Its prediction intervals keep widening, because the model has a unit root: the series could drift anywhere, given time.

Your turn. Forecast the next 12 weeks, and find the forecasts and 95% intervals one and twelve weeks ahead.

Week 12’s upper limit is in row 12.

fit <- forecast::Arima(x, order = c(1, 1, 0))
fc <- forecast::forecast(fit, h = 12)
rbind(week_1 = c(fc$mean[1], fc$lower[1, 2], fc$upper[1, 2]),
      week_12 = c(fc$mean[12], fc$lower[12, 2], fc$upper[12, 2]))

Next week: 0.775 wt% (95% interval 0.72 to 0.83). Twelve weeks out: 0.784, with an interval from 0.40 to 1.16. The last change was up, +0.012 wt%, so the forecast continues upward a little and then levels off. The interval at twelve weeks is seven times as wide as at one week. For planning the hydrotreaters, that’s the honest message: next week is predictable, next quarter much less so.

fit <- Arima(feed$sulfur_wt_pct, order = c(1, 1, 0))
fc <- forecast(fit, h = 12)
hist_df <- data.frame(week = 261:364, y = feed$sulfur_wt_pct[261:364])
fc_df <- data.frame(week = 365:376, m = as.numeric(fc$mean), lo80 = fc$lower[, 1], hi80 = fc$upper[, 1], lo95 = fc$lower[, 2], hi95 = fc$upper[, 2])
ggplot() +
  geom_ribbon(data = fc_df, aes(x = week, ymin = lo95, ymax = hi95), fill = "#F4C0D1", alpha = 0.6) +
  geom_ribbon(data = fc_df, aes(x = week, ymin = lo80, ymax = hi80), fill = "#F4C0D1") +
  geom_line(data = hist_df, aes(x = week, y = y), color = "#C2416C") +
  geom_line(data = fc_df, aes(x = week, y = m), color = "#3A1F35", linewidth = 0.9) +
  labs(x = "Week (since January 2019)", y = "Sulfur (wt%)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · ARIMA and exponential smoothing. Lesson 2’s simple exponential smoothing is exactly the forecast of an ARIMA(0,1,1) model, with \alpha = 1 + \theta. Fit ARIMA(0,1,1) to the pressure drop from Lesson 2, and compare 1 + \hat\theta with the \alpha that ses() chose, 0.349.

One MA term: q = 1.

dp <- read_csv("data/pressure_drop.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)$dp_bar
fit <- forecast::Arima(dp, order = c(0, 1, 1))
c(theta = coef(fit)[[1]], one_plus_theta = 1 + coef(fit)[[1]])

\hat\theta = -0.648, so 1 + \hat\theta = 0.352, close to ses()’s 0.349 (the two estimate the starting values differently). The two families overlap: every linear exponential smoothing model is an ARIMA model, and ARIMA(0,1,1) is SES. Holt’s linear method is ARIMA(0,2,2). Many seasonal ARIMA models and ETS models have no equivalent in the other family, though, which is why Lesson 8 compares them on the data.

Challenge 2 · A sweet-crude quarter? The refinery earns a premium in any quarter when the feed’s sulfur is below 0.5 wt%. Using the forecast distribution twelve weeks ahead, which is normal with the forecast as its mean, find the probability that the sulfur is below 0.5. The 95% interval’s half-width is 1.96 standard errors.

The 95% interval’s half-width is qnorm(0.975) standard errors.

fit <- forecast::Arima(x, order = c(1, 1, 0))
fc <- forecast::forecast(fit, h = 12)
se12 <- (fc$upper[12, 2] - fc$mean[12]) / qnorm(0.975)
c(se = se12, p_below_0.5 = pnorm(0.5, fc$mean[12], se12))

A forecast standard error of 0.19 wt% at twelve weeks, and a 7% probability that the sulfur is below 0.5 by then. Unlikely, but not negligible: the series was below 0.5 for weeks at a time in 2022 and 2025. Probabilities like this, from the full forecast distribution, are usually more useful to planners than a single number.

Quick quiz

1. What does ARIMA(1,1,0) mean?

The middle number, d, is the number of differences.

2. What are the four steps of the Box-Jenkins approach?

And if the check fails, back to identification.

3. Can you compare the AIC of an ARIMA(1,1,0) with that of an ARIMA(1,0,0)?

Choose d first, by unit-root tests, then compare models with the same d.

4. What does a drift term do in a model with one difference?

Include it only if the data support a steady trend.

5. Why do an ARIMA(1,1,0)’s prediction intervals keep widening with the horizon?

A stationary model’s intervals would level off at a fixed width.

6. Which ARIMA model gives the same forecasts as simple exponential smoothing?

With \alpha = 1 + \theta. ARIMA(0,1,0) is the random walk, whose forecast is the last value.

Wrap-up

You’ve built an ARIMA model the Box-Jenkins way. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Differencing: the feed’s sulfur needs one difference (d = 1): ADF p = 0.31 for the level, 0.01 for the changes.
  • Identification: the changes’ ACF decays and their PACF cuts off after lag 1, pointing to ARIMA(1,1,0).
  • Estimation: \hat\phi = 0.575 (SE 0.043). AIC is -2\log L + 2k, −1607.4 here, and it’s the lowest AICc in a grid of nine models.
  • auto.arima() agrees, stepwise and with a full search. A drift term isn’t needed (−0.002, SE 0.003).
  • Forecasts: 0.775 wt% next week (0.72 to 0.83), and 0.784 in twelve weeks (0.40 to 1.16), with a 7% chance of being below 0.5.
  • ARIMA(0,1,1) is simple exponential smoothing, with \alpha = 1 + \theta.

NoteKey words from this lesson
ARIMA(p, d, q) model
An ARMA(p, q) model for the series differenced d times.
Integrated
A series that needs differencing to become stationary. The I in ARIMA.
Box-Jenkins approach
Identify, estimate, check, forecast, and repeat until the check passes.
Akaike information criterion (AIC)
-2\log L + 2k: a measure of fit penalized for the number of parameters. Lower is better.
AICc
AIC with a small-sample correction, used by auto.arima().
Drift
A constant in a differenced model, which gives the forecasts a straight-line trend.
Stepwise search
A model search that moves from model to neighboring model while the criterion improves.
