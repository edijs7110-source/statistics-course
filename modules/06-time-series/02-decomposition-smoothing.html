Decomposition and smoothing

Separating the signal from the seasons and the noise

Split the steam demand into its trend, its seasonal pattern and what’s left, first with moving averages and classical decomposition, then with STL, and measure how strong each component is. Then forecast a catalyst bed’s drifting pressure drop with exponential smoothing: by hand, with an optimized smoothing parameter, and with the forecast package’s ses(), holt() and ets().

Module 6 · Lesson 2 of 13 Week 91 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 1 saw three things at once in the steam demand: a rising trend, a winter-summer season, and month-to-month noise. To understand a series, and to forecast it, it helps to pull those apart. A decomposition writes the series as

y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t,

a trend-cycle T_t, a seasonal component S_t and a remainder R_t. (When the seasonal swings grow with the level, a multiplicative version, y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t, fits better.) The second half of this lesson turns to the simplest forecasting methods that work in practice, exponential smoothing, which weight recent observations more than old ones.

Pulling a series apart is like a distillation column for data: trend off the top, seasons in the middle, noise at the bottom!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • estimate a trend with a centered moving average
  • decompose a series into trend, seasonal and remainder, classically and with STL
  • adjust a series for seasonality, and measure how strong each component is
  • forecast with simple exponential smoothing, by hand and with ses()
  • choose the smoothing parameter, and compare SES, Holt’s method and ETS

Moving averages

A moving average smooths out short-term noise by averaging each observation with its neighbors. To remove a monthly seasonal pattern completely, average over exactly a year. Twelve is even, so a centered version averages 13 months, giving the two end months half weight: the 2×12 moving average,

\hat T_t = \tfrac{1}{12}\left(\tfrac12 y_{t-6} + y_{t-5} + \cdots + y_{t+5} + \tfrac12 y_{t+6}\right).

Every month of the year gets the same total weight, so the seasonal pattern averages out, and what’s left is the trend-cycle.

Your turn. Compute the 2×12 moving average of the steam demand with stats::filter(), which multiplies each window by a vector of weights. Find the first month it’s defined for, its value there, and its value for December 2019, month 72.

The last of the 13 weights is a half, like the first.

weights <- c(0.5, rep(1, 11), 0.5) / 12
trend <- stats::filter(y, weights, sides = 2)      # sides = 2: centered on each month
c(first_month = which(!is.na(trend))[1], first_value = trend[7], december_2019 = trend[72])

The first defined value is for month 7, July 2014, at 37.2 kt: it needs six months on each side. The last is for month 138, June 2025. So the trend is missing at both ends, which is exactly where you’d want it most. In December 2019, it’s 38.4. (Write stats::filter() in full: dplyr also has a filter(), which does something else.)

Classical decomposition

Classical decomposition goes one step further. It estimates the trend with the 2×12 moving average, subtracts it, and averages what’s left for each calendar month, to get the seasonal component. Those twelve seasonal indices are adjusted to sum to 0. The remainder is whatever is left over. decompose() does all of it.

Your turn. Decompose the steam demand, and look at the seasonal indices for January and August, in $figure.

August is month 8.

d <- decompose(y)
c(january = d$figure[1], august = d$figure[8], total = sum(d$figure))

January is 6.0 kt above the trend, and August 5.2 below, and the twelve indices sum to 0 (to within rounding error, 10^{-15}). Classical decomposition assumes that the seasonal pattern is the same every year, which can’t be quite true: Lesson 1 found the peak in December some years and February in others.

Removing the seasonal component gives the seasonally adjusted series, the trend plus the remainder. It’s what official statistics usually report, so that a month can be compared with the month before it.

Your turn. Compute the seasonally adjusted series, and compare December 2025, the last month, with its raw value.

Subtract the seasonal component, d$seasonal, which repeats the twelve indices over every year.

d <- decompose(y)
adjusted <- y - d$seasonal
c(raw = y[144], adjusted = adjusted[144])

December 2025 used 49.5 kt, the most of any month in the twelve years. But December is always high: seasonally adjusted, it’s 44.9. Comparing adjusted values tells you whether demand is really rising, rather than just being winter.

STL decomposition

STL (Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess) is the modern replacement. It estimates the trend and the seasonal component with local regression (Module 4’s smoothing), so the trend is defined right to the ends, and the seasonal pattern can change slowly over the years. Its main setting, s.window, is how many years the seasonal pattern is smoothed over: "periodic" forces it to be the same every year, and a number such as 13 lets it evolve.

st <- stl(y, s.window = 13)
autoplot(st) +
  labs(x = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

How strong are the trend and the seasonality? Compare the remainder’s variance with the variance of each component plus the remainder. The strength of seasonality and the strength of trend (Hyndman and Athanasopoulos’s definitions) are

F_S = \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(R_t)}{\text{Var}(S_t + R_t)}\right), \qquad F_T = \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(R_t)}{\text{Var}(T_t + R_t)}\right),

each between 0 (none) and 1 (all of the variation, apart from the remainder).

Your turn. Compute both from the STL decomposition.

The trend column is "trend".

st <- stl(y, s.window = 13)
R <- st$time.series[, "remainder"]
S <- st$time.series[, "seasonal"]
Tr <- st$time.series[, "trend"]
c(seasonal_strength = max(0, 1 - var(R) / var(S + R)), trend_strength = max(0, 1 - var(R) / var(Tr + R)))

A seasonal strength of 0.94 and a trend strength of 0.82. Both are strong, the seasonality especially. The remainder’s SD, 1.05 kt, is the month-to-month variation that neither the trend nor the season explains. A forecast can’t do much better than that, unless it brings in more information, like next month’s weather forecast.

Simple exponential smoothing

The plant’s hydrotreater has a catalyst bed whose pressure drop creeps up as it fouls and settles, and the operators read it every day. The readings are noisy, and the level they’re measuring drifts. What’s the best estimate of today’s level, and of tomorrow’s reading?

Taking the last reading trusts the noise. Taking the average of all 200 days ignores the drift. Simple exponential smoothing (SES) is in between: each new estimate of the level is a weighted average of the newest reading and the previous estimate,

\ell_t = \alpha\, y_t + (1 - \alpha)\,\ell_{t-1}, \qquad \hat y_{t+1} = \ell_t,

with a smoothing parameter \alpha between 0 and 1. Unrolled, \ell_t is a weighted average of all past readings, with weights \alpha(1-\alpha)^k that fall off exponentially with age k. With \alpha near 1, it’s the last reading, and near 0, an average over a long memory.

sm <- ses(dp, alpha = 0.35)
ggplot(data.frame(day = 1:200, dp = dp, level = as.numeric(fitted(sm))), aes(x = day)) +
  geom_point(aes(y = dp), color = "grey60", size = 1) +
  geom_line(aes(y = level), color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.9) +
  labs(x = "Day", y = "Pressure drop (bar)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Your turn. Run SES by hand, with \alpha = 0.3, starting the level at the first reading. At each step, record the one-step-ahead forecast error, y_t - \ell_{t-1}. Report the sum of squared errors and the final level.

The new level combines the new reading with the previous level.

ses_by_hand <- function(alpha) {
  level <- dp[1]
  err <- numeric(length(dp) - 1)
  for (t in 2:length(dp)) {
    err[t - 1] <- dp[t] - level                         # the forecast error
    level <- alpha * dp[t] + (1 - alpha) * level         # the new level
  }
  c(sse = sum(err^2), level = level)
}
ses_by_hand(0.3)

A sum of squared errors of 0.357, and a final level of 1.301 bar: the forecast for day 201. With \alpha = 1, the naive “last reading” forecast, the SSE is 0.492, and with \alpha = 0.05, 0.945. Neither extreme is as good as smoothing.

Your turn. Which \alpha is best? Minimize the sum of squared one-step errors with optimize().

Minimize the "sse" element.

ses_by_hand <- function(alpha) {
  level <- dp[1]; err <- numeric(length(dp) - 1)
  for (t in 2:length(dp)) { err[t - 1] <- dp[t] - level; level <- alpha * dp[t] + (1 - alpha) * level }
  c(sse = sum(err^2), level = level)
}
best <- optimize(function(a) ses_by_hand(a)[["sse"]], interval = c(0.01, 1))
c(alpha = best$minimum, sse = best$objective)

\alpha = 0.35, with an SSE of 0.355. About a third of each new reading goes into the level, so the forecast “remembers” roughly the last week of readings. The best \alpha depends on how much the level really drifts compared with the noise: more drift, larger \alpha.

Exponential smoothing with the forecast package

The forecast package’s ses() does the same, estimating \alpha and the starting level by maximum likelihood, and adds prediction intervals. Its relatives add a trend, holt(), and seasonality, hw(). And ets() chooses among the whole family of ETS models (Error, Trend, Seasonal), each component additive, multiplicative or absent, by AICc.

Your turn. Forecast the pressure drop 7 days ahead with ses(), and find \alpha, the forecast for day 201, its 80% interval, and the 95% interval for day 207.

Seven days ahead: h = 7.

fc <- forecast::ses(dp, h = 7)
c(alpha = fc$model$par[["alpha"]], day_201 = fc$mean[1], lo80 = fc$lower[1, 1], hi80 = fc$upper[1, 1],
  lo95_day207 = fc$lower[7, 2], hi95_day207 = fc$upper[7, 2])

\alpha = 0.349, as by hand. Tomorrow’s reading: 1.300 bar, with an 80% interval from 1.246 to 1.354. SES forecasts are flat, the same for every future day, but their intervals widen: a week out, the 95% interval runs from 1.19 to 1.41. The level could have drifted by then, and SES knows it.

Your turn. For the steam demand, let ets() choose the model, and forecast January 2026 with its 95% interval.

Forecast a year ahead, h = 12. (Any h of 1 or more gives the same January forecast.)

fit <- forecast::ets(y)
fit$method
fc <- forecast::forecast(fit, h = 12)
c(january_2026 = fc$mean[1], lo95 = fc$lower[1, 2], hi95 = fc$upper[1, 2])

ets() chose ETS(A,A,A): additive errors, an additive trend and an additive seasonal pattern, Holt-Winters’ additive method in statistical form. It forecasts 50.0 kt for January 2026, with a 95% prediction interval from 47.4 to 52.6. Its smoothing parameters for the trend and the season are tiny, 0.0001: the data say both are nearly constant, so the model barely updates them.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Is there a trend? The pressure drop is creeping up. Holt’s method adds a trend term to SES. Compare the two methods’ forecasts 30 days ahead.

Holt’s method is forecast::holt().

ses_fc <- forecast::ses(dp, h = 30)
holt_fc <- forecast::holt(dp, h = 30)
c(ses_day_230 = ses_fc$mean[30], holt_day_230 = holt_fc$mean[30], holt_beta = holt_fc$model$par[["beta"]])

SES says 1.30 bar on day 230, and Holt 1.38. Holt estimates a steady rise of about 0.0025 bar a day, with a trend smoothing parameter near 0, so the trend is treated as fixed. If the bed’s design limit were 1.4 bar, that difference would decide when to plan a catalyst change. Which forecast is better? Lesson 8 answers that with time-series cross-validation, not by eye.

Challenge 2 · Multiplicative seasonality. R’s built-in AirPassengers, monthly airline passengers from 1949 to 1960, is the classic multiplicative series: its seasonal swings grow with its level. Decompose it multiplicatively, and find the seasonal indices for July and November.

The type is "multiplicative".

d <- decompose(AirPassengers, type = "multiplicative")
c(july = d$figure[7], november = d$figure[11])

July is 23% above the trend and November 20% below. Multiplicative seasonal indices are ratios that average 1, so the swing in passengers grows as the airline grows. Taking logs turns a multiplicative series into an additive one, \log y_t = \log T_t + \log S_t + \log R_t, which is often the simplest way to handle it.

Quick quiz

1. Why does a 2×12 moving average remove a monthly seasonal pattern?

The seasonal ups and downs cancel over a full year.

2. What does a seasonally adjusted series show?

It lets you compare consecutive months without the season getting in the way.

3. Name two advantages of STL over classical decomposition.

Classical decomposition loses six months at each end and forces the same seasonal pattern every year.

4. In simple exponential smoothing, what happens as \alpha approaches 1?

All the weight goes to the newest observation: the naive forecast.

5. Why do SES prediction intervals widen with the forecast horizon, even though the point forecast is flat?

The model treats the level as wandering, so its uncertainty grows with time.

6. What does ETS(A,A,A) mean?

ETS names its Error, Trend and Seasonal components, each N (none), A (additive) or M (multiplicative).

Wrap-up

You can now take a series apart, and forecast it with smoothing. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Moving averages: a 2×12 moving average estimates the trend of monthly data, but loses six months at each end.
  • Classical decomposition: January’s steam demand is 6.0 kt above the trend and August’s 5.2 below. December 2025’s record 49.5 kt is 44.9 seasonally adjusted.
  • STL handles the ends and an evolving season. The steam demand has a seasonal strength of 0.94 and a trend strength of 0.82.
  • Simple exponential smoothing weights recent readings exponentially more. For the pressure drop, the best \alpha is 0.35, and tomorrow’s forecast is 1.30 bar (80% interval 1.25 to 1.35).
  • The ETS family adds trends and seasons, and ets() chooses among them: ETS(A,A,A) for the steam demand, forecasting 50.0 kt for January 2026.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Decomposition
Writing a series as a trend-cycle, a seasonal component and a remainder, added or multiplied.
Moving average
An average of each observation with its neighbors. A 2×12 moving average estimates the trend of monthly data.
Seasonally adjusted series
The series with its seasonal component removed.
STL
Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess, which allows a changing seasonal pattern and estimates the trend to the ends.
Strength of seasonality, strength of trend
How much of the variation, apart from the remainder, each component explains, on a scale from 0 to 1.
Simple exponential smoothing
Forecasting with a level that’s a weighted average of past observations, with weights falling off exponentially with age.
Smoothing parameter
The weight \alpha that SES gives to each new observation.
ETS model
A model of the exponential smoothing family, named by its Error, Trend and Seasonal components.
