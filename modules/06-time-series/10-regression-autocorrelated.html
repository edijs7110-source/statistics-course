Regression with autocorrelated errors

Explaining a series with another one, honestly

Regress the steam demand on the heating degree days and a trend, and see how autocorrelated residuals make ordinary least squares’ standard errors too small. Fit the regression with AR(1) errors, by maximum likelihood with Arima() and by generalized least squares with gls(), check that the weather removes the structure Lesson 9 left behind, and forecast under normal and cold weather. Finish with spurious regression: two unrelated random walks that look strongly related.

Module 6 · Lesson 10 of 13 Week 99 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Lesson 9’s seasonal models forecast the steam demand from the calendar alone, and left behind structure only the weather could explain. The natural fix is a regression: steam demand depends on the heating degree days, and on the plant’s slow growth,

y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{HDD}_t + \beta_2\, t + \eta_t .

Module 2 would fit that by least squares, and trust its standard errors. But here the error term \eta_t is a time series itself: a cold spell, a slow month of production or a long shutdown affects several months in a row. Ordinary least squares assumes the errors are independent. When they aren’t, its estimates are still unbiased, but its standard errors are wrong, usually too small, and every test and interval built on them is overconfident.

The fix is to model the errors too: regression with ARIMA errors, where \eta_t follows an ARMA model, and its white-noise part, \varepsilon_t, is what’s left.

Steam follows the weather, but the weather has memory, and so do the errors. Let’s give the regression a memory too!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • fit a regression of one series on another, and find its autocorrelated residuals
  • measure the autocorrelation with the Durbin-Watson statistic
  • fit a regression with AR(1) errors, with Arima() and with generalized least squares
  • check that the predictor removes the structure a seasonal model left behind
  • forecast with a predictor, under different scenarios
  • recognize spurious regression between unrelated trending series

Least squares, and its residuals

Your turn. Fit the regression by least squares, and look at the coefficients, their standard errors, and the residuals’ lag-1 autocorrelation.

Lag 1 is the second element of the ACF.

ols <- lm(steam_kt ~ hdd + t, data = steam)
c(coef(ols), se = summary(ols)$coefficients[, "Std. Error"], lag1 = acf(resid(ols), plot = FALSE)$acf[2])

Each extra heating degree day adds 0.0229 kt of steam (SE 0.0005), and demand grows by 0.051 kt a month (SE 0.0022), on top of the weather. But the residuals have a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.57: a month with more steam than the weather explains is usually followed by another. So these standard errors can’t be trusted.

The classical check for this is the Durbin-Watson statistic,

DW = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^n (e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^n e_t^2} \approx 2(1 - r_1),

which is near 2 for independent residuals, and near 0 for strongly positively autocorrelated ones.

Your turn. Compute it.

The numerator adds up the squared changes, diff(e)^2.

e <- resid(lm(steam_kt ~ hdd + t, data = steam))
sum(diff(e)^2) / sum(e^2)

0.84, far below 2, and close to 2(1 - 0.57) = 0.86. lmtest::dwtest() turns it into a formal test, but with a value this low, there’s no doubt. Module 2 met the Durbin-Watson test as a regression diagnostic. For time series data, it should be the first thing you check.

Regression with AR(1) errors

Arima() fits the regression and the error model together, by maximum likelihood, when you give it the predictors as xreg. With order = c(1, 0, 0), the errors are AR(1): \eta_t = \phi\,\eta_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t.

Your turn. Fit it, and compare the coefficients and standard errors with least squares.

The predictors are in the setup code’s matrix X.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), xreg = X)
rbind(estimate = coef(fit), se = sqrt(diag(vcov(fit))))

The error AR coefficient is 0.58. The coefficients barely move, 0.0226 kt per degree day and 0.051 kt a month of growth, but their standard errors grow: from 0.0005 to 0.0007 for the heating degree days, and from 0.0022 to 0.0041 for the trend, almost twice as large. Least squares claimed the growth rate was known twice as precisely as it is. With the honest standard error, the 95% interval for the yearly growth is 12 \times (0.051 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0041), from 0.52 to 0.71 kt a year.

Your turn. Now the decisive check. Lesson 9’s seasonal model left residual autocorrelation at lags 5, 9 and 18. Test this model’s residuals over 24 lags, and count how many autocorrelations are outside \pm 1.96/\sqrt{144}.

One ARMA coefficient in the errors: fitdf = 1.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), xreg = X)
r <- acf(residuals(fit), lag.max = 24, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1]
c(ljung_box_p = Box.test(residuals(fit), lag = 24, type = "Ljung-Box", fitdf = 1)$p.value,
  outside = sum(abs(r) > 1.96 / sqrt(144)), lag5 = r[5], lag9 = r[9], lag18 = r[18])

The residuals pass easily, p = 0.62, and none of the 24 autocorrelations is outside the bounds. Lags 5, 9 and 18 are now −0.06, 0.00 and 0.12. The weather was the missing piece. A model built on the cause of the seasonality, rather than its calendar, explained it better, with fewer parameters, and with no seasonal differencing at all.

Generalized least squares

Another route to the same model is generalized least squares (GLS), from Module 2, with a correlation structure for the errors. The nlme package’s gls() fits a regression with AR(1) errors, correlation = corAR1(), using the formula interface.

Your turn. Fit the model with gls(), and find the coefficients, the estimated AR coefficient \phi and the standard errors.

The AR(1) correlation structure is corAR1().

g <- nlme::gls(steam_kt ~ hdd + t, data = steam, correlation = nlme::corAR1())
c(coef(g), phi = coef(g$modelStruct$corStruct, unconstrained = FALSE), se = sqrt(diag(vcov(g))))

Nearly the same as Arima(): 0.0226 per degree day and 0.051 a month, with standard errors 0.0007 and 0.0044, and \hat\phi = 0.60. (gls() maximizes the restricted likelihood, REML, so its numbers differ slightly.) gls() fits into the usual regression workflow, with summary(), anova() and predict(), and handles irregularly spaced data with corCAR1(). Arima() is the better tool for forecasting, and for more complex error models.

Forecasting with a predictor

There’s a catch to forecasting with a regression: you need the predictors’ future values. The trend is easy. Next year’s heating degree days aren’t known, so the forecast has to assume something. A common choice is the climatology: each month’s average over past years.

Your turn. Forecast 2026 with the average heating degree days for each calendar month, and find January’s forecast, its 95% interval, and July’s.

2026 is months 145 to 156.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), xreg = X)
normal_hdd <- tapply(steam$hdd, as.integer(substr(steam$month, 6, 7)), mean)     # the average for each month
future_X <- cbind(hdd = as.numeric(normal_hdd), t = 145:156)
fc <- forecast(fit, xreg = future_X)
c(normal_jan_hdd = normal_hdd[[1]], january = fc$mean[1], lo95 = fc$lower[1, 2], hi95 = fc$upper[1, 2], july = fc$mean[7])

With a normal January, 515 degree days, the forecast is 50.0 kt, with a 95% interval from 48.2 to 51.8, and July 38.5. That interval is narrower than Lesson 9’s, 47.6 to 52.9, but it’s not a fair comparison: it’s conditional on January’s weather being exactly normal. It leaves out the weather’s own uncertainty, which is the biggest uncertainty of all. Forecasts with predictors are best presented as scenarios.

Your turn. The weather service forecasts a cold January, 600 degree days. How much does that change January’s steam forecast?

January’s degree days are 600.

fit <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), xreg = X)
normal_hdd <- tapply(steam$hdd, as.integer(substr(steam$month, 6, 7)), mean)
normal <- forecast(fit, xreg = cbind(hdd = as.numeric(normal_hdd), t = 145:156))
cold <- forecast(fit, xreg = cbind(hdd = c(600, normal_hdd[2:12]), t = 145:156))
c(normal = normal$mean[1], cold = cold$mean[1], difference = cold$mean[1] - normal$mean[1])

51.9 kt instead of 50.0: 85 extra degree days, times 0.0226 kt each, is 1.9 kt more steam. That’s what the boiler house can plan for: “if January is as cold as forecast, we’ll need about 2 kt more steam than usual.” A calendar model like Lesson 9’s can’t answer that question at all.

Spurious regression

The opposite danger: two series that seem related, but aren’t. Any two series that both trend, or both wander, will be correlated over a stretch of time, and a regression of one on the other will look highly significant. Granger and Newbold called it spurious regression in 1974.

Your turn. Simulate two independent random walks of 200 steps, regress one on the other, and look at the R² and the slope’s p-value. Then do the same with their differences.

Regress the changes on the changes: diff(a).

set.seed(6101)
a <- cumsum(rnorm(200))
b <- cumsum(rnorm(200))
levels <- summary(lm(b ~ a))
changes <- summary(lm(diff(b) ~ diff(a)))
c(r2_levels = levels$r.squared, p_levels = levels$coefficients[2, 4], r2_changes = changes$r.squared, p_changes = changes$coefficients[2, 4])

In levels: R² = 0.62, and a p-value of 10^{-43}, for two series that have nothing to do with each other. In differences: R² = 0.004, p = 0.38, the truth. Each random walk wanders, and over 200 steps, their wanderings happen to line up. The regression’s residuals are a random walk too, so the independence assumption fails completely. The rule: never regress one non-stationary series on another without checking the residuals, or differencing. Lesson 12 covers the one exception, cointegration.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · How often? One spurious regression could be bad luck. Repeat the simulation 1,000 times, and find how often the slope is “significant” at the 5% level.

The 5% level is 0.05.

set.seed(6102)
p_values <- replicate(1000, {
  a <- cumsum(rnorm(200))
  b <- cumsum(rnorm(200))
  summary(lm(b ~ a))$coefficients[2, 4]
})
mean(p_values < 0.05)

83% of the regressions find a “significant” relationship, where a valid test would find one 5% of the time. And the rate gets worse with longer series, not better. Plant data are full of slowly drifting series, such as fouling, catalyst age, ambient temperature and inventories. Regressing one on another is a reliable way to discover relationships that aren’t there.

Challenge 2 · How much did the weather explain? Compare the shock variance of the regression with AR(1) errors with that of Lesson 9’s best seasonal model, ARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}.

Lesson 9’s model has a seasonal MA(1): seasonal = c(0, 1, 1).

with_weather <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), xreg = X)
calendar_only <- Arima(y, order = c(1, 1, 1), seasonal = c(0, 1, 1))
c(with_weather = with_weather$sigma2, calendar_only = calendar_only$sigma2, ratio = with_weather$sigma2 / calendar_only$sigma2)

0.82 against 1.80: knowing the month’s weather removes more than half of the one-step-ahead forecast variance. Most of what the calendar model calls “noise” is weather. That’s the general lesson of this module’s first half: a good predictor beats a clever model of the series’ own past.

Quick quiz

1. What goes wrong with ordinary least squares when the errors are positively autocorrelated?

The estimates are still unbiased. It’s their claimed precision that’s wrong.

2. A regression has a Durbin-Watson statistic of 0.8. What does that suggest?

DW \approx 2(1 - r_1). Values near 2 are fine.

3. What does Arima(y, order = c(1, 0, 0), xreg = X) fit?

The ARIMA order describes the errors, not y itself.

4. Why is the regression’s forecast interval for January narrower than the seasonal ARIMA’s?

Forecasts with predictors are conditional on the predictors’ future values.

5. Two independent random walks are regressed on each other. What usually happens?

That’s spurious regression. Regress the differences instead, or test for cointegration.

6. What’s the advantage of gls() over Arima() for a regression with AR(1) errors?

Arima() is the better tool for forecasting.

Wrap-up

You can now regress one series on another without fooling yourself. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Least squares found 0.0229 kt of steam per degree day and 0.051 kt a month of growth, but its residuals had a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.57 and a Durbin-Watson statistic of 0.84.
  • Regression with AR(1) errors (\hat\phi = 0.58) kept the coefficients, and nearly doubled the trend’s standard error, to 0.0041. gls() with corAR1() agrees.
  • The weather explained the leftovers: the residuals pass the Ljung-Box test (p = 0.62), and the shock variance is less than half the seasonal ARIMA’s (0.82 against 1.80).
  • Forecasts are conditional on the predictors: 50.0 kt for a normal January, and 1.9 kt more if it’s as cold as 600 degree days.
  • Spurious regression: two unrelated random walks gave R² = 0.62, and 83% of such pairs look “significant”.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Regression with ARIMA errors
A regression whose error term follows an ARIMA model, fitted with the regression by maximum likelihood.
Durbin-Watson statistic
\sum(e_t - e_{t-1})^2/\sum e_t^2, about 2(1 - r_1): a check for autocorrelated regression residuals.
Generalized least squares (GLS)
Least squares that allows for correlated or unequal-variance errors, such as gls() with corAR1().
Conditional forecast
A forecast that assumes given future values of the predictors.
Climatology
The average weather for each time of year, used as a “normal” scenario.
Spurious regression
An apparently significant relationship between unrelated non-stationary series.
