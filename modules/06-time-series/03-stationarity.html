Stationarity and autocorrelation

Series that settle down, and series that wander

Compare white noise, a random walk, a controlled reactor temperature and a tank level. Read their autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions, make the wandering tank level stationary by differencing, and confirm it with the augmented Dickey-Fuller and KPSS tests. Finish with the Ljung-Box test and why a random walk’s variance grows without limit.

Module 6 · Lesson 3 of 13 Week 92 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Most time series models, including the ARMA models of the next three lessons, assume that the series is stationary: its behavior doesn’t depend on when you look at it. Its mean and variance are constant, and the correlation between two observations depends only on how far apart they are, not on the date. A stationary series fluctuates around a fixed level and keeps coming back to it.

Many real series aren’t like that. They wander, trend, or have seasons. This lesson is about telling the two kinds apart, and about the simplest way of turning one into the other, differencing. The examples come from the plant’s control system: 600 minutes of a reactor temperature’s deviation from its set point, under PID control, and the level of an intermediate tank, which simply accumulates whatever flows in minus whatever flows out.

A controlled temperature always comes home to its set point. A tank level goes wherever the flows take it. Same plant, very different statistics!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • recognize white noise, and its autocorrelation function
  • see why a random walk isn’t stationary
  • read the ACF and PACF of real process signals
  • make a series stationary by differencing
  • test for a unit root with the augmented Dickey-Fuller and KPSS tests
  • test for any autocorrelation with the Ljung-Box test

White noise

The simplest stationary series is white noise: independent draws with mean 0 and a constant variance. It has no memory at all, so its autocorrelations at every lag beyond 0 are 0. In a sample, they won’t be exactly 0, but for white noise of length n, about 95% of them should be within \pm 1.96/\sqrt n. Those are the dashed lines on every ACF plot.

Your turn. Simulate 600 values of white noise, compute their autocorrelations at lags 1 to 20, and count how many fall outside \pm 1.96/\sqrt{600}.

The series has 600 values.

set.seed(6031)
e <- rnorm(600)
r <- acf(e, lag.max = 20, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1]          # drop lag 0
c(lag1 = r[1], outside = sum(abs(r) > 1.96 / sqrt(600)))

The lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.05, and none of the 20 is outside \pm 0.080. On average, 1 in 20 would be, by chance, so a single autocorrelation just outside the lines is no evidence of anything. A pattern, or a large spike at a meaningful lag such as 1 or 12, is.

Random walks

Add up white noise, and you get a random walk: y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t. Each step is independent, but the level is the sum of all of them, so it wanders off and never comes back on purpose. Its variance grows with time, \text{Var}(y_t) = t\sigma^2, so it isn’t stationary. A random walk is said to have a unit root, because the coefficient on y_{t-1} is exactly 1.

Your turn. Simulate a random walk of 600 steps, and look at its autocorrelations at lags 1 and 30.

A random walk is the cumulative sum of the steps: cumsum().

set.seed(6032)
w <- cumsum(rnorm(600))
r <- acf(w, lag.max = 30, plot = FALSE)$acf
c(lag1 = r[2], lag30 = r[31])

0.99 at lag 1, and still 0.82 at lag 30. That slow, almost linear decay is the signature of a non-stationary series in an ACF plot. (Strictly, a random walk has no ACF, since it isn’t stationary. The sample ACF still computes, and that’s what it looks like.)

Two process signals

Here are the plant’s two signals:

sig <- rbind(data.frame(minute = ps$minute, value = ps$temp_dev_C, signal = "reactor temperature, deviation from set point (°C)"),
             data.frame(minute = ps$minute, value = ps$tank_level_pct, signal = "tank level (%)"))
ggplot(sig, aes(x = minute, y = value)) +
  geom_line(color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.4) +
  facet_wrap(~ signal, ncol = 1, scales = "free_y") +
  labs(x = "Minute", y = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The temperature keeps returning to 0: the controller sees to that. The tank level has no controller holding it at a target, so it goes wherever the flows take it.

Your turn. Compute the tank level’s autocorrelations at lags 1, 10 and 30.

The tank level is in the setup code’s tank.

r <- acf(tank, lag.max = 30, plot = FALSE)$acf
c(lag1 = r[2], lag10 = r[11], lag30 = r[31])

0.99, 0.86 and 0.52: the slow decay of a random walk. Half an hour later, the level still remembers where it was, because it’s an accumulation, an integral of the flow imbalance. In chemical engineering terms, a tank without level control is an integrating process, and integrating processes are non-stationary.

Differencing

If the level is a random walk, its changes are white noise. Differencing, y'_t = y_t - y_{t-1}, removes a unit root, and often makes a wandering series stationary. Lesson 1’s seasonal difference, diff(y, lag = 12), does the same for a seasonal pattern.

Your turn. Difference the tank level, and look at the lag-1 autocorrelation, the mean and the SD of the changes.

diff() computes the differences.

changes <- diff(tank)
c(lag1 = acf(changes, plot = FALSE)$acf[2], mean = mean(changes), sd = sd(changes))

A lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.009: the changes look like white noise, with an SD of 0.34 percentage points a minute, and a mean of 0.01, so essentially no drift. The tank level is a random walk, a model with no parameters to estimate beyond that SD. The next lessons build models for series whose differences, or whose levels, still have structure.

Unit-root tests

Looking at plots is the first step, but there are formal tests too. The augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test regresses the change \Delta y_t on the previous level y_{t-1} (and some lagged changes). If the series has a unit root, the coefficient on y_{t-1} is 0. The null hypothesis is a unit root (non-stationary), so a small p-value is evidence of stationarity. tseries::adf.test() runs it.

Your turn. Run the ADF test on the tank level and on its differences.

Test the differenced series, diff(tank), too.

c(level = tseries::adf.test(tank)$p.value, changes = tseries::adf.test(diff(tank))$p.value)

p = 0.52 for the level: no evidence against a unit root. For the changes, p = 0.01 (the test only reports p-values down to 0.01, and warns when the real one is smaller): strong evidence that they’re stationary. So the tank level is integrated of order 1, I(1): one difference makes it stationary.

The KPSS test turns the hypotheses around: its null is stationarity, so a small p-value is evidence of a unit root. Using both is good practice, since unit-root tests have low power, and a large p-value from either isn’t proof.

Your turn. Run the KPSS test on the tank level and on the temperature.

Use kpss.test() for both.

c(tank = tseries::kpss.test(tank)$p.value, temperature = tseries::kpss.test(temp)$p.value)

For the tank, p = 0.01 (or smaller): KPSS rejects stationarity. For the temperature, p = 0.10 (or larger): no evidence against stationarity. The two tests agree on both signals. (KPSS reports p-values only between 0.01 and 0.10, from a table, and warns at the ends.)

The partial autocorrelation function

The temperature is stationary, but far from white noise. Its ACF falls off fast, oscillating as it goes, and that alone doesn’t say what model would describe it. The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) helps: the partial autocorrelation at lag k is the correlation between y_t and y_{t-k} after removing the effect of the lags in between, 1 to k - 1. It’s the last coefficient in a regression of y_t on its first k lags.

a <- acf(ps$temp_dev_C, lag.max = 20, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1]
pa <- pacf(ps$temp_dev_C, lag.max = 20, plot = FALSE)$acf
cf <- rbind(data.frame(lag = 1:20, value = a, fn = "ACF"), data.frame(lag = 1:20, value = as.numeric(pa), fn = "PACF"))
ggplot(cf, aes(x = lag, y = value)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, color = "grey50") +
  geom_hline(yintercept = c(-1, 1) * 1.96 / sqrt(600), linetype = "dashed", color = "#2a78d6") +
  geom_segment(aes(xend = lag, yend = 0), color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 1) +
  facet_wrap(~ fn) +
  labs(x = "Lag (minutes)", y = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Your turn. Find the temperature’s partial autocorrelations at lags 1 to 4. (Unlike acf(), pacf() starts at lag 1.)

Lags 1 to 4 are the first 4 elements.

pacf(temp, lag.max = 5, plot = FALSE)$acf[1:4]

0.81, −0.59, −0.02 and 0.04. Two large partial autocorrelations, then nothing: each minute’s temperature depends directly on the last two minutes, and once those are known, earlier minutes add nothing. That pattern points to an autoregressive model of order 2, AR(2), the subject of Lesson 4. The negative second coefficient is what makes a controlled loop oscillate: an overshoot is followed by a correction.

Your turn. The forecast package’s ndiffs() runs a unit-root test repeatedly to decide how many differences a series needs. Ask it about both signals.

The temperature is temp.

c(tank = forecast::ndiffs(tank), temperature = forecast::ndiffs(temp))

One difference for the tank, none for the temperature. Lesson 6’s auto.arima() uses ndiffs() this way to choose the “I” in ARIMA. Differencing a stationary series, overdifferencing, would be a mistake: it adds a characteristic pattern to the ACF, a large negative autocorrelation at lag 1, that Lesson 5 explains.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Any autocorrelation at all? Testing each autocorrelation separately invites false alarms. The Ljung-Box test combines the first h of them into one statistic, Q = n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^h r_k^2/(n-k), whose null hypothesis is white noise. Run it with 10 lags on the white noise from earlier and on the temperature.

Use type = "Ljung-Box" for both.

set.seed(6031)
e <- rnorm(600)
c(white_noise = Box.test(e, lag = 10, type = "Ljung-Box")$p.value,
  temperature = Box.test(temp, lag = 10, type = "Ljung-Box")$p.value)

p = 0.63 for the white noise, and effectively 0 for the temperature, with Q = 600 on 10 degrees of freedom. Lesson 7 uses this test on a model’s residuals: if the model has captured the structure, they should look like white noise.

Challenge 2 · A random walk’s variance. Simulate 2,000 random walks of 400 steps each, and compare the variance of their values at step 100 with the variance at step 400. The theory says t\sigma^2.

Row 400 holds the 400th step of every walk.

set.seed(6033)
walks <- replicate(2000, cumsum(rnorm(400)))       # 400 steps x 2,000 walks
c(step_100 = var(walks[100, ]), step_400 = var(walks[400, ]))

101 and 396, close to the theoretical 100 and 400: the variance grows in proportion to time, so the spread grows with its square root. That’s why a random walk’s forecasts have prediction intervals that keep widening without limit, while a stationary series’ intervals settle at a fixed width.

Quick quiz

1. What makes a series stationary?

No trend is necessary, but not sufficient: a random walk has no trend and still isn’t stationary.

2. A sample ACF decays very slowly, almost linearly, from 0.99. What does that suggest?

Difference it and look again.

3. What is the null hypothesis of the augmented Dickey-Fuller test?

A small p-value is evidence of stationarity. KPSS has the opposite null.

4. The PACF has two large spikes, at lags 1 and 2, and nothing after. Which model does that suggest?

An AR(p) process has partial autocorrelations of 0 beyond lag p.

5. Why is an uncontrolled tank level non-stationary?

Integrating processes have a unit root. A level controller would make the level stationary.

6. What does a Ljung-Box test on 10 lags check?

It’s most useful on a model’s residuals, in Lesson 7.

Wrap-up

You can now tell a series that settles from one that wanders. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • White noise has autocorrelations near 0, within \pm 1.96/\sqrt n, and passes the Ljung-Box test (p = 0.63).
  • A random walk is the sum of white noise. Its variance grows with time, and its sample ACF decays slowly: 0.99 at lag 1 and 0.82 at lag 30.
  • The tank level is a random walk, an integrating process, and differencing makes it stationary.
  • Unit-root tests agree: ADF finds a unit root in the level (p = 0.52) but not in the changes, and KPSS rejects stationarity for the tank but not for the temperature.
  • The PACF of the controlled temperature cuts off after lag 2 (0.81, −0.59), pointing to an AR(2) model.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Stationary series
A series whose mean, variance and autocorrelations don’t change over time.
White noise
Independent observations with mean 0 and constant variance: no autocorrelation at any lag.
Random walk
A series whose changes are white noise: y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t. It has a unit root and isn’t stationary.
Unit root
A coefficient of exactly 1 on the previous value, which makes a series non-stationary.
Differencing
Replacing a series by its changes, y_t - y_{t-1}, to remove a unit root.
Integrated of order d, I(d)
A series that becomes stationary after d differences.
Augmented Dickey-Fuller test
A test whose null hypothesis is a unit root.
KPSS test
A test whose null hypothesis is stationarity.
Partial autocorrelation function (PACF)
The correlation between y_t and y_{t-k} after removing the effect of the lags in between.
Ljung-Box test
A test of whether the first h autocorrelations, taken together, are consistent with white noise.
