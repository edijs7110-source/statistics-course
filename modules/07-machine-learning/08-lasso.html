Lasso and elastic net

Shrinkage that chooses

Swap ridge’s squared penalty for an absolute-value one, and some coefficients become exactly zero: the lasso selects predictors as it fits. Let it pick the soft sensor’s tags from twenty candidates, with cross-validation folds that respect time, and see why its chosen coefficients are biased and how refitting fixes them. Then see where the lasso struggles, on correlated spectra, and how the elastic net helps, and fit a lasso logistic regression to the pump failures.

Module 7 · Lesson 8 of 14 Week 111 of 160 About 5–7 hours

Ridge regression kept every predictor and shrank them all. That’s ideal for spectra, where every wavelength carries a little of the signal. But the soft sensor of Lesson 2 is a different kind of problem: 20 candidate tags, of which the chemistry says only four should matter. There, you’d like a method that selects: sets the useless coefficients to exactly zero, and leaves a model that an engineer can read.

Module 2 selected predictors with stepwise searches, which are greedy, unstable and hard to cross-validate properly. The lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) does it in one smooth optimization, with a single tuning parameter. Its only change from ridge is the penalty:

\hat\beta^{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_\beta \; \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\Big)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p |\beta_j| .

That small change, |\beta_j| instead of \beta_j^2, makes coefficients hit exactly zero, as the Go deeper box explains.

Ridge turns every knob down a little. The lasso switches some knobs off completely. When you suspect most of your tags are noise, that’s exactly what you want.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • fit the lasso path with glmnet, and see predictors enter one at a time
  • choose \lambda by cross-validation with time-blocked folds, and read off the selected tags
  • see the lasso’s bias, and remove it by refitting the selected model
  • see the lasso struggle with correlated predictors, and the elastic net blend it with ridge
  • fit a lasso logistic regression, scored by AUC

The lasso path

The setup code builds the soft sensor’s predictor matrix Xs (the log hydrogen ratio and the 19 other tags) and response ys (log MI), and the time blocks from Lesson 2: 10 folds of 50 consecutive samples. It also loads the spectra and the pump data, for later.

Your turn. Fit the lasso path to the soft-sensor data with glmnet(..., alpha = 1), and find the order in which the tags enter the model as \lambda falls: for each tag, the largest \lambda at which its coefficient is nonzero.

The lasso is alpha = 1 (it’s glmnet’s default, too).

fit <- glmnet::glmnet(Xs, ys, alpha = 1)
enter <- apply(as.matrix(fit$beta) != 0, 1, function(nz) fit$lambda[which(nz)[1]])
head(sort(enter, decreasing = TRUE), 5)

The hydrogen ratio enters first, at \lambda = 0.83, then the reactor temperature (0.097), the ethylene pressure (0.056) and the comonomer ratio (0.032): the four chemistry tags, in order of importance, before any of the others. The fifth, the isopentane concentration, comes in at 0.014, by which point the lasso is starting to fit noise. fit$beta is the matrix of coefficients, one column per \lambda.

fit <- glmnet(Xs, ys, alpha = 1)
B <- as.matrix(fit$beta)
paths <- data.frame(loglam = rep(log(fit$lambda), each = 20), beta = as.vector(B),
                    tag = rep(rownames(B), length(fit$lambda)))
paths$group <- ifelse(paths$tag %in% c("log_h2", "reactor_temp_C", "c2_pressure_bar", "comonomer_ratio"), paths$tag, "the other 16 tags")
ggplot(paths, aes(loglam, beta, group = tag, color = group)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, color = "grey80") +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.6) +
  scale_x_reverse() +
  scale_color_manual(values = c(log_h2 = "#C2416C", reactor_temp_C = "#2a78d6", c2_pressure_bar = "#1baf7a",
                                comonomer_ratio = "#8a5a00", `the other 16 tags` = "grey70"), name = NULL) +

NoteGo deeper: why the lasso gives exact zeros

Both penalized problems can be written as constrained ones: minimize the RSS subject to \sum_j \beta_j^2 \le s (ridge) or \sum_j |\beta_j| \le s (lasso). In two dimensions, ridge’s constraint region is a disk, and the lasso’s is a diamond with corners on the axes. The RSS has elliptical contours around the least-squares estimate. The solution is where the smallest contour touches the region, and an ellipse usually touches a diamond at a corner, where one coefficient is zero; a disk has no corners. Algebraically, with orthonormal predictors, the two have simple forms in terms of the least-squares \hat\beta_j: ridge gives \hat\beta_j/(1 + \lambda), a proportional shrink, and the lasso gives \text{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\,(|\hat\beta_j| - \lambda/2)_+, soft thresholding: subtract a constant, and set to zero anything that would cross zero. glmnet fits the lasso by cyclic coordinate descent, applying soft thresholding to one coefficient at a time. ISLR’s figure 6.7 draws the diamond and the disk, and its section 6.2.2 does the algebra.

Choosing λ, honestly

Lesson 2 found that random folds flatter models on the soft-sensor data, because neighboring samples share the catalyst drift. cv.glmnet() takes the folds you give it through foldid.

Your turn. Cross-validate the lasso with the time blocks as folds, and list the tags with nonzero coefficients at lambda.min.

The folds are the time blocks: foldid = block.

cv <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(Xs, ys, alpha = 1, foldid = block)
b <- coef(cv, s = "lambda.min")
setNames(as.numeric(b), rownames(b))[as.numeric(b) != 0]

Exactly the four chemistry tags, and none of the other 16: the lasso has rediscovered the chemistry from the data. The blocked CV RMSE at lambda.min is 0.207. With foldid, no random numbers are used, so the result is the same every time.

Your turn. Find the tags kept at lambda.1se, and compare the blocked CV RMSE at the two values of \lambda.

Compare with cv$lambda.1se.

cv <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(Xs, ys, alpha = 1, foldid = block)
b1 <- coef(cv, s = "lambda.1se")
print(rownames(b1)[as.numeric(b1) != 0])
sqrt(c(at_min = cv$cvm[cv$lambda == cv$lambda.min], at_1se = cv$cvm[cv$lambda == cv$lambda.1se]))

At lambda.1se, the comonomer ratio drops out, leaving three tags, and the CV RMSE rises from 0.207 to 0.226. cvm holds the CV mean squared errors, hence the square root. The comonomer is a real but small effect, and the one-standard-error rule, which prefers simpler models, sacrifices it. Neither choice is wrong; the rule is a heuristic. Here, the chemistry says the comonomer matters, which argues for lambda.min.

The lasso’s bias, and refitting

The lasso’s blocked CV RMSE, 0.207, is worse than Lesson 2’s least-squares fit on the same four tags (0.194, on the same blocks). That’s not a contradiction. The penalty shrinks the coefficients it keeps, not just the ones it drops, so the selected coefficients are biased toward zero. When the selection is right, that bias is pure cost.

Your turn. Compare the lasso’s coefficients at lambda.min with a least-squares fit of the same four tags.

Refit with the kept columns: Xs[, kept].

cv <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(Xs, ys, alpha = 1, foldid = block)
b <- as.matrix(coef(cv, s = "lambda.min"))[-1, 1]
kept <- names(b)[b != 0]
refit <- lm(ys ~ Xs[, kept])
round(rbind(lasso = b[kept], least_squares = coef(refit)[-1]), 4)

Every lasso coefficient is smaller in size than least squares’: hydrogen 0.997 against 1.035, temperature 0.083 against 0.100, and the comonomer ratio 4.2 against 8.1, half its size. The comonomer entered the path last, so it has been shrunk the most. Using the lasso to select and least squares to estimate is called the relaxed lasso (glmnet does a tuned version with relax = TRUE), and least squares on these four tags had a blocked CV RMSE of 0.194 in Lesson 2. The catch: the least-squares standard errors after selection are too small, because they ignore that the data chose the model. Honest inference after selection is a research topic of its own.

Correlated predictors: where the lasso struggles

The soft sensor suits the lasso: a few clear signals among independent-ish noise. The spectra are the opposite: the signal is spread over many wavelengths that are all correlated with each other.

Your turn. Fit the lasso to the spectra (the same 80 calibration samples and the same long \lambda grid as in Lesson 7), and find the number of wavelengths it keeps and the test RMSE.

The lasso is alpha = 1.

set.seed(7053)
cv_l <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 1, lambda = lam)
b <- as.numeric(coef(cv_l, s = "lambda.min"))[-1]
c(kept = sum(b != 0), test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - predict(cv_l, X[-train, ], s = "lambda.min"))^2)))

41 wavelengths, and a test RMSE of 0.49, against ridge’s 0.416. Among a group of highly correlated predictors, the lasso tends to keep one or a few and drop the rest, more or less arbitrarily. On spectra, averaging over many neighboring wavelengths is what cancels the noise, and that’s what ridge does and the lasso refuses to do.

The elastic net mixes the two penalties:

\lambda \Big( \alpha \sum_j |\beta_j| + \frac{1 - \alpha}{2} \sum_j \beta_j^2 \Big),

with \alpha = 1 the lasso and \alpha = 0 ridge, which is what glmnet’s alpha argument has meant all along. The ridge part makes correlated predictors share their coefficient, the grouping effect, while the lasso part still sets the useless ones to zero.

Your turn. Compare \alpha = 1, 0.5, 0.1 and 0 on the spectra: the number of wavelengths kept and the test RMSE.

Use the function’s argument: alpha = a.

t(sapply(c(1, 0.5, 0.1, 0), function(a) {
  set.seed(7053)
  cv_a <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = a, lambda = lam)
  b <- as.numeric(coef(cv_a, s = "lambda.min"))[-1]
  c(alpha = a, kept = sum(b != 0), test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - predict(cv_a, X[-train, ], s = "lambda.min"))^2)))
}))

As \alpha falls from 1 to 0, the number of wavelengths kept rises (41, 52, 70, 100) and the test RMSE falls (0.49, 0.45, 0.43, 0.42). For spectra, the more ridge, the better. For the soft sensor, it would be the other way round. \alpha is a second tuning parameter, and it can be chosen by cross-validation too: Lesson 14’s tools tune both at once.

Lasso logistic regression

The same penalties work for any likelihood: glmnet maximizes the log-likelihood minus the penalty. With family = "binomial", that’s penalized logistic regression, and type.measure = "auc" makes cv.glmnet() choose \lambda by the cross-validated AUC of Lesson 6. glmnet needs a numeric matrix, so categorical predictors become dummy columns with model.matrix().

Your turn. Fit a lasso logistic regression to the pump failures, with all seven measurements and the site as candidates, and look at the coefficients at lambda.min.

Logistic regression is family = "binomial".

Xp <- model.matrix(~ vibration_mm_s + bearing_temp_C + motor_current_pct + seal_flush_bar +
                     hours_since_service + pump_type + site, data = pumps)[, -1]
yp <- as.integer(pumps$failed_30d == "yes")
set.seed(7082)
cvp <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(Xp, yp, family = "binomial", type.measure = "auc")
print(max(cvp$cvm))
round(as.matrix(coef(cvp, s = "lambda.min")), 4)

A cross-validated AUC of 0.894, and at lambda.min the lasso keeps everything, including the site, with a coefficient of +1.42 for South. That’s Lesson 6’s calibration problem, found by the model itself: at the same vibration reading, a South pump is about e^{1.42} = 4 times as likely (in odds) to fail, because South’s sensors read low. [, -1] drops model.matrix()’s intercept column, since glmnet adds its own.

Your turn. Look at the coefficients at lambda.1se instead.

s = "lambda.1se".

Xp <- model.matrix(~ vibration_mm_s + bearing_temp_C + motor_current_pct + seal_flush_bar +
                     hours_since_service + pump_type + site, data = pumps)[, -1]
yp <- as.integer(pumps$failed_30d == "yes")
set.seed(7082)
cvp <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(Xp, yp, family = "binomial", type.measure = "auc")
b <- as.matrix(coef(cvp, s = "lambda.1se"))[, 1]
b[b != 0]

Only the vibration and the bearing temperature survive, with smaller coefficients than at lambda.min. The one-standard-error rule trades a slightly lower AUC for a model with two inputs, which is easier to explain to the reliability team, and to check. But it has also dropped the site, and with it the correction for South’s sensors. A sparser model isn’t automatically a better one: here, it’s simpler partly by ignoring a real problem.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Random folds choose differently. Cross-validate the soft-sensor lasso with ordinary random folds (nfolds = 10, after set.seed(7081)), and count the tags kept at lambda.min.

nfolds = 10.

set.seed(7081)
cv_r <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(Xs, ys, alpha = 1, nfolds = 10)
b <- coef(cv_r, s = "lambda.min")
rownames(b)[as.numeric(b) != 0]

Seven tags: the four chemistry tags, plus the bed level, the superficial gas velocity and the isopentane concentration. With random folds, every held-out sample has neighbors in the training data that share its catalyst drift, and a tag that happens to track the drift over a few weeks looks predictive. The blocked folds didn’t fall for it. Leakage doesn’t just make the error estimate optimistic; it changes which model you choose.

Challenge 2 · How stable is the selection? Draw 10 bootstrap resamples of the 80 calibration spectra, fit the cross-validated lasso to each, and count how often each wavelength is selected.

Bootstrap resamples are drawn with replacement.

sel <- sapply(1:10, function(k) {
  set.seed(7100 + k)
  rows <- sample(train, replace = TRUE)
  cv_b <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[rows, ], y[rows], alpha = 1, lambda = lam)
  as.numeric(coef(cv_b, s = "lambda.min"))[-1] != 0
})
freq <- rowSums(sel)
c(always = sum(freq == 10), sometimes = sum(freq > 0 & freq < 10), never = sum(freq == 0))

15 wavelengths are selected in all 10 resamples, the other 85 in some but not all, and none is never selected. Every wavelength gets picked by some resample, so a list of “the wavelengths the lasso chose” from one fit would mean very little. When predictors are this correlated, the lasso’s choice among them is close to arbitrary, and a small change in the data changes it. Stability selection (Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2010) builds on exactly this: keep only the predictors chosen in most resamples.

Quick quiz

1. What’s the only difference between the lasso and ridge?

That one change makes some coefficients exactly zero.

2. Why does the lasso give exact zeros, and ridge doesn’t?

A diamond has corners; a disk doesn’t. With orthonormal predictors, the lasso soft-thresholds.

3. The lasso selected the right four tags, but its CV error was worse than least squares on those tags. Why?

Refitting the selected model by least squares removes that bias.

4. On the spectra, which did best?

When the signal is spread over many correlated predictors, averaging beats selecting.

5. What does the elastic net’s ridge part add?

The lasso part still sets useless coefficients to zero.

6. Random folds let a noise tag into the soft sensor. Why?

Blocked folds, which respect time, chose exactly the chemistry tags.

Wrap-up

You can now select predictors and shrink them in one step. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The lasso penalizes \sum|\beta_j|, which sets some coefficients exactly to zero. The soft sensor’s four chemistry tags entered its path first.
  • Blocked CV chose exactly those four tags at lambda.min; lambda.1se dropped the comonomer too.
  • The lasso shrinks what it keeps: the comonomer’s coefficient was half its least-squares size. Refitting the selected tags by least squares removes the bias (Lesson 2’s blocked CV RMSE for them was 0.194, against the lasso’s 0.207).
  • Correlated predictors defeat the lasso: on spectra, it kept 41 wavelengths, unstably, with a test RMSE of 0.49, against ridge’s 0.416.
  • The elastic net (0 < \alpha < 1) blends the two, with the grouping effect.
  • Penalized logistic regression (family = "binomial") found the pump data’s site effect, with a CV AUC of 0.894.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Lasso
Least squares (or another likelihood) with a penalty \lambda\sum_j|\beta_j|, which shrinks the coefficients and sets some exactly to zero.
Sparsity
Having many coefficients exactly equal to zero.
Soft thresholding
Shrinking a value toward zero by a constant, and setting it to zero if it would cross zero.
Elastic net
A penalty that mixes the lasso and ridge penalties, controlled by \alpha.
Grouping effect
The tendency of the elastic net to give correlated predictors similar coefficients.
Relaxed lasso
Using the lasso to select the predictors, then refitting them with less (or no) shrinkage.
Coordinate descent
An optimization method that updates one parameter at a time, cycling through them, as glmnet does.
