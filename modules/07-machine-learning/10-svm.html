Support vector machines

The widest street between the classes

Map a distillation tray’s safe operating window from the operators’ logs: is it weeping, running normally, or flooding? Separate flooding from the rest with a support vector classifier, see how the cost parameter sets the margin and the number of support vectors, then bend the boundary with polynomial and radial kernels. Tune them by cross-validation, classify all three states at once, read the probabilities near the corner where the window closes, and watch a radial kernel overfit.

Module 7 · Lesson 10 of 14 Week 113 of 160 About 5–7 hours

A distillation tray works only inside a window of vapor and liquid flows. Push too much vapor up through it, and the liquid can’t drain down: the tray floods, separation collapses, and the column has to be backed off. Too little vapor, and liquid rains through the holes instead of flowing across: the tray weeps, and efficiency falls. Every tray design has an operating chart showing that window, but the real column rarely matches the vendor’s chart exactly.

The plant has 300 operating points from a revamped column, each with its liquid load (m³ of liquid per m² of tray area per hour) and its F-factor (the vapor velocity times the square root of its density, a measure of vapor load), labeled from the operators’ logs as weeping, normal or flooding:

ggplot(col, aes(liquid_load_m3_m2h, f_factor_Pa05, color = state)) +
  geom_point(size = 1.6) +
  scale_color_manual(values = scol, name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Liquid load (m³/m²h)", y = "F-factor (Pa^0.5)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) + theme(legend.position = "top")

The window is curved, and it closes at high liquid loads, where the flooding and weeping limits meet. This lesson maps it with support vector machines (SVMs), which come from computer science rather than statistics, and were among the best off-the-shelf classifiers in the 1990s and 2000s. They’re built on a geometric idea, the widest possible street between the classes, and a trick, kernels, that bends that street into any shape.

Every distillation student draws the tray’s operating window from correlations. Today the data draw it, and the SVM tells us exactly which operating points it based the boundary on.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • fit a support vector classifier with e1071::svm(), and see the support vectors that define it
  • see how the cost parameter trades margin width against violations
  • tune the cost by cross-validation with e1071::tune()
  • bend the boundary with polynomial and radial kernels
  • classify three classes at once, and get class probabilities
  • watch a radial kernel overfit when its \gamma is too large

The widest street

Start with two classes, flooding or not. If the two classes could be separated by a straight line, there would be infinitely many such lines. The maximal margin classifier chooses the one that’s as far as possible from the nearest points of each class: the middle of the widest street that separates them. Only the points on the edges of the street matter. They’re the support vectors, and moving any other point (without crossing the street) changes nothing.

Real classes overlap, so no street is perfectly clear. The support vector classifier allows some points inside the street, or even on the wrong side, at a price. In e1071::svm(), the price is the cost parameter C: a small cost tolerates many violations and gives a wide street, held up by many support vectors; a large cost tolerates few, and gives a narrow street. Cost plays the part that 1/\lambda played in ridge regression: small cost, more regularization.

The setup code reads the 300 points and makes two factors: state, with all three classes, and flood, flooding or not.

Your turn. Fit a support vector classifier for flooding with a linear kernel and a cost of 1. Find the number of support vectors and the training error rate.

kernel = "linear".

fit <- e1071::svm(flood ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "linear", cost = 1)
c(support_vectors = fit$tot.nSV, training_error = mean(predict(fit) != col$flood))

63 support vectors out of 300 points, and a training error of 7.3%. Only those 63 points determine the boundary; the other 237 could be deleted without changing it. svm() standardizes both predictors by default (scale = TRUE), which matters for the same reason as in kNN: the margin is a distance.

sapply(c(0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000), function(C) {

111, 63, 47, 42 and 42. A low cost makes violations cheap, so the street widens until it holds a third of the data, and every point inside it is a support vector: the boundary is an average over many points, stable but possibly biased. A high cost narrows the street to the few points that are hardest to separate: low bias, high variance. Past a cost of 100 nothing changes, because the street is already as narrow as the overlap allows.

NoteGo deeper: the optimization behind the street

Code the classes as y_i = \pm 1. The support vector classifier solves

\min_{\beta_0, \beta, \xi} \; \tfrac12 \|\beta\|^2 + C \sum_i \xi_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\beta_0 + x_i^\top\beta) \ge 1 - \xi_i, \;\; \xi_i \ge 0 .

The street’s half-width is 1/\|\beta\|, so minimizing \|\beta\| widens it; the slack variables \xi_i measure each point’s violation (\xi_i > 1 means it’s on the wrong side). It’s a quadratic program. Its solution has the form \hat\beta = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i, with \alpha_i > 0 only for the support vectors, and the decision function is f(x) = \beta_0 + \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \langle x, x_i\rangle: it depends on the data only through inner products \langle x, x_i\rangle. Replacing them with a kernel K(x, x_i) gives the SVM. Equivalently, the problem is \min \sum_i \max\big(0, 1 - y_i f(x_i)\big) + \lambda\|\beta\|^2: the hinge loss plus a ridge penalty. Logistic regression with a ridge penalty has almost the same form, with the log loss instead, which is why the two often give similar answers. ISLR section 9.5 makes the comparison.

Tuning the cost

The cost is a tuning parameter, chosen by cross-validation. e1071::tune() does it: give it the fitting function, the model, and a list of values to try, and it runs 10-fold CV on each.

Your turn. Tune the linear classifier’s cost over 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10 and 100.

The last cost to try is 100.

set.seed(7101)
tl <- e1071::tune(e1071::svm, flood ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "linear",
                  ranges = list(cost = c(0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100)))
tl$performances

A cost of 10 is best, with a CV error of 6.3%, but every cost is within 2 points of it, and the dispersion column (the SD of the error across folds) is about 5 points. The cost hardly matters here, because the real limitation is the straight line: the flood limit bends at high liquid loads, and no straight street can follow it.

Kernels: bending the street

Here is the trick that turns a support vector classifier into a support vector machine. The classifier depends on the data only through inner products \langle x, x_i \rangle, a measure of how similar two points are. Replace that with another similarity measure, a kernel K(x, x_i), and the boundary is still a straight line, but in a transformed space that may have many (even infinitely many) dimensions. Back in the original space, it’s curved. The two common kernels:

  • polynomial: K(x, x') = (\text{coef0} + \gamma\langle x, x'\rangle)^d, for boundaries that are polynomials of degree d;
  • radial (Gaussian): K(x, x') = \exp(-\gamma \|x - x'\|^2). Points close together are similar, and far apart, not at all. The boundary is then built from bumps around the support vectors, and \gamma sets how narrow they are: a large \gamma gives a wiggly, local boundary, like kNN with a small k.

Your turn. Tune a radial-kernel SVM for flooding over costs of 1, 10 and 100 and \gamma of 0.5, 1 and 2, and find the best combination and its CV error.

kernel = "radial".

set.seed(7102)
tr <- e1071::tune(e1071::svm, flood ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "radial",
                  ranges = list(cost = c(1, 10, 100), gamma = c(0.5, 1, 2)))
c(tr$best.parameters, error = tr$best.performance)

A cost of 10 and \gamma = 2, with a CV error of 2.0%, a third of the linear classifier’s 6.3%. The radial kernel follows the flood limit’s bend. With two predictors, you can see the gain on a plot; with twenty, you can’t, which is exactly when kernels are most useful.

Three classes

SVMs are built for two classes. For more, svm() uses the one-versus-one approach: it fits a classifier for every pair of classes (K(K-1)/2 of them, here 3), and each new point gets the class that wins the most pairwise votes.

Your turn. Tune a linear SVM for the three states over costs of 0.1, 1, 10 and 100.

The response with all three classes is state.

set.seed(7103)
tl3 <- e1071::tune(e1071::svm, state ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "linear",
                   ranges = list(cost = c(0.1, 1, 10, 100)))
tl3$best.performance

A CV error of 7.7%, at a cost of 100. Three straight boundaries can’t follow the window’s curved edges, especially at the right, where it closes.

A cost of 10 and \gamma = 0.1, with a CV error of 3.3%, against 7.7% for straight lines. Most of the 12 combinations are between 3.3% and 4.7%: the radial SVM isn’t fussy, as long as \gamma and the cost aren’t both large. A polynomial kernel of degree 2 (kernel = "polynomial", degree = 2, coef0 = 1) does about as well, 3.7%.

Probabilities near the edge

An SVM’s raw output is a class, or a signed distance from the boundary, not a probability. With probability = TRUE, svm() fits an extra model (Platt scaling: a logistic regression on the decision values, by internal cross-validation) that converts them into probabilities.

Your turn. Fit the tuned radial SVM with probability = TRUE, and find the class probabilities at four operating points: (10, 0.6), (30, 1.5), (50, 1.4) and (55, 0.9), as (liquid load, F-factor).

probability = TRUE, both when fitting and when predicting.

set.seed(7105)
fit <- e1071::svm(state ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "radial",
                  cost = 10, gamma = 0.1, probability = TRUE)
new <- data.frame(liquid_load_m3_m2h = c(10, 30, 50, 55), f_factor_Pa05 = c(0.6, 1.5, 1.4, 0.9))
p <- predict(fit, new, probability = TRUE)
round(attr(p, "probabilities")[, c("weeping", "normal", "flooding")], 3)

At (10, 0.6), low vapor at a low liquid load, it’s 80% weeping. At (30, 1.5), right in the middle of the window, it’s normal with near certainty. At (50, 1.4), 91% flooding: at high liquid loads, the flood limit has dropped below an F-factor of 1.4. And at (55, 0.9), the model is split: 57% flooding, 39% weeping, and only 4% normal. That’s the corner where the window closes: at that liquid load, the vapor rate that stops the weeping is already enough to flood the tray. The engineer’s conclusion is not about statistics: at liquid loads above about 55 m³/m²h, this tray has no safe operating point, and the column needs a higher-capacity tray or less liquid.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Too narrow bumps. Fit radial SVMs for the three states with a cost of 100 and \gamma of 1, 10, 50 and 200. For each, find the training error, the number of support vectors, and the CV error (with tune() and a single value of each parameter).

Use the function’s argument: gamma = g.

t(sapply(c(1, 10, 50, 200), function(g) {
  fit <- e1071::svm(state ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "radial", cost = 100, gamma = g)
  set.seed(7106)
  cv <- e1071::tune(e1071::svm, state ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05, data = col, kernel = "radial",
                    ranges = list(cost = 100, gamma = g))
  c(gamma = g, train_error = mean(predict(fit) != col$state), n_sv = fit$tot.nSV, cv_error = cv$best.performance)
}))

From \gamma = 10 on, the training error is 0: every point is classified correctly, mislabeled ones included. But the CV error climbs from 4.3% to 7.7% to 19.7%, and at \gamma = 200, 293 of the 300 points are support vectors: the model is a bump around each point, a lookup table that has memorized the data. It’s Lesson 1’s overfitting, and Lesson 3’s k = 1, in kernel form.

Challenge 2 · A curved logistic regression. SVMs aren’t the only way to curve a boundary. Cross-validate a multinomial logistic regression with squared terms and the interaction, on 10 random folds.

Predict the held-out fold, col[fold == j, ].

set.seed(7107)
fold <- sample(rep(1:10, length.out = 300))
pred <- factor(rep(NA, 300), levels = levels(col$state))
fq <- state ~ liquid_load_m3_m2h + f_factor_Pa05 + I(liquid_load_m3_m2h^2) + I(f_factor_Pa05^2) + liquid_load_m3_m2h:f_factor_Pa05
for (j in 1:10) {
  fit <- nnet::multinom(fq, data = col[fold != j, ], trace = FALSE, maxit = 1000)
  pred[fold == j] <- predict(fit, col[fold == j, ])
}
mean(pred != col$state)

4.0%, close to the radial SVM’s 3.3% (on different folds, so the gap is within noise). With a few predictors and a smooth boundary, a logistic regression with the right terms does nearly as well as an SVM, and it gives calibrated probabilities and readable coefficients. SVMs earn their keep when there are many predictors and nobody knows which terms to add.

Quick quiz

1. What are the support vectors?

Moving any other point, without crossing the margin, doesn’t change the classifier.

2. What happens as the cost C increases?

Violations get more expensive. Low cost is more regularization, like a large \lambda.

3. What does a kernel do?

The boundary is linear in a transformed space that’s never computed explicitly.

4. What does a large \gamma do in a radial kernel?

At \gamma = 200, 293 of 300 points were support vectors, and the CV error was 20%.

5. How does svm() handle three classes?

With K classes, K(K-1)/2 classifiers.

6. Where do an SVM’s probabilities come from?

That’s what probability = TRUE adds.

Wrap-up

You can now fit, tune and read support vector machines. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The support vector classifier finds the widest street between the classes, allowing violations at a price C. Only the support vectors (63 of 300 at C = 1) define it.
  • The cost sets the trade-off: low cost, a wide street and many support vectors; high cost, a narrow one. tune() chooses it by CV.
  • Kernels curve the boundary. For flooding, a radial kernel cut the CV error from 6.3% to 2.0%.
  • Three classes are handled one-versus-one: 7.7% wrong with straight boundaries, 3.3% with a radial kernel.
  • Probabilities come from Platt scaling. Near the corner where the window closes, the model was split between weeping and flooding.
  • Large \gamma overfits: zero training error, and a CV error that rose to 20%.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Maximal margin classifier
The linear boundary that separates two classes with the widest possible margin.
Margin
The distance from the boundary to the nearest training points.
Support vector classifier
A maximal margin classifier that allows violations of the margin, at a cost C.
Support vectors
The training points on or inside the margin, or on the wrong side; they alone determine the classifier.
Kernel
A function K(x, x') that measures similarity, replacing the inner product to give curved boundaries.
Radial (Gaussian) kernel
K(x, x') = \exp(-\gamma\|x - x'\|^2).
Support vector machine (SVM)
A support vector classifier with a kernel.
One-versus-one
Handling K classes by fitting a classifier to every pair and taking a vote.
Hinge loss
\max(0, 1 - y f(x)), the loss that the support vector classifier minimizes, plus a penalty.
