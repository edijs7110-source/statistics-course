Ridge regression

When there are more predictors than samples

Calibrate a near-infrared analyzer: predict a solvent’s ethanol content from its absorbance at 100 wavelengths, with only 80 calibration samples. Watch least squares fit the training data perfectly and fail on new samples, then tame it by shrinking the coefficients. Write ridge regression by hand, count its effective degrees of freedom, choose its penalty by cross-validation with glmnet, and see what happens when the penalty grid is too short.

Module 7 · Lesson 7 of 14 Week 110 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The solvent plant blends ethanol, water and glycerol, and the blend’s ethanol content has to be right. The lab’s gas chromatograph takes 40 minutes per sample. A near-infrared (NIR) analyzer on the line takes a spectrum every few seconds: the light absorbed at each of 100 wavelengths from 1,100 to 2,486 nm. Ethanol absorbs at some wavelengths, water and glycerol at others, and their bands overlap. The analyzer needs a calibration: a model that turns 100 absorbances into an ethanol percentage.

nir_spectra.csv has 120 calibration samples, each with its spectrum and its ethanol content from the GC. Here are 20 of them:

sp <- data.frame(wl = rep(wl, each = 20), A = as.vector(X[1:20, ]), ethanol = rep(y[1:20], 100), id = rep(1:20, 100))
ggplot(sp, aes(wl, A, group = id, color = ethanol)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.4) +
  scale_color_gradient(low = "#F4C0D1", high = "#3A1F35", name = "Ethanol (%)") +
  labs(x = "Wavelength (nm)", y = "Absorbance") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The differences that carry the ethanol information are small, they’re spread across many wavelengths, and they’re mixed with the other components’ bands and a random offset for each sample. This is the classic setting for regularization: many correlated predictors, few samples. Chemometricians have calibrated spectrometers this way for decades, and this lesson and the next two use their problem.

An NIR analyzer is a soft sensor with 100 inputs! Least squares can’t handle that on its own, and this lesson shows the fix that every spectroscopist uses.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • see least squares fail when predictors outnumber samples
  • write ridge regression by hand, from its closed-form solution
  • trace how the penalty \lambda trades bias for variance, and count the effective degrees of freedom
  • fit and cross-validate ridge with glmnet, and choose \lambda with the minimum and the one-standard-error rules
  • see what goes wrong when the \lambda grid doesn’t reach the best value

Least squares with 100 predictors

The setup code reads the spectra into a 120 \times 100 matrix X, with the ethanol in y, and picks 80 samples at random for calibration (train); the other 40 are for testing. It also defines a grid of \lambda values, lam, which you’ll need later.

Your turn. Find the correlations between neighboring wavelengths: wavelength 1 with 2, 2 with 3, and so on.

The neighbor of wavelength i is wavelength i + 1: 2:100.

r <- cor(X)
range(r[cbind(1:99, 2:100)])

From 0.90 to 0.9987. Neighboring wavelengths carry almost the same information, and so, to a lesser degree, do wavelengths far apart, since all of them move with the sample’s offset. r[cbind(i, j)] picks the elements (i_1, j_1), (i_2, j_2), \ldots of a matrix, here the entries just above the diagonal.

With 80 calibration samples and 101 coefficients (an intercept and 100 slopes), least squares has more unknowns than equations. There are infinitely many coefficient vectors that fit the 80 samples exactly.

Your turn. Fit least squares to the 80 calibration samples. Find the fit’s rank, the number of coefficients lm() sets to NA, the training RMSE and the test RMSE (with the NA coefficients set to 0).

The test samples are y[-train].

ols <- lm(y[train] ~ X[train, ])
b <- coef(ols)
b[is.na(b)] <- 0
pred <- cbind(1, X[-train, ]) %*% b
c(rank = ols$rank, n_na = sum(is.na(coef(ols))),
  train_rmse = sqrt(mean(resid(ols)^2)), test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - pred)^2)))

A rank of 80, so 21 coefficients are NA. The training RMSE is 0, a perfect fit, and the test RMSE is 9.9 percentage points, when the ethanol content itself only ranges from 5% to 30% (its SD is about 7). This is Lesson 1’s overfitting at its most extreme: with as many parameters as samples, the model can fit anything, noise included. Even with only every fifth wavelength (20 predictors), the test RMSE is 1.13, and the coefficients reach \pm 85, enormous positive and negative weights on neighboring wavelengths that nearly cancel.

Ridge regression

Ridge regression keeps all the predictors but stops the coefficients from growing large. It minimizes the residual sum of squares plus a penalty on the size of the coefficients:

\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_\beta \; \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^p x_{ij}\beta_j\Big)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j^2 .

The tuning parameter \lambda \ge 0 sets the trade-off. At \lambda = 0, it’s least squares. As \lambda grows, the coefficients shrink toward zero, and at \lambda = \infty, they’re all zero and the model predicts the mean. The intercept isn’t penalized.

The penalty treats every \beta_j alike, so the predictors should be on comparable scales: the usual practice is to standardize them first. With standardized predictors Z and a centered response, ridge has a closed-form solution, just like least squares:

\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} = (Z^\top Z + \lambda I)^{-1} Z^\top y .

Adding \lambda to the diagonal makes the matrix invertible however many predictors there are. That’s where the name comes from: a “ridge” added along the diagonal.

Your turn. Standardize the calibration spectra, compute the ridge coefficients for \lambda = 1 by hand, and find the test RMSE. Standardize the test spectra with the calibration means and SDs.

The identity matrix is 100 \times 100: diag(100).

mu <- colMeans(X[train, ])
s <- apply(X[train, ], 2, sd)
Z <- scale(X[train, ], center = mu, scale = s)
yc <- y[train] - mean(y[train])
beta <- solve(crossprod(Z) + 1 * diag(100), crossprod(Z, yc))
pred <- mean(y[train]) + scale(X[-train, ], center = mu, scale = s) %*% beta
sqrt(mean((y[-train] - pred)^2))

A test RMSE of 0.41 percentage points: 24 times better than least squares, from one extra term. crossprod(Z) is Z^\top Z, and crossprod(Z, yc) is Z^\top y. The intercept is the mean of the calibration y, because the predictors are centered.

The penalty’s dial

Your turn. Repeat the calculation for \lambda = 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10 and 100, and find the test RMSE for each.

The penalty is the function’s argument, l.

mu <- colMeans(X[train, ])
s <- apply(X[train, ], 2, sd)
Z <- scale(X[train, ], center = mu, scale = s)
yc <- y[train] - mean(y[train])
Ztest <- scale(X[-train, ], center = mu, scale = s)
sapply(c(0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100), function(l) {
  beta <- solve(crossprod(Z) + l * diag(100), crossprod(Z, yc))
  sqrt(mean((y[-train] - mean(y[train]) - Ztest %*% beta)^2))
})

0.68, 0.51, 0.41, 0.72 and 2.34. Lesson 1’s U-shape again, with \lambda as the dial: too little penalty lets the coefficients chase the noise (high variance), too much shrinks away the real signal (high bias). \lambda = 1 is the best of these five. But choosing \lambda by the test set uses up the test set, so in practice it’s chosen by cross-validation, below.

How flexible is a ridge fit? Least squares with p predictors uses p degrees of freedom. Ridge uses fewer, because the penalty restrains them. Its effective degrees of freedom come from the singular values d_j of Z:

\text{df}(\lambda) = \sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda} .

Each direction in the data counts as one degree of freedom if its d_j^2 is much larger than \lambda, and as almost nothing if it’s much smaller.

Your turn. Compute the effective degrees of freedom at \lambda = 1.

Use \lambda = 1.

mu <- colMeans(X[train, ])
s <- apply(X[train, ], 2, sd)
Z <- scale(X[train, ], center = mu, scale = s)
d <- svd(Z)$d
sum(d^2 / (d^2 + 1))

34 effective degrees of freedom, from 100 predictors. At \lambda = 0.01, 10 and 100, it’s 75, 11 and 4. The first three squared singular values are 5,328, 2,016 and 389, and they fall below 1 after a few dozen: most of the 100 directions carry almost no variation, and ridge spends almost nothing on them. Lesson 12 looks at these directions directly: they’re the principal components.

NoteGo deeper: ridge as shrinkage along principal directions

Write the singular value decomposition Z = UDV^\top. Least squares’ fitted values are U U^\top y = \sum_j u_j (u_j^\top y). Ridge’s are

Z\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} = \sum_j u_j \, \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda} \, (u_j^\top y) .

So ridge takes least squares’ fit along each principal direction u_j and shrinks it by the factor d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda). Directions with large d_j (lots of spread in the predictors) are barely shrunk; directions with small d_j, where the data give little information and the coefficients are the most variable, are shrunk hard. Challenge 2 computes these factors. There’s also a Bayesian view, from Module 5: ridge’s \hat\beta is the posterior mode (and mean) when each \beta_j has a N(0, \sigma^2/\lambda) prior. A bigger \lambda is a tighter prior around zero. The Elements of Statistical Learning, section 3.4.1, has both derivations.

Ridge with glmnet

The glmnet package fits ridge (and the lasso, next lesson) efficiently for a whole sequence of \lambda values at once, and cross-validates them. glmnet(x, y, alpha = 0) is ridge. It standardizes the predictors internally and reports the coefficients on the original scale. Its \lambda is scaled differently from the formula above (it divides the sum of squares by 2n), so don’t compare its \lambda values with yours.

cv.glmnet() runs k-fold CV (10 folds by default) over the \lambda sequence, and reports two choices: lambda.min, with the smallest CV error, and lambda.1se, the largest \lambda whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum.

Your turn. Run cv.glmnet() for ridge on the calibration samples, with glmnet’s default \lambda sequence. Compare lambda.min with the smallest \lambda in the sequence, and find the test RMSE.

Ridge is alpha = 0.

set.seed(7053)
cv0 <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0)
c(lambda_min = cv0$lambda.min, smallest_in_grid = min(cv0$lambda),
  test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - predict(cv0, X[-train, ], s = "lambda.min"))^2)))

The best \lambda is 62.1, which is the smallest value in the grid, and the test RMSE is 3.45, eight times worse than your hand-made ridge. When the best value of a tuning parameter is at the edge of the range searched, the search was too narrow: the true optimum is somewhere beyond it. glmnet’s default sequence stops at 1% of its largest \lambda when there are more predictors than samples, which leaves far too much shrinkage for spectra. Always check.

Your turn. Cross-validate again with the longer grid lam, from 100 down to 10^{-6}, and find lambda.min and its test RMSE.

Pass the grid: lambda = lam.

set.seed(7053)
cv <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0, lambda = lam)
c(lambda_min = cv$lambda.min,
  test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - predict(cv, X[-train, ], s = "lambda.min"))^2)))

lambda.min is 0.059, well inside the grid, and the test RMSE is 0.416, essentially the same as the best hand-made ridge (0.41). CV found it without touching the test set. plot(cv) draws the CV error against \log\lambda, with error bars: a U-shape with its bottom near \log\lambda = -3.

As \lambda falls, the coefficients grow from zero, each along its own path:

fit <- glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0, lambda = lam)
B <- as.matrix(fit$beta)
paths <- data.frame(loglam = rep(log(fit$lambda), each = 100), beta = as.vector(B), wl = rep(1:100, length(fit$lambda)))
set.seed(7053); cvf <- cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0, lambda = lam)
ggplot(paths, aes(loglam, beta, group = wl)) +
  geom_line(color = "grey55", linewidth = 0.25) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = log(cvf$lambda.min), linetype = "dashed", color = "#C2416C") +
  labs(x = "log(λ)", y = "Coefficient") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

The one-standard-error rule

The CV error curve is itself an estimate, with noise. Often many values of \lambda are statistically indistinguishable. The one-standard-error rule picks the simplest model (here, the most shrunk) whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum: it gives up a little estimated accuracy for a model that’s less likely to have been chosen by luck.

Your turn. Find lambda.1se, and its test RMSE.

Ask for s = "lambda.1se".

set.seed(7053)
cv <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0, lambda = lam)
c(lambda_1se = cv$lambda.1se,
  test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - predict(cv, X[-train, ], s = "lambda.1se"))^2)))

\lambda = 0.148, two and a half times more shrinkage, and a test RMSE of 0.417, no worse than lambda.min’s 0.416. Here the two rules agree on the test set; in general, lambda.1se gives a slightly simpler, more conservative model. Which to use is a judgment call, and many practitioners default to lambda.1se.

Your turn. Compare the range of the ridge coefficients at lambda.min with the range of least squares’ coefficients.

Least squares has NA coefficients: na.rm = TRUE.

set.seed(7053)
cv <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0, lambda = lam)
b_ridge <- as.numeric(coef(cv, s = "lambda.min"))[-1]
b_ols <- coef(lm(y[train] ~ X[train, ]))[-1]
rbind(ridge = range(b_ridge), ols = range(b_ols, na.rm = TRUE))

Ridge’s coefficients run from -18 to 23; least squares’ from -546 to 495. Those huge least-squares coefficients are positive and negative weights on neighboring wavelengths, nearly cancelling, and they amplify the noise in each new spectrum. The penalty forbids such delicate balancing acts.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · To standardize or not? glmnet standardizes by default. Spectra are all absorbances, in the same units, and chemometricians often don’t standardize them. Cross-validate ridge with standardize = FALSE, and compare the test RMSE.

standardize = FALSE.

set.seed(7053)
cv_raw <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X[train, ], y[train], alpha = 0, lambda = lam, standardize = FALSE)
c(lambda_min = cv_raw$lambda.min,
  test_rmse = sqrt(mean((y[-train] - predict(cv_raw, X[-train, ], s = "lambda.min"))^2)))

A test RMSE of 0.400, slightly better than 0.416, with a much smaller \lambda (0.00018), because unstandardized absorbances have small SDs (0.04 to 0.14). Standardizing gives every wavelength an equal say, including the ones that barely vary and carry mostly noise. Without it, the strongly varying wavelengths, which carry the chemistry, dominate. Like kNN’s distances in Lesson 3, the penalty depends on the scales, and standardizing is a choice to make, not a ritual. When predictors are in different units (temperatures and pressures), standardize; when they’re in the same units and their spread means something, think twice.

Challenge 2 · Shrinkage factors. Compute ridge’s shrinkage factor d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda) along the first 8 principal directions of the standardized calibration spectra, for \lambda = 1.

\lambda = 1.

mu <- colMeans(X[train, ])
s <- apply(X[train, ], 2, sd)
d <- svd(scale(X[train, ], center = mu, scale = s))$d
round(d[1:8]^2 / (d[1:8]^2 + 1), 4)

0.9998, 0.9995, 0.9974, 0.98, 0.98, then 0.80, 0.78 and 0.77. The first five directions are kept almost intact, and from the sixth on, everything is shrunk by about 20% or more. The data have five strong directions: the three components (ethanol, water and glycerol), the offset and the slope of each sample’s baseline. Everything beyond is mostly noise. That’s the structure Lesson 12’s principal components regression uses directly.

Quick quiz

1. What happens to least squares with more predictors than samples?

Infinitely many coefficient vectors fit exactly. lm() picks one by setting some to NA.

2. What does ridge regression add to least squares?

The lasso, next lesson, penalizes \sum |\beta_j| instead.

3. What happens as \lambda grows?

Ridge shrinks all the coefficients, but none becomes exactly zero.

4. lambda.min equals the smallest value in the \lambda grid. What should you do?

glmnet’s default grid stopped too early for the spectra, and the test error was 8 times too high.

5. What does the one-standard-error rule choose?

It prefers a simpler (more shrunk) model that’s statistically as good.

6. Ridge with \lambda = 1 had 34 effective degrees of freedom from 100 predictors. What does that mean?

All 100 coefficients are nonzero, but the penalty restrains them.

Wrap-up

You can now fit a model with more predictors than samples. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Least squares fails with p \ge n: 80 samples and 100 wavelengths gave a perfect fit and a test RMSE of 9.9.
  • Ridge regression adds \lambda\sum\beta_j^2, with the closed form (Z^\top Z + \lambda I)^{-1}Z^\top y. At \lambda = 1, by hand, the test RMSE was 0.41.
  • \lambda is the dial: too small overfits, too large underfits. The effective degrees of freedom, 34 at \lambda = 1, measure the flexibility.
  • glmnet (alpha = 0) fits the whole path and cross-validates it. Its default grid stopped too early here, at a test RMSE of 3.45; a longer grid gave 0.416.
  • The one-standard-error rule picks a more shrunk model that’s statistically as good.
  • Standardizing is a choice: for spectra in one unit, not standardizing did slightly better.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Regularization
Adding a penalty to a model’s fitting criterion to restrain its flexibility.
Ridge regression
Least squares with a penalty \lambda\sum_j\beta_j^2, which shrinks all the coefficients toward zero.
Shrinkage
Pulling estimates toward zero (or another value), trading a little bias for less variance.
Tuning parameter
A setting that controls a method’s flexibility and isn’t estimated by the fit itself, like \lambda or k.
Effective degrees of freedom
A measure of a regularized fit’s flexibility, \sum_j d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda) for ridge.
One-standard-error rule
Choosing the simplest model whose CV error is within one standard error of the best.
Calibration (chemometrics)
A model that converts an instrument’s signal, such as a spectrum, into a concentration.
