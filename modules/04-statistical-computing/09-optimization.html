Multivariate optimization and maximum likelihood

Downhill in several dimensions, for pumps that are still running

Maximize likelihoods with several parameters: fit a Weibull model to pump lifetimes with right-censored data using optim(), with Nelder-Mead and BFGS, on a log scale that keeps the parameters positive. Get standard errors from the Hessian, compare gradient descent with Newton’s method (Fisher scoring) for logistic regression, and see what ignoring the censored pumps does to the B10 life.

Lesson 8’s seals were run to failure on a test rig. The plant’s own pumps tell a messier story. The maintenance team followed the 36 pumps installed between 2019 and 2022 until 31 October 2026, and recorded the days from installation to each pump’s first seal failure:

head(pumps)

#>   pump_id installed   days failed
#>   <chr>   <date>     <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 P-101   2019-01-01  2860      0
#> 2 P-102   2019-01-10  1685      1
#> 3 P-103   2019-01-16  2397      1
#> 4 P-104   2019-03-01  1185      1

27 pumps had a seal failure. The other 9 are still running: for them, days is how long they’ve lasted so far. Their true lifetimes are longer, but we don’t know by how much. Such observations are right-censored. They can’t be thrown away, because they’re the toughest pumps, and dropping them would make the seals look worse than they are. And they can’t be treated as failures either.

Maximum likelihood handles censoring naturally, but it leaves you with two parameters to estimate at once, and no score equation to solve by hand. This lesson is about optimization in several dimensions: the algorithms inside R’s optim(), and inside glm().

Nine pumps are still happily spinning. They haven’t told us their secrets yet, but they’re not keeping quiet either!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • write a likelihood for censored data
  • maximize it with optim(), using Nelder-Mead and BFGS
  • transform parameters so that the optimizer can’t break them
  • get standard errors and confidence intervals from the Hessian
  • compare gradient descent with Newton’s method, on a logistic regression

A likelihood for censored data

A pump that failed at x_i days contributes the probability density of failing then, f(x_i), as in Lesson 8. A pump still running at x_i days contributes the probability of lasting at least that long, the survival function S(x_i) = P(T > x_i). For the Weibull distribution, S(x) = \exp[-(x/\lambda)^k]. With \delta_i = 1 for a failure and 0 for a censored pump, the log-likelihood is

\ell(k, \lambda) = \sum_i \delta_i \left[\log k - \log\lambda + (k - 1)\log\frac{x_i}{\lambda}\right] - \sum_i \left(\frac{x_i}{\lambda}\right)^k ,

because \log f = \log k - \log\lambda + (k-1)\log(x/\lambda) - (x/\lambda)^k and \log S = -(x/\lambda)^k: every pump contributes the last term, and only the failures contribute the first.

Optimizers conventionally minimize, so we’ll work with the negative log-likelihood. And we’ll give the optimizer \theta = (\log k, \log\lambda) instead of (k, \lambda). Then any value it tries corresponds to a positive shape and scale, and the two parameters are on similar scales (about 0.7 and 7.5, instead of 2 and 1,800).

Your turn. Complete the negative log-likelihood, and minimize it with optim(), starting from \theta = (0, 7): a shape of 1 and a scale of e^7 \approx 1100 days. Without a method, optim() uses Nelder-Mead.

The last term is \sum (x_i/\lambda)^k: the exponent is k.

nll <- function(theta) {
  k <- exp(theta[1]); lambda <- exp(theta[2])
  -(sum(failed * (log(k) - log(lambda) + (k - 1) * log(days / lambda))) - sum((days / lambda)^k))
}
fit_nm <- optim(c(0, 7), nll)
c(shape = exp(fit_nm$par[1]), scale = exp(fit_nm$par[2]), evaluations = fit_nm$counts[["function"]])

A shape of 1.94 and a scale of 1,758 days, after 101 evaluations of the likelihood. The shape is above 1, so the seals wear out, as on the test rig. The scale is the age by which 63% of seals have failed, about 4.8 years. Always check fit_nm$convergence too: 0 means optim() thinks it converged.

Inside optim(): Nelder-Mead and BFGS

Nelder-Mead uses function values only. In two dimensions, it keeps a triangle of three points, called a simplex. At each step, it replaces the worst point by reflecting it through the other two, stretching the triangle when that goes well, and shrinking it when it doesn’t. The triangle tumbles downhill and contracts around the minimum. It’s robust and needs no derivatives, but it’s slow, and in many dimensions it can stall. Here are the points it tried, on the contours of the negative log-likelihood:

tried <- matrix(numeric(0), ncol = 2)
nll_logged <- function(theta) { tried <<- rbind(tried, theta); nll(theta) }
invisible(optim(c(0, 7), nll_logged))
grid <- expand.grid(logk = seq(-0.2, 1.2, length.out = 120), loglam = seq(6.8, 8.0, length.out = 120))
grid$nll <- apply(grid, 1, function(th) nll(th))
path <- data.frame(logk = tried[, 1], loglam = tried[, 2], step = seq_len(nrow(tried)))
ggplot(grid, aes(x = logk, y = loglam)) +
  geom_contour(aes(z = nll), breaks = 223.53 + c(0.5, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80), color = "grey70") +
  geom_path(data = path, color = "#C2416C", linewidth = 0.5, alpha = 0.7) +
  geom_point(data = path, color = "#C2416C", size = 1.3) +
  annotate("point", x = 0, y = 7, color = "#3A1F35", size = 3) +
  annotate("text", x = 0.03, y = 6.93, label = "start", hjust = 0, size = 3.6, color = "#3A1F35") +
  labs(x = "log shape", y = "log scale (log days)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

BFGS (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb and Shanno, who found it independently in 1970) is a quasi-Newton method. It uses the gradient, the vector of partial derivatives, which optim() approximates by finite differences unless you supply it. And it builds up an estimate of the curvature as it goes, which lets it take Newton-like steps without ever computing second derivatives. With hessian = TRUE, optim() also returns the matrix of second derivatives at the answer, which you’ll need for standard errors.

Your turn. Fit the model with BFGS, and compare its answer and its work with Nelder-Mead’s.

method = "BFGS".

fit_nm <- optim(c(0, 7), nll)
fit_bfgs <- optim(c(0, 7), nll, method = "BFGS", hessian = TRUE)
rbind(nelder_mead = c(exp(fit_nm$par), fit_nm$counts),
      bfgs = c(exp(fit_bfgs$par), fit_bfgs$counts))

The same answer, a shape of 1.94 and a scale of 1,758 days, from 35 function and 9 gradient evaluations, against Nelder-Mead’s 101 (it uses no gradients, so that column is NA). Each finite-difference gradient costs a few function evaluations, but BFGS still needs far fewer steps. With more parameters, the gap grows. survival::survreg(), the standard function for censored lifetimes, gives the same estimates.

The log scale isn’t just tidy. Here’s BFGS on the original scale, starting from a shape of 1 and a scale of 1,000 days:

nll_raw <- function(th) {
  k <- th[1]; lambda <- th[2]
  if (k <= 0 || lambda <= 0) return(Inf)
  -(sum(failed * (log(k) - log(lambda) + (k - 1) * log(days / lambda))) - sum((days / lambda)^k))
}
raw <- optim(c(1, 1000), nll_raw, method = "BFGS")
c(raw$par, value = raw$value, convergence = raw$convergence)
#>                               value convergence 
#>    1.214661 1052.372917  234.141146    1.000000

It stopped at a shape of 1.21 and a scale of 1,052, far from the answer, with a worse negative log-likelihood (234.1 against 223.5). Its convergence code, 1, says it ran out of iterations. A change of 1 in the scale barely moves the likelihood, while a change of 1 in the shape is enormous, so the gradient points almost entirely along the shape, and progress along the scale is painfully slow. On the log scale, both parameters move the likelihood about equally. Rescale and transform your parameters, and always check convergence.

Standard errors from the Hessian

Near its maximum, a log-likelihood is shaped like an upside-down bowl. A sharply curved bowl pins the estimate down, and a flat one doesn’t. The curvature is measured by the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives of the negative log-likelihood. At the maximum, it’s called the observed information, and its inverse estimates the covariance matrix of the estimates:

\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\theta) \approx H^{-1}.

The square roots of its diagonal are the standard errors. They’re for \log k and \log\lambda, so build the 95% intervals on the log scale and take the exponential of the ends.

Your turn. The setup code has fitted fit_bfgs with hessian = TRUE. Compute 95% confidence intervals for the shape and the scale.

Invert the Hessian: fit_bfgs$hessian.

V <- solve(fit_bfgs$hessian)         # the estimated covariance matrix of the log parameters
se <- sqrt(diag(V))
est <- fit_bfgs$par
rbind(estimate = exp(est), lower = exp(est - 1.96 * se), upper = exp(est + 1.96 * se))

The shape is between 1.42 and 2.65, and the scale between 1,447 and 2,135 days. The shape’s interval is entirely above 1, so the wear-out is clear even with 36 pumps. The intervals are asymmetric on the original scale, which is right for positive quantities. And on the log scale, the two estimates are almost uncorrelated (a correlation of 0.02), another sign that it’s a good parameterization.

Gradient descent

The simplest optimizer of all steps downhill: compute the gradient \nabla f(\theta), and move a little way against it,

\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, \nabla f(\theta),

with a step size (or learning rate) \alpha. It’s how most neural networks are trained (Module 7). It needs no second derivatives, but it’s slow when the bowl is long and narrow, and the step size needs tuning: too small and it crawls, too large and it overshoots and diverges.

Try it on a logistic regression (Lesson 2.15). The plant pressure-tested 80 gaskets at different pressures and temperatures, and 23 of them leaked. The model is \operatorname{logit} P(\text{leak}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{pressure} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{temperature}, and its negative log-likelihood has the gradient

\nabla = -X^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{p}),

where X is the model matrix and \mathbf{p} the fitted leak probabilities. The setup code has standardized both predictors (mean 0, SD 1), which makes the bowl rounder and gradient descent much happier.

Your turn. Run 500 steps of gradient descent with a step size of 0.01.

The step size is 0.01.

beta <- c(0, 0, 0)
for (i in 1:500) {
  p <- plogis(X %*% beta)                      # fitted probabilities
  gradient <- -t(X) %*% (y - p)                # gradient of the negative log-likelihood
  beta <- beta - 0.01 * gradient               # a small step downhill
}
drop(beta)

After 500 steps, \boldsymbol\beta = (-2.0065, 2.6016, 0.6204), correct to about 3 digits: glm() gives (-2.0068, 2.6019, 0.6205). It took 2,000 steps to match all the printed digits. Pressure matters most: one SD more pressure (8.8 bar) multiplies the odds of a leak by e^{2.6} \approx 13.

Newton’s method and Fisher scoring

Newton’s method in several dimensions uses the curvature as well as the slope. It replaces the function by the quadratic bowl that matches its gradient and Hessian, and jumps straight to that bowl’s bottom:

\theta \leftarrow \theta - H^{-1} \nabla f(\theta).

For logistic regression, the Hessian is X^\top W X, with W the diagonal matrix of p_i(1 - p_i). So the Newton step solves

(X^\top W X)\, \Delta = X^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{p}).

Fisher scoring uses the expected information instead of the observed Hessian. For logistic regression the two are the same, and so is a third name for the algorithm, iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), because each step is a weighted least squares fit. It’s exactly what glm() does, and it’s what the “Number of Fisher Scoring iterations” in summary() of a glm() counts.

Your turn. Run Newton’s method from \boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{0} for 8 steps, and watch the digits settle.

The right-hand side is X^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{p}).

beta <- c(0, 0, 0)
for (i in 1:8) {
  p <- drop(plogis(X %*% beta))
  W <- p * (1 - p)                                          # the weights
  beta <- beta + solve(t(X) %*% (W * X), t(X) %*% (y - p))
  cat(i, format(drop(beta), digits = 10), "\n")
}
drop(beta)

Six steps reach 8 digits, and the seventh changes nothing that a double can show: quadratic convergence again. Gradient descent needed 2,000 steps for the same answer. glm(leak ~ scale(pressure_bar) + scale(temp_C), family = binomial, data = leaks) reports “Number of Fisher Scoring iterations: 6”. (W * X multiplies each row of X by its weight, which is WX without building the diagonal matrix.)

NoteGo deeper: which optimizer when?
  • Newton’s method is the fastest near the answer, but it needs second derivatives, and each step solves a linear system with one row per parameter, which is expensive for thousands of parameters. Far from the answer, it can jump the wrong way, as in Lesson 8, so good implementations add a line search that shortens a step that doesn’t improve the function.
  • Quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, and L-BFGS-B with bounds on the parameters) are the general-purpose default: fast, and they need only gradients.
  • Nelder-Mead is for small problems where the function isn’t smooth, or the gradient is unreliable.
  • Gradient descent, and its random-subsample version, stochastic gradient descent, wins for huge problems such as neural networks, where even storing a Hessian is impossible.

All of them find a local minimum. If a likelihood has several, try several starting points.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Throwing away the survivors. What if the analyst had simply dropped the 9 pumps that are still running, and fitted a Weibull distribution to the 27 failures alone?

Keep the pumps that failed: failed == 1.

fail_days <- days[failed == 1]
nll_fail <- function(theta) -sum(dweibull(fail_days, exp(theta[1]), exp(theta[2]), log = TRUE))
fit_fail <- optim(c(0, 7), nll_fail, method = "BFGS")
c(shape = exp(fit_fail$par[1]), scale = exp(fit_fail$par[2]))

A scale of 1,429 days instead of 1,758: the seals look 19% shorter-lived than they are, because the 9 longest-lived pumps were thrown out. The shape barely changes (2.03 against 1.94). Censored observations carry real information: “this pump lasted at least 2,860 days” is a lot of evidence for long lives.

Challenge 2 · The B10 life. Maintenance planners use the B10 life: the age by which 10% of seals have failed, t_{10} = \lambda\,(-\log 0.9)^{1/k}. To get a confidence interval, simulate 5,000 parameter vectors from the estimates’ approximate normal distribution, N(\hat\theta, V), and compute the B10 life for each. (Multiplying independent normals by chol(V) gives them the covariance V.)

Apply the function b10 to each row.

V <- solve(fit_bfgs$hessian)
est <- fit_bfgs$par
b10 <- function(theta) exp(theta[2]) * (-log(0.9))^(1 / exp(theta[1]))
set.seed(4091)
draws <- sweep(matrix(rnorm(10000), ncol = 2) %*% chol(V), 2, est, "+")   # 5,000 rows of (log k, log lambda)
b10_draws <- apply(draws, 1, b10)
c(b10 = b10(est), quantile(b10_draws, c(0.025, 0.975)))

A B10 life of 551 days, with a 95% interval from 344 to 804 days. So inspecting the seals at about a year (365 days) would be a cautious plan. The model that dropped the survivors would have said 471 days. Simulating from the estimates’ distribution like this is a quick alternative to the delta method, and to the bootstrap of Lesson 6 (which would refit the model for every resample).

Quick quiz

1. How does a right-censored observation enter the likelihood?

It’s evidence that the lifetime is longer than x, and that’s exactly what S(x) measures.

2. Why optimize over \log k and \log\lambda instead of k and \lambda?

On the original scale, BFGS ran out of iterations far from the answer.

3. What does Nelder-Mead use to find a minimum?

That makes it robust, but slow.

4. What does the inverse of the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at its minimum estimate?

Sharp curvature means a well-determined estimate and a small standard error.

5. What does glm() use to fit a logistic regression?

That’s why it reports the number of Fisher scoring iterations.

6. Gradient descent needed 2,000 steps and Newton’s method 6. Why use gradient descent at all?

For three parameters, Newton wins easily. For a neural network, a Hessian wouldn’t even fit in memory.

Wrap-up

You can now maximize likelihoods with many parameters, and get their uncertainty too. Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Censored data: failures contribute the density, and survivors the survival function. The pumps’ seals have a Weibull shape of 1.94 and a scale of 1,758 days.
  • optim(): Nelder-Mead uses function values only (101 evaluations here), and BFGS uses gradients and a built-up curvature (35).
  • Transform the parameters: on the log scale they stay positive and comparable. On the original scale, BFGS stalled far from the answer, and said so with convergence = 1.
  • Standard errors come from the inverse Hessian: a shape between 1.42 and 2.65, clear wear-out.
  • Gradient descent vs Newton: 2,000 cheap steps against 6 expensive ones for the gasket logistic regression. Newton’s method for logistic regression is Fisher scoring, the IRLS inside glm().
  • Don’t drop the survivors: without the censored pumps, the scale falls by 19%, and the B10 life from 551 to 471 days.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Right-censored observation
An observation known only to be larger than a recorded value, such as a pump that hasn’t failed yet.
Survival function
S(x) = P(T > x), the probability of lasting longer than x.
Nelder-Mead
A derivative-free optimizer that moves and reshapes a simplex of points.
Quasi-Newton method
An optimizer, such as BFGS, that uses gradients and builds up an approximation to the curvature.
Hessian
The matrix of second derivatives. For a negative log-likelihood at its minimum, it’s the observed information, and its inverse estimates the covariance matrix of the estimates.
Gradient descent
An optimizer that repeatedly steps against the gradient, with a chosen step size.
Fisher scoring
Newton’s method with the expected information in place of the Hessian. For logistic regression, it’s the same as iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS).
B10 life
The age by which 10% of items have failed.
