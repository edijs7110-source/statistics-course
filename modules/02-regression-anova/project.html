Module 2 project: a complete regression analysis

Turn the new reactor’s commissioning runs into a recommendation the plant can act on

The Module 2 project brief: analyze 64 commissioning runs of the new 2,000-liter reactor R3, check the records, build and check models for yield and purity, recommend operating conditions with honest uncertainty, decide whether catalyst B is worth its price, and write it up for an engineer.

The plant has built a new reactor, R3: 2,000 liters, ten times the size of the pilot reactors. Between September and mid-October, the process engineers ran it 64 times to commission it, varying the temperature, the residence time and the agitator speed, and trying both catalysts. Production on R3 starts in November, and Dana, the process engineer in charge, has a question for you: how should we run it?

That’s the whole of Module 2 in one project: exploring relationships, building a multiple regression with curves and categories, checking it, finding the runs that don’t belong, choosing between models, and turning the result into a decision with honest uncertainty.

Module 2 project Week 46 of the study plan About 8–10 hours

This time, you’re the statistician the plant calls in. Dana’s note warns you about two things. You know by now that real data always have a few more surprises. Good luck, detective!

What you’ll make

One report, written in Quarto in your plant-study RStudio project, that Dana could act on tomorrow. It should contain:

  1. A summary for Dana, at the top, in plain words: the settings you recommend, the yield she can expect, whether catalyst B is worth its price, and the main caveats.
  2. The evidence: what you found in the data, the models you built for the yield and the purity, how you checked them, and what you changed because of the checks.
  3. The uncertainty: confidence intervals for what’s known, and prediction intervals for what a single batch might do.
  4. A cleaning log and a list of limitations: every decision about the data, and everything that these 64 runs can’t tell you.

Nobody grades it, but write it as if Dana will base November’s production plan on it. Because in real work, she would.

The handover

Download both files into a new folder in your project, data-raw/r3/, and don’t edit them:

  • README.txt: Dana’s note, with the question, the columns and the known issues. Read it first.
  • r3_runs.csv: the 64 commissioning runs.

Then download the project template, save it in the top folder of your project as r3-analysis.qmd, and click Render. It reads the data, prints a first look at it and draws some scatterplots, with headings for every step. Fill it in one section at a time, and render often.

The brief, step by step

  1. Explore (Lessons 1, 5). Plot the yield and the purity against every predictor. Look at the ranges, the notes and anything odd. What relationships do you expect from the pilot plant, and which ones look different at this scale?
  2. Check the records (Lessons 8, 10). The run sheets were typed in by hand. Fit a first, simple model and look for runs that don’t fit, with studentized residuals, leverage and Cook’s distance. For each suspect, look for an explanation in the notes and the other columns. Correct what you can confirm, exclude only with a reason, and write every decision in the cleaning log.
  3. Build a model for the yield (Lessons 5, 7, 9). Start from what you learned on the pilot reactors: which terms should be in the model, and in what form? Does the yield curve with temperature? Dana’s note asks you to check the agitator speed again, so look at it carefully: does it matter, and does it matter everywhere in its range? Center the temperature.
  4. Check the model (Lessons 8, 10, 11). Residuals against the fitted values, against every predictor and in run order, a Q-Q plot, the influence measures, and the VIFs. If a residual plot shows a pattern, change the model and look again.
  5. Compare candidate models (Lessons 6, 12). Use partial F-tests for nested models, and AIC, BIC or cross-validation to choose between a few serious candidates. Prefer the simplest model that the diagnostics are happy with.
  6. Model the purity (Lessons 5, 9). It’s the constraint: the customer’s limit is 98.5%. Which conditions lower it? Two purity results are missing: note why, and check that leaving them out is reasonable (Module 1, Lesson 12).
  7. Choose the operating conditions (Lessons 3, 9). Search a grid of settings inside the range of the data, keep only those where the purity is safely above 98.5%, and find the highest predicted yield. “Safely” is your call: the template suggests using the lower end of the purity’s 95% prediction interval, so that a single batch would rarely fall below the limit. Say what you chose, and why.
  8. Say how sure you are (Lessons 3, 6). At your recommended settings, give a confidence interval for the average batch’s yield and a prediction interval for a single batch, and explain the difference in words Dana would use. For catalyst B, the question is whether it adds at least 2 points: give its interval, and test it against 2 (an offset() from Lesson 6 does it).
  9. Write it up. Summary first, then the details. Plots that make each point, tables that are easy to read, and the limitations at the end.

Stretch goal. Kestrel’s feed lots run at 260 ppm of water or more. How should the operating conditions change for a wet lot, and what yield should Dana expect then? And here’s a different angle on the purity constraint: turn the purity into a yes-or-no outcome, “at least 98.5%”, and fit a logistic regression for it (Lesson 15). With only about 60 runs, how does that compare with modeling the purity itself? (It throws away information, which is a lesson in itself.)

Check your own work

When you’ve finished, assess your project against this rubric. Aim for the right-hand column.

Tips

  • Look before you model. Many of this project’s surprises are visible in a scatterplot. One run’s yield is more than eight points above any other run’s. Is that believable?
  • Use the notes, and be fair about them. A note can explain an outlier, but not every note means a run is bad.
  • Don’t trust the pilot plant blindly. R3 is ten times bigger. Things that didn’t matter in 200 liters, like mixing, can matter in 2,000.
  • A curve in the residuals is a clue about the physics. If yield stops improving above some agitator speed, that’s a statement about mixing, and it suggests a simple model term. Try a few forms and compare them.
  • Stay inside the data. The best settings often sit at the edge of what was tried. Say so, and suggest runs that would confirm them.
  • Two kinds of “how sure”. Dana’s planners care about the average batch (a confidence interval). The customer cares about their batch (a prediction interval).
  • Write for the reader. Dana knows the chemistry better than you do. She doesn’t need the formula for Cook’s distance. She needs to know which runs you set aside, and why.

When it’s done, you’ll have taken a real question from data to decision: cleaning, modeling, checking, choosing and communicating. That’s what applied statisticians do all day. Congratulations on finishing Module 2! Module 3 looks at several responses at once, with multivariate methods.
