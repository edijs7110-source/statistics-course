Regression in matrix notation

One formula for every linear model: the design matrix, the hat matrix and the variance of the estimates

Write any linear model as y = Xβ + ε, solve the normal equations for any number of predictors, meet the hat matrix and leverage, see why residuals are orthogonal to the predictors, and get every standard error from σ²(X’X)⁻¹.

Module 2 · Lesson 4 of 15 Week 34 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The formulas for simple regression were tidy: a slope, an intercept, a couple of sums of squares. But the March batches have a temperature, a pressure, a catalyst and an alarm count too, and writing out separate formulas for models with three or five predictors would be hopeless. Matrices fix that. With them, every linear model, with any number of predictors, has the same few formulas.

You met the main one in Lesson 0.14: the least-squares coefficients are \mathbf{b} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}. This lesson builds on it. You’ll see what’s inside X^\top X, meet the matrix that turns data into fitted values, and find every standard error in a single matrix.

Don’t worry if matrices still feel new: every formula here gets checked against lm(), so you can see that the algebra is just bookkeeping. Very tidy bookkeeping!

In this lesson you’ll:

  • write a regression model for a whole dataset as one matrix equation
  • solve the normal equations for a model with two predictors
  • meet the hat matrix, and see what it does
  • measure each observation’s leverage
  • see why residuals are orthogonal to every predictor
  • get the standard errors, and the uncertainty of a prediction, from \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}

One equation for the whole dataset

The simple regression model is really 40 equations, one for each batch:

\begin{aligned} y_1 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \varepsilon_1 \\ y_2 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_2 + \varepsilon_2 \\ &\;\;\vdots \\ y_{40} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{40} + \varepsilon_{40} \end{aligned}

Stack them up, and they become one equation between vectors and a matrix:

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_{40} \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{40} \end{pmatrix}}_{X} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_{40} \end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon .

X is the design matrix: one row per observation, and one column per coefficient. The column of 1s carries the intercept. With p coefficients and n observations, X is n \times p, and the model looks the same whatever p is. A second predictor is just another column.

The assumptions get a compact form too. “The errors average zero” becomes E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0}. “Independent errors with equal variance” becomes \text{Var}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I: a covariance matrix with \sigma^2 all along its diagonal (equal variances) and zeros everywhere else (no correlation between errors).

model.matrix() shows you the design matrix that lm() builds:

fit <- lm(yield_pct ~ residence_min, data = batches)
head(model.matrix(fit), 4)
#>   (Intercept) residence_min
#> 1           1          47.2
#> 2           1          37.9
#> 3           1          32.5
#> 4           1          60.7

Your turn. The design matrix X and the yields y are ready for you. Compute X^\top X with crossprod(). What do its four numbers mean?

crossprod(X) computes t(X) %*% X, a little faster.

crossprod(X)

Each entry is a column of X multiplied by another column, then summed. The 1s column times itself counts the batches, 40. The 1s column times the residence column gives the sum of the residence times, 1,790 minutes, and the residence column times itself gives the sum of their squares, 85,025. So X^\top X holds n, \sum x_i and \sum x_i^2: everything least squares needs to know about the xs.

The least-squares solution

Least squares minimizes \text{SSE} = (\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{b})^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{b}). Setting its gradient to zero, as in Lesson 0.14, gives the normal equations and their solution:

X^\top X\,\mathbf{b} = X^\top \mathbf{y} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{b} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y} .

For one predictor, this reproduces b_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx} and b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x} from Lesson 2. But it works unchanged for any number of predictors. That’s the whole point.

In code, solve(A, b) solves the system A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} directly, which is faster and more accurate than computing the inverse first:

X <- model.matrix(fit)
y <- batches$yield_pct
solve(crossprod(X), crossprod(X, y))
#>                    [,1]
#> (Intercept)   27.680575
#> residence_min  0.669652

The same 27.68 and 0.670 that lm() found. (lm() itself uses an even more stable method, the QR decomposition, but the answer is the same.)

Your turn. Now add a second predictor. Build the design matrix for yield_pct ~ residence_min + temp_C and solve the normal equations. model.matrix() leaves out the two batches with no temperature, so drop them from the yields too.

Exactly as before: solve(crossprod(X2), crossprod(X2, y2)).

b2 <- filter(batches, !is.na(temp_C))
X2 <- model.matrix(~ residence_min + temp_C, data = b2)
y2 <- b2$yield_pct
solve(crossprod(X2), crossprod(X2, y2))

An intercept of 5.08, 0.644 points per minute of residence time, and 0.132 points per degree. Check it with coef(lm(yield_pct ~ residence_min + temp_C, data = b2)). What these coefficients mean, now that there are two of them, is the subject of the next lesson.

The hat matrix

Put the solution back into the model to get the fitted values:

\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{b} = \underbrace{X (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top}_{H}\, \mathbf{y} = H\mathbf{y} .

The n \times n matrix H is called the hat matrix, because it puts the hat on \mathbf{y}. Each fitted value is a weighted sum of all the observed responses: \hat{y}_i = \sum_j h_{ij} y_j. Notice that H depends only on X. The weights are set by the predictors, before any yield is measured.

H has three neat properties:

  • It’s symmetric: H^\top = H.
  • It’s idempotent: HH = H. Applying it twice changes nothing, because the fitted values already lie on the fitted line. Fitting a line to points that are on a line gives the same line.
  • Its trace, the sum of its diagonal, equals p, the number of coefficients.
NoteGo deeper: least squares is a projection

Think of \mathbf{y} as a single point in 40-dimensional space, one coordinate per batch. The columns of X are two more points in that space, and all their combinations X\mathbf{b} form a flat two-dimensional plane through the origin: every possible set of fitted values that a straight line could produce.

Least squares finds the point of that plane closest to \mathbf{y}. The closest point is the one you reach by dropping a perpendicular, so \hat{\mathbf{y}} is the orthogonal projection of \mathbf{y} onto the plane, and H is the matrix that performs it. That’s why HH = H (projecting a point that’s already on the plane leaves it where it is), and why the residual vector \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} is perpendicular to the plane, which is the next section’s X^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}.

Pythagoras finishes the picture. \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1} lies in the plane, \mathbf{e} is perpendicular to it, and their sum is \mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}. So the squared lengths add up: \text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE}. The decomposition from Lesson 2 is a right-angled triangle in 40 dimensions!

Your turn. Build the hat matrix for the simple model, and check its trace.

The trace is the sum of the diagonal: diag(H) pulls the diagonal out.

H <- X %*% solve(crossprod(X)) %*% t(X)
sum(diag(H))

Exactly 2, the number of coefficients. You can also check that max(abs(H %*% y - fitted(fit))) is essentially zero, and that H %*% H equals H.

Leverage

The diagonal entries h_{ii} are special. Each one says how much observation i’s own response pulls on its own fitted value, and it’s called that observation’s leverage. In simple regression, it has a formula you can read:

h_{ii} = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_i - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} .

A batch with an average residence time has the smallest possible leverage, 1/n. The further its x is from the average, the more leverage it has, like a person sitting at the end of a seesaw. Leverages always lie between 1/n and 1, and since they add up to p, they average p/n, here 2/40 = 0.05. A common rule of thumb flags any observation with more than twice the average, 2p/n.

hatvalues() returns the leverages of any model:

batches |>
  mutate(leverage = hatvalues(fit)) |>
  arrange(desc(leverage)) |>
  select(batch_id, residence_min, leverage) |>
  head(5)
#> # A tibble: 5 × 3
#>   batch_id residence_min leverage
#>   <chr>            <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1 B40               15.9    0.194
#> 2 B29               71.7    0.173
#> 3 B10               21      0.140
#> 4 B33               65.4    0.112
#> 5 B07               64      0.100

Five batches pass 2p/n = 0.1, B07 only just: the two shortest runs and the three longest. High leverage isn’t bad in itself. It means those batches have a big say in where the line goes, so if one of them had a typo in its yield, the line would follow it. Lesson 10 is about telling a harmless high-leverage point from a harmful one.

Your turn. Which batch has the highest leverage? Use slice_max().

hatvalues(fit) returns the leverages, in row order.

batches |>
  mutate(leverage = hatvalues(fit)) |>
  slice_max(leverage) |>
  select(batch_id, residence_min, leverage)

B40, the shortest run at 15.9 minutes, with a leverage of 0.19: almost four times the average. The batch with the lowest leverage, B19, ran for 44.9 minutes, right next to the average, and its leverage is 1/40 = 0.025.

Residuals in matrix form

The residuals are what’s left after the fit:

\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = (I - H)\,\mathbf{y} .

Rearrange the normal equations, X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{0}, and you get one of the most useful facts in regression:

X^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0} .

Each row of this equation multiplies one column of X by the residuals and sums. The 1s column gives \sum e_i = 0: the residuals sum to zero. Every other column says that the residuals are orthogonal to that predictor, which, together with the zero sum, means they’re uncorrelated with it. With five predictors, you get six such equations. Whatever linear pattern the predictors could explain, the fit has already taken out.

One more fact about the residuals, which matters in Lesson 10. Since \text{Var}(\mathbf{e}) = \sigma^2 (I - H), each residual has variance \sigma^2(1 - h_{ii}). They’re not all equally variable: high-leverage observations have smaller residuals, because they pull the line towards themselves. B40’s residual has only 81% of the variance that an average-leverage batch’s would have.

Your turn. Compute the residuals with the hat matrix, then check that X^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}.

crossprod(X, e) computes X^\top \mathbf{e}.

H <- X %*% solve(crossprod(X)) %*% t(X)
e <- y - H %*% y
crossprod(X, e)

Both entries are around 10^{-11} or smaller: zero, apart from rounding errors in the computer’s arithmetic.

The variance of the estimates

Now the payoff. Substitute the model into the solution: \mathbf{b} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \boldsymbol\beta + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol\varepsilon. Two results follow in a line each:

E(\mathbf{b}) = \boldsymbol\beta, \qquad \text{Var}(\mathbf{b}) = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top (\sigma^2 I)\, X (X^\top X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1} .

The estimates are unbiased, and their whole covariance matrix is \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}. Replace \sigma^2 by s^2, and its diagonal holds the squared standard errors. vcov() returns it:

vcov(fit)
#>               (Intercept) residence_min
#> (Intercept)    17.0415230  -0.358769075
#> residence_min  -0.3587691   0.008017186
sqrt(diag(vcov(fit)))
#>   (Intercept) residence_min 
#>    4.12813796    0.08953874

There are the standard errors from summary(), 4.13 and 0.0895, for any model you’ll ever fit. The off-diagonal entry is the covariance of the intercept and slope estimates. Converted to a correlation, cov2cor(vcov(fit)), it’s −0.97! The fitted line pivots around (\bar{x}, \bar{y}), and since \bar{x} = 44.75 is far from 0, a line that comes out a little too steep must cross the axis a little too low. So the two estimates are tied together, which matters whenever you combine them, as in a prediction.

NoteGo deeper: the Gauss-Markov theorem

Least squares isn’t just a way of estimating \boldsymbol\beta. Among all estimators that are linear in \mathbf{y} and unbiased, it has the smallest variance. That’s the Gauss-Markov theorem, and it needs only E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0} and \text{Var}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I, not normality.

The proof is short. Any linear estimator is \tilde{\mathbf{b}} = C\mathbf{y} for some matrix C. Write C = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D. Unbiasedness, E(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}) = CX\boldsymbol\beta = \boldsymbol\beta for every \boldsymbol\beta, forces DX = 0. Then

\text{Var}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}) = \sigma^2 CC^\top = \sigma^2\left[(X^\top X)^{-1} + DD^\top\right],

because the cross terms contain DX = 0. DD^\top can never reduce a variance (each diagonal entry is a sum of squares), so the best choice is D = 0: least squares. Statisticians call it BLUE, the best linear unbiased estimator.

The conditions matter, though. If the errors have unequal variances or are correlated, least squares is no longer best, and Lesson 9’s weighted least squares can beat it.

Your turn. Compute the covariance matrix of the estimates from the formula, s^2(X^\top X)^{-1}, and take the square roots of its diagonal.

The matrix to invert is X^\top X: crossprod(X).

V <- sigma(fit)^2 * solve(crossprod(X))
sqrt(diag(V))

4.13 for the intercept and 0.0895 for the slope, the same as summary().

The variance of a prediction

A prediction at new settings is a linear combination of the estimates: \hat{y}_0 = \mathbf{x}_0^\top \mathbf{b}, where \mathbf{x}_0 is a row of a design matrix, like (1, 60) for a 60-minute batch. A linear combination’s variance comes straight from the covariance matrix:

\text{Var}(\hat{y}_0) = \mathbf{x}_0^\top\, \text{Var}(\mathbf{b})\, \mathbf{x}_0 = \sigma^2\, \mathbf{x}_0^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \mathbf{x}_0 .

For simple regression, this works out to the confidence interval formula from Lesson 3, s^2\left[1/n + (x_0 - \bar{x})^2/S_{xx}\right]. But it works for any model, and it uses the covariance of the estimates, which is why you can’t just add up their separate standard errors.

Your turn. Compute the standard error of the predicted mean yield at 60 minutes. Last lesson, predict() gave 1.69.

The new row of the design matrix is a 1 for the intercept and the residence time, 60.

V <- vcov(fit)
x0 <- c(1, 60)
sqrt(t(x0) %*% V %*% x0)

1.69, matching predict(fit, tibble(residence_min = 60), se.fit = TRUE)$se.fit. If you had ignored the covariance and added the two variances, 17.04 + 60^2 \times 0.008, you’d get a standard error of 6.8, four times too big.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Centering untangles the estimates. Subtract the average residence time from every residence time, refit, and look at the correlation between the intercept and slope estimates.

Subtract mean(residence_min).

centered <- mutate(batches, res_c = residence_min - mean(residence_min))
fit_c <- lm(yield_pct ~ res_c, data = centered)
list(coefficients = coef(fit_c), correlation = cov2cor(vcov(fit_c))[1, 2])

The slope is unchanged, 0.670. The intercept is now 57.65, the mean yield: the predicted yield at an average residence time, which actually means something. And the correlation between the two estimates is zero. With a centered predictor, the 1s column and the residence column are orthogonal, so X^\top X is diagonal, and its inverse is too. You’ll use centering again with interactions (Lesson 7) and polynomials (Lesson 11).

Quick quiz

1. A model has an intercept and two predictors, fitted to 38 batches. What are the dimensions of its design matrix?

One row per observation and one column per coefficient: the intercept’s column of 1s plus one column for each predictor.

2. What does the hat matrix do?

H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top projects \mathbf{y} onto the space spanned by the columns of X.

3. The diagonal of the hat matrix always adds up to…

The trace of H is p, so the average leverage is p/n.

4. What does an observation’s leverage depend on?

H is built from X alone. An observation has high leverage when its x values are far from the rest, whatever its y.

5. How do you get the standard errors of the coefficients from \text{Var}(\mathbf{b}) = s^2(X^\top X)^{-1}?

The diagonal holds the variances of the estimates, and the off-diagonal entries hold their covariances.

6. The residuals of a least-squares fit with an intercept are always…

That’s X^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}, a direct consequence of the normal equations. Normality is an assumption about the errors, not a property of the residuals.

Wrap-up

You now have the matrix machinery that every regression program runs on! Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • The model: \mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, with E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0} and \text{Var}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I. The design matrix X has a row per observation and a column per coefficient. model.matrix() shows it.
  • Least squares: X^\top X\,\mathbf{b} = X^\top\mathbf{y}, so \mathbf{b} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}, for any number of predictors.
  • The hat matrix H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top turns \mathbf{y} into \hat{\mathbf{y}}. It’s symmetric and idempotent, with trace p. Geometrically, it’s a projection.
  • Leverage is h_{ii}, the diagonal of H. It’s large for observations with unusual x values, and it averages p/n.
  • Residuals: \mathbf{e} = (I - H)\mathbf{y}, with X^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0} and \text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma^2(1 - h_{ii}).
  • Uncertainty: \text{Var}(\mathbf{b}) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1} gives every standard error and covariance (vcov()), and \text{Var}(\mathbf{x}_0^\top\mathbf{b}) = \sigma^2\mathbf{x}_0^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0 gives a prediction’s.
  • Gauss-Markov: with uncorrelated, equal-variance errors, least squares is the best linear unbiased estimator.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Design matrix (X)
The n \times p matrix of predictor values, with a column of 1s for the intercept: one row per observation and one column per coefficient.
Normal equations
X^\top X\,\mathbf{b} = X^\top\mathbf{y}, the equations that the least-squares coefficients solve.
Hat matrix (H)
H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top, the matrix that turns the responses into the fitted values.
Idempotent
A matrix that equals its own square, HH = H, like every projection.
Leverage
An observation’s diagonal entry h_{ii} of the hat matrix, which measures how unusual its predictor values are and how much it can pull the fit.
Orthogonal
Perpendicular. Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero, as the residuals are to every column of X.
Covariance matrix of the estimates
\text{Var}(\mathbf{b}) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}, with the variances of the coefficients on its diagonal and their covariances off it.
Gauss-Markov theorem
With uncorrelated errors of equal variance, least squares has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators (BLUE).
