Multicollinearity

When predictors tell the same story, and the model can’t tell them apart

See how correlated predictors inflate standard errors and destabilize coefficients without hurting predictions, detect multicollinearity with variance inflation factors and condition numbers, and choose a remedy: dropping, combining, centering or collecting better data.

Module 2 · Lesson 11 of 15 Week 41 of 160 About 5–7 hours

The process study records the reactor temperature. It also records the temperature of the heating jacket around the reactor, and the reactor pressure, which rises with temperature. Three measurements of nearly the same thing. If more information is always better, why not put them all in the model? This lesson shows what happens when you do: the coefficients go haywire, the standard errors explode, and yet the predictions are fine. That’s multicollinearity, and knowing when it matters, and when it doesn’t, will save you a lot of confusion.

From this lesson on, we use the cleaned study from Lesson 10: P066’s temperature corrected to 185.1 °C, and P047, with its failed stirrer, left out.

Imagine asking two friends who always agree which of them gave you good advice. You can’t tell! That’s multicollinearity: the data never show one predictor moving without the other.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • see where correlated predictors come from in plant data
  • watch what they do to coefficients, standard errors and predictions
  • meet perfect collinearity, where the model can’t be fitted at all
  • measure multicollinearity with variance inflation factors and condition numbers
  • see why centering fixes the collinearity in polynomial and interaction models
  • choose a remedy

Predictors that move together

Here are the correlations between six of the process study’s predictors:

study <- read_csv("data/process_study.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(temp_C = if_else(run_id == "P066", 185.1, temp_C)) |>
  filter(run_id != "P047")
round(cor(select(study, temp_C, jacket_C, pressure_bar, residence_min, feed_rate_Lmin, feed_water_ppm)), 2)
#>                temp_C jacket_C pressure_bar residence_min feed_rate_Lmin
#> temp_C           1.00     0.99         0.96         -0.07           0.10
#> jacket_C         0.99     1.00         0.95         -0.08           0.10
#> pressure_bar     0.96     0.95         1.00         -0.05           0.08
#> residence_min   -0.07    -0.08        -0.05          1.00          -0.95
#> feed_rate_Lmin   0.10     0.10         0.08         -0.95           1.00
#> feed_water_ppm   0.05     0.05         0.05          0.40          -0.38
#>                feed_water_ppm
#> temp_C                   0.05
#> jacket_C                 0.05
#> pressure_bar             0.05
#> residence_min            0.40
#> feed_rate_Lmin          -0.38
#> feed_water_ppm           1.00

Three tight clusters, each with a physical reason:

  • Temperature, jacket and pressure (correlations 0.95 to 0.99). The jacket heats the reactor, so it runs about 11 °C hotter; and in a closed reactor, the vapor pressure rises with the temperature.
  • Residence time and feed rate (−0.95). The residence time is the reactor volume divided by the flow rate: a slower feed means a longer stay.
  • Residence time and feed water (0.40), from the operators’ habit you met in Lesson 5.

This is completely normal for process data. Many variables are measured because they’re easy to measure, not because they’re independent.

What collinearity does

Start with Lesson 5’s three-predictor model, and then add the jacket temperature:

fit1 <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm, data = study)
fit2 <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + jacket_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm, data = study)
summary(fit1)$coefficients
#>                   Estimate  Std. Error   t value     Pr(>|t|)
#> (Intercept)    -56.1138294 6.123064512 -9.164337 2.295926e-15
#> temp_C           0.5984626 0.031165642 19.202641 1.130589e-37
#> residence_min    0.3303743 0.029331362 11.263517 2.766609e-20
#> feed_water_ppm  -0.0554867 0.008431755 -6.580682 1.451738e-09
summary(fit2)$coefficients
#>                    Estimate  Std. Error    t value     Pr(>|t|)
#> (Intercept)    -53.66436728 7.088488352 -7.5706363 1.048213e-11
#> temp_C           0.79860302 0.291521480  2.7394312 7.144914e-03
#> jacket_C        -0.20123827 0.291433073 -0.6905128 4.912753e-01
#> residence_min    0.32941521 0.029431104 11.1927575 4.527337e-20
#> feed_water_ppm  -0.05539789 0.008451979 -6.5544279 1.690387e-09

Look at what happened:

  • The temperature’s standard error jumped from 0.031 to 0.29, almost ten times bigger. Its confidence interval now runs from about 0.2 to 1.4 points per degree.
  • The jacket has a negative coefficient, −0.20 per degree. Taken literally, a hotter jacket lowers the yield, which makes no physical sense.
  • Neither temperature looks very important on its own, yet together they’re clearly essential, and on its own, the jacket temperature works just as well as the reactor’s (0.59 per degree, t = 18).
  • The fit hasn’t changed: R^2 is 0.799 in one model and 0.800 in the other, and the partial F-test for the jacket says p = 0.49.

Why? Each coefficient is a partial effect: the change in yield when one temperature rises and the other stays fixed. But in this study, the two temperatures never move apart: the jacket is always 8 to 14 °C hotter. The data contain almost no information about what happens when one changes without the other, so the model can’t split the credit between them. It knows that their combined effect is about 0.6 points per degree, and almost nothing about how to share it out. That’s why the individual coefficients are so uncertain, and so sensitive: drop or add a few runs, and the split can swing wildly.

Predictions are a different matter. As long as you predict for conditions like the data, where the jacket runs about 11 °C above the reactor, both models agree. Multicollinearity is a problem for interpreting coefficients, not for prediction within the pattern of the data.

Your turn. By what factor did adding the jacket inflate the temperature’s standard error?

The model with the jacket is fit2.

se1 <- summary(fit1)$coefficients["temp_C", "Std. Error"]
se2 <- summary(fit2)$coefficients["temp_C", "Std. Error"]
se2 / se1

9.4 times bigger. To get the temperature’s coefficient back to its old precision while keeping the jacket in the model, you’d need about 9.4^2 = 88 times as many runs.

Perfect collinearity

At the extreme, one predictor is an exact linear combination of others. Then the model can’t be fitted at all, because X^\top X has no inverse (Lesson 4). It happens more often than you’d think: the same temperature in °C and °F, a total and all of its parts, or a dummy for every category of a factor. R doesn’t stop with an error. It quietly drops a column and reports NA for its coefficient, so watch out for NAs in a coefficient table.

Your turn. Add the temperature in Fahrenheit to a model that already has it in Celsius. Which coefficient comes out as NA?

is.na(b) is TRUE for the missing coefficient.

study_f <- mutate(study, temp_F = 1.8 * temp_C + 32)
fit_f <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + temp_F + residence_min, data = study_f)
b <- coef(fit_f)
names(b)[is.na(b)]

temp_F. R noticed that it’s exactly 1.8 \times temp_C + 32, a combination of the temperature and the intercept, and dropped it. alias(fit_f) shows the exact relationship it found.

Variance inflation factors

How do you measure multicollinearity? Ask how well each predictor can be predicted from the others. Regress predictor j on all the other predictors, and call that regression’s R^2 R_j^2. Then the variance inflation factor is

\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} .

It’s exactly the factor by which predictor j’s variance is inflated, compared with a world where it was uncorrelated with the others. So its standard error is inflated by \sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}. A VIF of 1 means no collinearity at all. Common rules of thumb call a VIF above 5, or above 10, a problem: that’s R_j^2 above 0.8 or 0.9, and a standard error more than about twice or three times as big as it could be.

The car package’s vif() computes them for every predictor:

car::vif(fit1)
#>         temp_C  residence_min feed_water_ppm 
#>       1.013209       1.202594       1.200013
car::vif(fit2)
#>         temp_C       jacket_C  residence_min feed_water_ppm 
#>      88.248540      88.322925       1.205278       1.200291

Without the jacket, every VIF is below 1.3. With it, both temperatures have VIFs of 88. Put all six correlated predictors in, and the VIFs reach 104 for the temperature, 89 for the jacket, 13 for the pressure, and about 11 for the residence time and the feed rate.

For a categorical predictor, or a predictor with interactions or polynomial terms, a single VIF per coefficient can mislead. car::vif() then reports a generalized VIF (GVIF) for each term, and its GVIF^(1/(2*Df)) column is comparable to \sqrt{\text{VIF}}.

Your turn. Compute the temperature’s VIF in fit2 by hand: regress the temperature on the model’s other predictors, and use that regression’s R^2.

The other predictors in fit2 are jacket_C, residence_min and feed_water_ppm.

r2_temp <- summary(lm(temp_C ~ jacket_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm, data = study))$r.squared
1 / (1 - r2_temp)

88.2: the other predictors explain 98.9% of the temperature’s variation. And \sqrt{88.2} = 9.4, just the inflation you found in the standard error.

Condition numbers

VIFs look at one predictor at a time. The condition number looks at all of them together. Standardize the predictors, and compute the eigenvalues of their correlation matrix (Lesson 0.14). Each eigenvalue measures how much the data spread out in one direction of the predictor space. A tiny eigenvalue means a direction in which the data hardly vary at all: a combination of predictors that’s almost constant, like “jacket minus temperature”. The condition number compares the biggest spread with the smallest:

\kappa = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}} .

A common rule of thumb says that values above about 30 mean serious trouble, and above 10 to 15 deserve a look. (R’s kappa() computes a condition number for any matrix. Use kappa(scale(X), exact = TRUE) for the standardized version.) The eigenvectors that go with the small eigenvalues even tell you which predictors are tangled together.

Your turn. Compute the condition number of all six predictors from the eigenvalues of their correlation matrix.

Divide by the smallest eigenvalue, min(ev).

X <- select(study, temp_C, jacket_C, pressure_bar, residence_min, feed_rate_Lmin, feed_water_ppm)
ev <- eigen(cor(X))$values
round(ev, 4)
sqrt(max(ev) / min(ev))

23.6. The smallest eigenvalue, 0.005, belongs to the jacket-minus-temperature direction, and the next two to the residence-feed-rate pair (0.047) and the pressure (0.056). With only the temperature, residence time and water, the condition number is 1.6.

Collinearity you make yourself: polynomials and interactions

Some collinearity comes from the model, not the data. Over the study’s range, from 167 to 206 °C, the temperature and its square are almost perfectly correlated: both just get bigger together. Fit a quadratic without centering, and the VIFs are over 1,300:

fit_raw <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + I(temp_C^2) + residence_min + feed_water_ppm, data = study)
fit_cen <- lm(yield_pct ~ I(temp_C - 185) + I((temp_C - 185)^2) + residence_min + feed_water_ppm, data = study)
car::vif(fit_raw)
#>         temp_C    I(temp_C^2)  residence_min feed_water_ppm 
#>    1349.858814    1351.459696       1.242972       1.218138
car::vif(fit_cen)
#>     I(temp_C - 185) I((temp_C - 185)^2)       residence_min      feed_water_ppm 
#>            1.241224            1.285389            1.242972            1.218138

Centered at 185 °C, they drop to about 1.3. The two models fit identically: same fitted values, same residuals, same squared-term coefficient. What changes is the linear term. Uncentered, it’s the slope at 0 °C, 6.8 ± 1.0 points per degree, a useless extrapolation. Centered, it’s the slope at 185 °C, 0.68 ± 0.03. That’s the collinearity Lesson 7 removed by centering the interaction, too. This kind is harmless once you know about it, because centering removes it completely, but it’s a common reason for alarming VIFs in perfectly good models.

Your turn. Compare the correlation between the temperature and its square, uncentered and centered at 185 °C.

Center at 185 °C.

t <- study$temp_C
tc <- t - 185
c(uncentered = cor(t, t^2), centered = cor(tc, tc^2))

0.9996 uncentered, 0.44 centered. (It isn’t zero, because the temperatures aren’t perfectly symmetric around 185 °C. Centering at the mean would get it closer still.)

Remedies

What should you do about multicollinearity? It depends on what you want the model for.

  • Nothing, if you only need predictions for conditions like the data. The combined effect is well estimated, and so are the predictions.
  • Drop redundant predictors. The jacket temperature adds nothing that the reactor temperature doesn’t already say, and the reactor temperature is the one that drives the chemistry. Keep the variable with the clearest physical meaning.
  • Combine them. If several sensors measure the same thing, like three thermocouples in a reactor, average them into one better measurement.
  • Center polynomial and interaction terms.
  • Collect data that break the pattern. The only way to learn the separate effects of two variables is to see them move apart. In a designed experiment, you set the factors independently, so there’s no collinearity at all. That’s the deep reason the next three lessons are about experiments.
  • Use methods built for it. Ridge regression and the lasso (Module 7) trade a little bias for much smaller variance, and principal components (Module 3) replace correlated predictors with uncorrelated combinations.

One trap to avoid: never interpret the sign of a coefficient with a big VIF. The jacket’s “negative effect” is noise, not chemistry.

Your turn. Predictions are fine within the pattern of the data. What about outside it? Use fit2 to predict the yield at 190 °C, 50 minutes and 200 ppm, with a jacket at 201 °C (normal, 11 degrees hotter) and at 215 °C (25 degrees hotter, never seen in the study). Compare the widths of their 95% confidence intervals.

The second jacket temperature is 215.

new <- tibble(temp_C = 190, jacket_C = c(201, 215), residence_min = 50, feed_water_ppm = 200)
p <- predict(fit2, newdata = new, interval = "confidence")
p[, "upr"] - p[, "lwr"]

1.8 points wide for the normal jacket, and 16.3 for the unusual one. Each value, 190 °C and 215 °C, is within the range of its own variable, but the combination is far outside the data: an extrapolation in a direction the data never explored. With correlated predictors, “inside the range of each variable” isn’t the same as “inside the data”.

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · Residence time or feed rate? The residence time and the feed rate are two views of the same thing. Put both in a model with the temperature and the water, and look at their VIFs. Then compare the R^2 of the model with the residence time alone and the model with the feed rate alone. Which would you keep?

The second model uses feed_rate_Lmin instead of residence_min.

r2_res <- summary(lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm, data = study))$r.squared
r2_feed <- summary(lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + feed_rate_Lmin + feed_water_ppm, data = study))$r.squared
c(residence = r2_res, feed_rate = r2_feed)

0.799 with the residence time, 0.784 with the feed rate. In a model with both, their VIFs are about 10.6, and the feed rate isn’t significant (p = 0.42). Keep the residence time: it fits better, because the yield depends on how long the reactants spend together, which is proportional to the residence time and only inversely related to the feed rate. The chemistry and the data agree.

Challenge 2 · The two-predictor formula. With only two predictors, R_j^2 is simply their squared correlation, so \text{VIF} = 1/(1 - r^2). Compute it from the correlation between the reactor and jacket temperatures. Why is it a little smaller than fit2’s VIF of 88.2?

1 / (1 - r^2).

r <- cor(study$temp_C, study$jacket_C)
1 / (1 - r^2)

About 84. In fit2, the temperature is predicted from the jacket and the residence time and water, which explain a sliver more of it, so its R_j^2 is a little higher and its VIF a little bigger. The formula also shows how fast things get worse: a correlation of 0.9 gives a VIF of 5.3, 0.99 gives 50, and 0.999 gives 500.

Quick quiz

1. Two predictors have a correlation of 0.99. What happens when both are in the model?

The data can’t separate their effects, so the split between them is very uncertain. Their combined effect, and the fit, are well determined.

2. A predictor has a VIF of 25. What does that mean?

\text{VIF} = 1/(1 - R_j^2) inflates the variance, and its square root inflates the standard error. Here R_j^2 = 0.96.

3. The coefficient table shows NA for one predictor. What’s the likeliest reason?

That’s perfect collinearity: the same quantity in two units, a total and all its parts, or one dummy too many.

4. A quadratic model in temperature has VIFs over 1,000. What’s the simplest fix?

Uncentered x and x^2 are almost perfectly correlated over a range far from zero. Centering removes that without changing the fit.

5. Your model has severe multicollinearity, but you only need it to predict yields for conditions like the ones in the data. What should you do?

Multicollinearity hurts the interpretation of individual coefficients. Predictions within the data’s pattern are well estimated. Beware of combinations outside it, though.

6. What’s the only way to learn the separate effects of two variables that always move together in your plant?

No analysis can extract information that isn’t in the data. An experiment that sets them independently puts it there.

Wrap-up

You can now spot predictors that are stepping on each other’s toes! Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • Multicollinearity means correlated predictors. It’s normal in process data: sensors that track each other, quantities defined from each other, and operators’ habits.
  • Its effects: large standard errors, unstable and sometimes nonsensical coefficients, but a good overall fit and good predictions within the data’s pattern.
  • Perfect collinearity makes a coefficient NA: R drops a column that’s an exact combination of others.
  • VIFs, 1/(1 - R_j^2) from car::vif(), say how much each predictor’s variance is inflated. Above 5 or 10 is worth a look.
  • Condition numbers, \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}} from the standardized predictors, measure the collinearity of the whole set. Above 30 is serious.
  • Model-made collinearity from polynomials and interactions disappears with centering.
  • Remedies: do nothing (for prediction), drop or combine redundant predictors, center, collect data that break the pattern, or use ridge regression or principal components.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Multicollinearity
Strong correlation among predictors, which makes their separate effects hard to estimate.
Perfect collinearity
An exact linear relationship among predictors, which makes X^\top X singular and some coefficients impossible to estimate.
Variance inflation factor (VIF)
1/(1 - R_j^2), the factor by which a coefficient’s variance is inflated by its predictor’s correlation with the others.
Generalized VIF (GVIF)
A version of the VIF for terms with several coefficients, like factors, polynomials and interactions.
Condition number
\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}} for the eigenvalues of the standardized predictors’ correlation matrix, a measure of the collinearity of the whole set.
Extrapolation in the predictor space
Predicting for a combination of predictor values unlike any in the data, even if each value is within its own range.
