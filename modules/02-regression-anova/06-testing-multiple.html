Hypothesis tests in multiple regression

Testing one coefficient, several at once, or any linear claim about them

Read the t-tests in a multiple regression, test the whole model with the overall F-test, compare nested models with partial F-tests, test a categorical predictor and general linear hypotheses, and see why sequential sums of squares depend on the order of the predictors.

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 15 Week 36 of 160 About 5–7 hours

With one predictor, there was one question to test: is the slope zero? With several, the questions multiply. Does the model explain anything at all? Do the catalysts differ? Are catalysts B and C equally good? Does the old rule of thumb for residence time still hold, now that we account for temperature and feed water? All these questions have the same shape: a simpler model against a bigger one. And one test, the F-test, answers all of them.

One test to rule them all! Once you see that every question is “small model or big model?”, the whole zoo of regression tests turns into one friendly animal.

In this lesson you’ll:

  • read the t-tests in a multiple regression correctly
  • test the whole model with the overall F-test
  • compare nested models with the partial F-test
  • test a categorical predictor, all of its dummy columns at once
  • test general linear hypotheses, like “catalysts B and C are equally good”
  • see why sequential sums of squares depend on the order of the predictors, and partial ones don’t

One coefficient at a time

Let’s add the catalyst to last lesson’s model. It has three types, so R gives it two dummy columns, for B and C, with A as the baseline:

Each row’s t-test asks whether that coefficient is zero, given all the other predictors in the model. So the feed_water_ppm row tests whether water matters for runs with the same temperature, residence time and catalyst. That’s a different question from last lesson’s simple regression, which is why it can give a different answer.

Two catalyst rows, two separate questions: is B different from A (t = 3.87, p = 0.0002)? Is C different from A (p = 0.037)? But neither row asks the obvious one: do the catalysts differ at all? And neither compares B with C. For those, you need a test of more than one coefficient at a time.

The overall F-test

The broadest question of all: does the model explain anything? The null hypothesis is that every slope is zero:

H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_k = 0 .

The test is the ANOVA F-test from Lesson 3, with k slopes instead of one:

F = \frac{\text{SSR}/k}{\text{SSE}/(n - p)} = \frac{R^2/k}{(1 - R^2)/(n - p)},

where p = k + 1 counts the intercept too. summary() prints it on its last line: here F = 89.8 on 5 and 114 degrees of freedom. A p-value that small says that at least one predictor matters. It doesn’t say which.

In practice, the overall F-test is rarely interesting: you usually know that something matters before you fit a model. But it’s the simplest example of the idea that drives the rest of this lesson.

Your turn. Last lesson’s three-predictor model, fit3, had R^2 = 0.7336 with 120 runs. Compute its overall F statistic from R^2.

The residual degrees of freedom are n - p, and p = k + 1 includes the intercept: n - k - 1.

r2 <- summary(fit3)$r.squared
n <- 120
k <- 3
(r2 / k) / ((1 - r2) / (n - k - 1))

F = 106.5 on 3 and 116 degrees of freedom, the same as the last line of summary(fit3).

Comparing nested models

Here’s the general idea. Two models are nested if the smaller one is a special case of the bigger one, which you get by setting some of the bigger model’s coefficients to zero (or, as you’ll see, by fixing them in some other way). The smaller one is called the reduced model, and the bigger one the full model.

The full model always fits at least as well: \text{SSE}_F \le \text{SSE}_R. The question is whether it fits enough better to justify its extra coefficients. The partial F-test compares the drop in SSE with what you’d expect from luck alone:

F = \frac{(\text{SSE}_R - \text{SSE}_F)/q}{\text{SSE}_F/(n - p_F)},

where q is the number of coefficients the reduced model leaves out, and p_F counts the full model’s coefficients. If the extra coefficients are all really zero, F follows an F distribution with q and n - p_F degrees of freedom. anova(reduced, full) does the arithmetic.

The overall F-test is the special case where the reduced model has only an intercept. A t-test is the special case where q = 1: then F = t^2, as you’ll check in the challenge round.

Your turn. In Lesson 5, adding the outdoor temperature, the stirrer speed and the operator to fit3 nudged R^2 up, and adjusted R^2 down. Test all seven extra coefficients at once.

Put the reduced model first and the full model second: anova(fit3, bigger).

bigger <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm + ambient_C + stir_rpm + operator_id,
             data = study)
anova(fit3, bigger)

The seven coefficients reduced the SSE by 73 out of 2,494. F = 0.47 on 7 and 109 degrees of freedom, p = 0.85. No evidence that any of them matters.

Testing a categorical predictor

The catalyst is one predictor, but two coefficients. “Do the catalysts differ?” means “are both dummy coefficients zero?”, and that’s a partial F-test with q = 2: the model without the catalyst against the model with it.

Why not just look at the two t-tests? Because they depend on the baseline. They compare B with A and C with A, but with B as the baseline, you’d get two different t-tests. The F-test asks the question about the catalyst as a whole, and it gives the same answer whichever catalyst is the baseline.

Your turn. Compute the partial F statistic for the catalyst by hand, from the two models’ SSEs. deviance() returns a linear model’s SSE.

The full model has two more coefficients than the reduced one: the dummies for B and C.

sse_r <- deviance(fit3)
sse_f <- deviance(fit)
q <- 2
((sse_r - sse_f) / q) / (sse_f / df.residual(fit))

F = 18.0 on 2 and 114 degrees of freedom, and anova(fit3, fit) gives p = 1.6 \times 10^{-7}. The catalysts clearly differ. Adding them cut the SSE from 2,494 to 1,895.

General linear hypotheses

Setting coefficients to zero isn’t the only way to make a reduced model. Any claim of the form “some combination of the coefficients equals some number” is a general linear hypothesis, and it can be tested the same way: build the model in which the claim is true, and compare.

Are catalysts B and C equally good? That’s H_0: \beta_B = \beta_C. If it’s true, B and C are really one catalyst, so the reduced model merges them into a single category, “B or C”:

study2 <- mutate(study, catalyst2 = if_else(catalyst == "A", "A", "B or C"))
merged <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm + catalyst2, data = study2)

Does the rule of thumb hold? The full model says each minute adds 0.358 points. Is that consistent with the pilot plant’s long-standing figure of 0.35? H_0: \beta_{\text{res}} = 0.35 is a reduced model in which the residence coefficient isn’t estimated at all, but fixed. offset() adds a term with a fixed coefficient of 1:

fixed <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + offset(0.35 * residence_min) + feed_water_ppm + catalyst, data = study)

Both are nested in the full model, so anova() tests them. You’ll do that next. For hypotheses with several parts, the car package’s linearHypothesis() builds the reduced model for you from the hypothesis written as text, and gives the same F-tests:

car::linearHypothesis(fit, "catalystB = catalystC")

#> catalystB - catalystC = 0

#> 2    114 1895.3  1    582.79 35.053 3.455e-08 ***

Your turn. Test whether catalysts B and C are equally good, by comparing the merged model with the full one.

The merged model is the reduced one, so it goes first: anova(merged, fit).

study2 <- mutate(study, catalyst2 = if_else(catalyst == "A", "A", "B or C"))
merged <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm + catalyst2, data = study2)
anova(merged, fit)

F = 35.1 on 1 and 114 degrees of freedom, p = 3.5 \times 10^{-8}. B and C are clearly different: B beats A by 3.6 points, while C trails A by 1.9, so B beats C by about 5.6 points.

Your turn. Now test the rule of thumb, \beta_{\text{res}} = 0.35.

The offset fixes the residence coefficient at the rule-of-thumb value, 0.35.

fixed <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + offset(0.35 * residence_min) + feed_water_ppm + catalyst, data = study)
anova(fixed, fit)

F = 0.076, p = 0.78. The rule of thumb fits these data fine. (The simple regression in Lesson 5 said 0.21 per minute, which would have contradicted it: that was the confounded estimate.) This is the same test as the t-test t = (0.358 - 0.35)/\text{SE}, squared.

Sequential and partial sums of squares

There’s one more way to split up the variation, and it causes a lot of confusion. Give anova() a single model, and it prints a table of sequential sums of squares (also called Type I): each row is the reduction in SSE from adding that predictor after the ones above it:

anova(lm(yield_pct ~ feed_water_ppm + residence_min + temp_C + catalyst, data = study))
#> Analysis of Variance Table
#> 
#> Response: yield_pct
#>                 Df Sum Sq Mean Sq  F value    Pr(>F)    
#> feed_water_ppm   1   59.0    59.0   3.5485   0.06215 .  
#> residence_min    1 1398.0  1398.0  84.0880 2.379e-15 ***
#> temp_C           1 5410.2  5410.2 325.4114 < 2.2e-16 ***
#> catalyst         2  598.9   299.4  18.0107 1.595e-07 ***
#> Residuals      114 1895.3    16.6                       
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Put the water first, and it explains only 59 units of the SSE (p = 0.062), because on its own the water’s effect is hidden by its confounding with residence time. Now put it after residence time:

anova(lm(yield_pct ~ residence_min + feed_water_ppm + temp_C + catalyst, data = study))
#> Analysis of Variance Table
#> 
#> Response: yield_pct
#>                 Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value    Pr(>F)    
#> residence_min    1  968.9   968.9  58.277 7.577e-12 ***
#> feed_water_ppm   1  488.1   488.1  29.360 3.393e-07 ***
#> temp_C           1 5410.2  5410.2 325.411 < 2.2e-16 ***
#> catalyst         2  598.9   299.4  18.011 1.595e-07 ***
#> Residuals      114 1895.3    16.6                      
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Now the water accounts for 488 units, and residence time’s own row shrinks from 1,398 to 969. When predictors are correlated, sequential sums of squares depend on the order, because each row only gets credit for what the predictors above it didn’t already explain. The rows always add up to the same total, SSR. Only the split between them changes.

The partial sums of squares (Type II) avoid this: each predictor is added last, after all the others, so the order doesn’t matter. Each row is then a partial F-test of that predictor. drop1() computes them, as does car::Anova():

drop1(fit, test = "F")
#> Single term deletions
#> 
#> Model:
#> yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm + catalyst
#>                Df Sum of Sq    RSS    AIC F value    Pr(>F)    
#> <none>                      1895.3 343.16                      
#> temp_C          1    4899.1 6794.5 494.36 294.671 < 2.2e-16 ***
#> residence_min   1    2266.2 4161.5 435.54 136.306 < 2.2e-16 ***
#> feed_water_ppm  1     851.3 2746.7 385.68  51.205 8.614e-11 ***
#> catalyst        2     598.9 2494.2 372.11  18.011 1.595e-07 ***
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Water, added last, accounts for 851 units, with F = 51.2. Which table should you use?

  • To test each predictor’s contribution given the others, which is usually the question, use the partial sums of squares: drop1() or car::Anova().
  • Sequential sums of squares suit a model built up in a meaningful order, like a polynomial (does the squared term add to the linear one?), or “does the catalyst matter after the process conditions?”.
  • In a designed experiment where the predictors are uncorrelated, the two tables agree, and the question goes away. That’s one more reason to design experiments. Lesson 14 meets a third kind, Type III, for unbalanced experiments.

Your turn. Find the water’s sequential sum of squares when it’s entered first, and its partial sum of squares (entered last).

In drop1()’s table, the column is called "Sum of Sq".

first <- anova(lm(yield_pct ~ feed_water_ppm + residence_min + temp_C + catalyst, data = study))
last <- drop1(fit, test = "F")
c(first = first["feed_water_ppm", "Sum Sq"], last = last["feed_water_ppm", "Sum of Sq"])

59 when entered first, 851 when entered last: a factor of 14, for the same predictor in the same data. When someone reports “variable X explains Y% of the variation”, always ask: entered in which order?

Challenge round

Challenge 1 · A t-test is an F-test. For a single coefficient, the partial F statistic is the square of its t statistic. Check it for the water: square the t value from summary(fit), and compare it with the F value in drop1(fit, test = "F").

The t statistics are in the column called "t value".

t_water <- summary(fit)$coefficients["feed_water_ppm", "t value"]
c(t_squared = t_water^2, F = drop1(fit, test = "F")["feed_water_ppm", "F value"])

Both are 51.2: (-7.16)^2 = 51.2. The t-tests in summary() are partial F-tests with q = 1, which is why they’re “given all the other predictors”.

Challenge 2 · Fishing. Add 20 columns of pure random noise to the model, and count how many of them get a p-value below 0.05 in summary(). Then test all 20 together with a partial F-test.

Compare the model without the noise, fit, with the one with it, fished.

set.seed(2037)
noise <- as.data.frame(matrix(rnorm(120 * 20), nrow = 120))
fished <- lm(yield_pct ~ temp_C + residence_min + feed_water_ppm + catalyst + .,
             data = bind_cols(select(study, yield_pct, temp_C, residence_min, feed_water_ppm, catalyst), noise))
p_noise <- summary(fished)$coefficients[paste0("V", 1:20), "Pr(>|t|)"]
c(significant = sum(p_noise < 0.05), joint_p = anova(fit, fished)[2, "Pr(>F)"])

Three of the twenty noise columns look “significant”! (On average, you’d expect one in twenty, since each test has a 5% false-alarm rate. This seed was a little unlucky, which happens.) Yet the partial F-test of all twenty together gives p = 0.11: no evidence that the noise matters, which is the truth. Testing many coefficients one at a time and reporting the winners is called fishing, and it’s one of the commonest ways to fool yourself. A joint test asks the question once.

Quick quiz

1. What’s the null hypothesis of the overall F-test?

It tests whether the model explains anything at all. Rejecting it says at least one predictor matters, not which.

2. In a model with temperature, residence time, water and catalyst, what does the t-test for catalystC test?

Each dummy coefficient compares one category with the baseline, given the other predictors. To test the catalyst as a whole, use a partial F-test of both dummies.

3. Which pair of models is nested?

The smaller model is the bigger one with the catalyst coefficients set to zero. The other pairs can’t be turned into one another that way.

4. How do you test H_0: \beta_B = \beta_C with a partial F-test?

If \beta_B = \beta_C, the model can’t tell B and C apart, so the reduced model treats them as one category.

5. When do sequential (Type I) sums of squares depend on the order of the predictors?

Each row only gets credit for what the rows above it didn’t explain. With uncorrelated predictors, as in a balanced designed experiment, there’s no shared credit to argue over.

6. You test 20 predictors that have no effect at all, each at the 5% level. How many would you expect to look significant?

Each test has a 5% chance of a false alarm, and 20 \times 0.05 = 1. A single joint F-test avoids this.

Wrap-up

You can now test almost any question about a linear model! Here’s what you’ve learned:

  • t-tests in summary() test one coefficient at a time, given all the other predictors.
  • The overall F-test tests whether all the slopes are zero: F = (R^2/k)/((1-R^2)/(n-p)).
  • Nested models: a reduced model is the full one with some coefficients constrained. The partial F-test, anova(reduced, full), compares their SSEs: F = [(\text{SSE}_R - \text{SSE}_F)/q] / [\text{SSE}_F/(n-p_F)].
  • Categorical predictors are tested as a whole with a partial F-test of all their dummies.
  • General linear hypotheses like \beta_B = \beta_C or \beta_{\text{res}} = 0.35 are partial F-tests too: merge categories, use offset(), or let car::linearHypothesis() build the reduced model.
  • Sums of squares: sequential ones (anova(fit)) depend on the order when predictors are correlated. Partial ones (drop1(), car::Anova()) add each predictor last.
  • Fishing: many separate tests produce false alarms. Ask the question once, with a joint test.

NoteKey words from this lesson
Overall F-test
A test of whether all the slopes in a model are zero.
Nested models
A reduced model that’s a special case of a full model, obtained by constraining some of its coefficients.
Partial F-test
A test comparing nested models by how much the full model reduces the SSE, per extra coefficient.
General linear hypothesis
A claim that some linear combination of the coefficients equals a given value, such as \beta_B - \beta_C = 0.
Offset
A term added to a model with its coefficient fixed at 1, used to fix a coefficient at a chosen value.
Sequential (Type I) sums of squares
The reduction in SSE from adding each predictor after the ones listed before it.
Partial (Type II) sums of squares
The reduction in SSE from adding each predictor after all the others.
Fishing
Running many tests and reporting the significant ones, which produces false discoveries.
